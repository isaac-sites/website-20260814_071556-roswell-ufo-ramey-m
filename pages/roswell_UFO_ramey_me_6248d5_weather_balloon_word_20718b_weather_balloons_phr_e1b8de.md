## Introduction

“WEATHER BALLOONS” stands out in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) because it survives a test that many more dramatic proposed readings do not: people approaching the blurred photograph with different expectations have independently recognised balloon-related wording in the same part of the document. The most important evidence comes from James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 reader experiment, in which participants were deliberately given different stories about what the photographed memo concerned. Despite strong evidence that suggestion changed what readers thought they saw elsewhere, “weather balloons” still appeared across the differently primed groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_weather_balloon_word_20718b_weather_balloons_phr_e1b8de-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Balloon Phrase illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_weather_balloon_word_20718b_weather_balloons_phr_e1b8de-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_weather_balloon_word_20718b_weather_balloons_phr_e1b8de-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That makes the phrase unusually useful as an evidential anchor. It does **not** make the surrounding sentence readable, prove that the memo endorsed the weather-balloon explanation, or establish that balloons were a cover story. Rather, it shows that at least a small part of the photographed text may contain enough surviving visual information for [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) to converge on substantially the same words. That distinction — between recognising a phrase and understanding what its sentence means — is the key to using the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo responsibly.

## Where the phrase appears in competing readings

The striking feature of “WEATHER BALLOONS” is not merely that one investigator reported seeing it. Proposed [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) of the memo disagree dramatically about neighbouring material while repeatedly placing balloon wording on the same lower line.

Houran and Randle reproduced several earlier readings side by side. Neil Morris rendered the relevant area with a partially reconstructed phrase ending in “WEATHER BALLOONS”; David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}) read wording including “OF WEATHER BALLOONS”; Don Burleson proposed a line containing “BY WEATHER BALLOONS”; and Tom Carey likewise placed “WEATHER BALLOONS” there. Their reconstructions of the rest of the line differed substantially. The comparison therefore illustrates both sides of the problem at once: considerable disagreement about syntax and unusually persistent agreement about those two words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

Claims of agreement among Roswell researchers alone would be weaker evidence than they initially appear. Investigators can influence one another, consult earlier transcriptions or simply approach the photograph knowing that a weather-balloon explanation is central to the Roswell story. Kevin Randle has described “weather balloons” and “Fort Worth, Texas” as nearly universal readings and has noted that readers with opposing views of Roswell have recognised the balloon wording. That history is relevant, but it cannot by itself eliminate shared expectations as the cause of agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey MemoOctober 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2015</span></span></span>

The physical source also demands caution. The United States Air Force's 1994 Roswell investigation obtained first-generation prints and negatives of the Fort Worth photographs from the University of Texas at Arlington. It noticed the printed sheet in Ramey's hand and sent the image to a national-level organisation for digitisation and photographic interpretation. That organisation reported in July 1994 that the image remained of insufficient quality to read the document reliably. The official examination therefore supplies an important counterweight to confident full-memo transcriptions: the source photograph really is operating close to the limits of recoverable detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

That combination is precisely why “WEATHER BALLOONS” deserves separate treatment. The document as a whole has never achieved a generally accepted transcription, yet this particular area has generated a much narrower range of readings than many of the sensationally disputed passages.

## Why cross-group recognition strengthens the reading

Houran and Randle's experiment provides the strongest reason to treat “WEATHER BALLOONS” differently from a phrase that merely looks persuasive in an annotated enlargement. Their study randomly assigned 176 participants to three conditions. Fifty-nine were told that the document concerned the Roswell UFO case; 58 were told that it related to secret atomic-bomb testing; and 59 received no proposed subject for the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The design mattered because blurred text invites what psychologists would call top-down interpretation: when letter shapes are incomplete, knowledge and expectation help the brain decide what the marks probably represent. The experiment demonstrated this effect quite clearly. Roswell-primed participants produced Roswell-compatible readings, while atomic-bomb-primed participants reported words such as “atomic”, “laboratory” and “flash”. The two primed groups also generated substantially more supposed words than the blind group. Houran and Randle reported a statistically significant effect of suggestion condition on the number of words deciphered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

Against that background, the balloon result becomes more interesting. The researchers' table records “weather balloons” from 20 participants in the Roswell condition and five in the atomic-bomb condition, while two blind participants reported “balloons”. “Fort Worth TX” also crossed all three conditions, with 17 Roswell-primed, 11 atomic-bomb-primed and 24 blind participants reporting it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

Those numbers should not be exaggerated into unanimity. Most participants did **not** independently produce the complete phrase “weather balloons”. Nor did all three groups reproduce precisely the same two-word expression at identical rates. But the result is stronger than a reading found only among people explicitly told that they were examining Roswell material. Readers primed with an unrelated atomic-bomb narrative still reported “weather balloons”, while completely unprimed readers reported “balloons”. That persistence is evidence that the relevant marks contain some genuine word-level signal rather than being wholly generated by a Roswell prompt.

The authors themselves drew essentially that limited conclusion. They wrote that the unexpectedly high agreement between participants and earlier investigators on particular words in the same locations suggested that **some** of the document was legible without computer enhancement. At the same time, they stressed that the meaning and context of those words remained ambiguous because interpretation was strongly affected by suggestion and cognitive factors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This makes “WEATHER BALLOONS” valuable for an unusual reason: the experiment that supports it simultaneously undermines overconfident readings of the rest of the memo. The study did not demonstrate that people can reliably reconstruct the document. It demonstrated how readily they can misread it — and then identified a small residue of recurring words that proved harder to explain solely as suggestion.

## Why agreement here matters more than dramatic alternatives

A blurred photograph can produce a deceptive form of [consensus]({{ 'consensus/' | relative_url }}). Once a proposed transcription is printed beside an indistinct line, readers may find it difficult *not* to see the suggested letters. This is particularly important with famous proposed Ramey-memo phrases involving wreckage or victims, because those readings carry obvious implications for the Roswell controversy and therefore attract disproportionate attention.

“WEATHER BALLOONS” has a comparative advantage: it is compatible with mutually opposed interpretations of Roswell. Someone who believes the Fort Worth explanation was substantially truthful has an obvious reason for weather balloons to appear in contemporary communications. Someone who believes the explanation was a cover story can equally expect a memo concerning public handling of the incident to mention weather balloons. Randle has explicitly made this point when discussing why readers on different sides of the controversy have accepted the phrase.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey MemoOctober 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2015</span></span></span>

That does not remove contextual bias altogether — everyone familiar with Roswell knows balloons are part of the story — but it reduces the phrase's dependence on one particular theory. By contrast, a purported word whose significance exists chiefly within a crashed-spacecraft interpretation has a greater risk of being selected because it completes the reader's expected narrative.

There is also an important difference between **lexical agreement** and **sentence agreement**. On the balloon line, competing investigators can recognise “WEATHER BALLOONS” while reconstructing the words before and after it differently. Houran and Randle's comparison makes that discrepancy visible: the shared phrase sits inside mutually incompatible proposed sentences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

That pattern is exactly what one might expect when a photograph preserves some relatively strong clusters of letter shapes separated by much weaker information. A readable island does not make the surrounding sea readable. In evidential terms, agreement on two adjacent words can therefore be meaningful even when agreement on the complete line is poor.

## What “WEATHER BALLOONS” still cannot tell us

The phrase's relative strength creates a tempting but invalid next step: treating recognition of the words as proof of what Ramey was saying about them. The photograph does not support that leap.

A reference to weather balloons could have served many functions. The document might have been describing the explanation being given to reporters, repeating information received from elsewhere, discussing a press release, referring to actual balloon equipment, evaluating an earlier statement, or doing something else whose meaning depends on words that cannot presently be established. Competing reconstructions demonstrate the problem because they preserve the same balloon phrase while supplying substantially different verbs, subjects and surrounding clauses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

This is especially important when claims are made that the line explicitly describes a planned cover story. Some pro-UFO transcriptions expand the surviving fragments into language about sending out a press release concerning weather balloons and then interpret that reconstruction as evidence of deliberate deception. Such readings exist, but the disputed connecting words carry most of that interpretation; “WEATHER BALLOONS” alone does not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

The historical circumstances make the phrase unsurprising whichever broad Roswell explanation one favours. Ramey was photographed in Fort Worth during the episode in which material associated with the Roswell report was publicly identified as balloon and radar-target debris. The Air Force's later investigation likewise examined the Fort Worth photographs in reaching its Project MOGUL explanation. A balloon reference on a document present during that episode is therefore relevant, but not intrinsically anomalous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

There is an additional unresolved question about the sheet itself: exactly what kind of document Ramey was holding. Accounts attributed to photographer J. That uncertainty makes it even less safe to infer the memo's author, recipient or purpose simply from isolated readable words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo THE PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoTHE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

## The strongest phrase is strong only in a limited sense

Calling “WEATHER BALLOONS” the Ramey memo's strongest phrase should therefore be understood as a statement about **comparative readability**, not about the truth of either side of the Roswell dispute.

The case for the phrase rests on several reinforcing observations: balloon wording appears in competing historical transcriptions; readers with differing positions on Roswell have accepted it; and, most importantly, a controlled experiment found balloon-related readings even when participants were given an unrelated atomic-bomb context or no contextual prompt. At the same time, that experiment directly demonstrated how strongly suggestion can distort attempts to read the rest of the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The safest conclusion is consequently narrower than either a full “decoding” or a declaration that nothing can be recovered. The Ramey photograph appears capable of preserving **some** linguistic information, and “WEATHER BALLOONS” is among the best-supported examples of it. But the surviving signal is not sufficient to establish the grammatical relationship between those words and the surrounding text.

That distinction gives the phrase its real significance. “WEATHER BALLOONS” is evidence that the Ramey memo may not be completely unreadable. It is not, by itself, evidence that the memo says weather balloons caused the Roswell incident, nor that Ramey was describing them as a cover story. The strongest reading in the photograph therefore also illustrates its most important limitation: individual words can be considerably more secure than the story constructed around them.
