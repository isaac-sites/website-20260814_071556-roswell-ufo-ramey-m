## Introduction

Fort Worth Star-Telegram photographer J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}) is central to the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) problem because he took the photographs in which Brigadier General Roger Ramey is seen holding the disputed document on 8 July 1947. Yet Johnson's later recollections leave a fundamental question unresolved: **where did that paper come from?** At one stage he said that he may have brought an Associated Press “flash” from the newspaper office and handed it to Ramey. Later, he explicitly withdrew that idea, describing it as an erroneous early speculation. Other accounts attributed to him instead place the paper on Ramey's desk and have Johnson handing it to the general merely as a photographic prop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

That contradiction matters more than it may initially appear. If Johnson supplied the paper, the famous “[Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo” could simply be a civilian newswire report about Roswell. If it was already in Ramey's office, a military [provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}) becomes more plausible — although by no means proven. Johnson's changing memory therefore prevents the photograph itself from establishing the document's origin, and that uncertainty should constrain any attempt to use its barely legible text as decisive evidence about the Army's 8 July reversal.

## Johnson's role in the Fort Worth photographs

Johnson was not a later investigator trying to reconstruct events from documents. He was physically present in Ramey's office and created most of the surviving photographic record. Research published in *UFO History Review* identifies him as the Fort Worth Star-Telegram photographer who took six photographs showing different arrangements of the debris and military officers, including Ramey, Colonel Thomas DuBose and Major [Jesse Marcel]({{ 'marcel/' | relative_url }}). In one image, Ramey is holding the folded sheet whose faint type has subsequently attracted decades of attempts at [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

The photographic provenance itself is unusually strong. The University of Texas at Arlington's Special Collections preserves the original Star-Telegram negatives, and its catalogue material identifies Johnson as the photographer and the images as having been made on 8 July 1947. The archive also describes the writing in Ramey's hand as something researchers have scrutinised without definitively deciphering it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span> Contemporary attribution is likewise reflected in surviving newspaper material associated with Johnson's coverage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGeneral_Ramey_with_Roswell_Memo.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:General Ramey with Roswell Memo.png</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:General Ramey with Roswell Memo.png - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

That makes an important distinction possible. There is little reason to doubt **that Ramey was photographed holding a document during the Fort Worth episode**. The problem is determining **what that document was and how it reached his hand**. Those are questions for which the photograph provides only circumstantial evidence, while Johnson's recollections — potentially the most useful eyewitness evidence — changed over time.

The timing also explains why provenance is consequential. Johnson recalled being dispatched after his newsroom received a teletype alert concerning material coming from Roswell. He drove to Fort Worth Army Air Field and photographed the debris in Ramey's office during the same afternoon in which the Roswell “flying disc” announcement was being replaced by the balloon explanation. Later reconstructions consistently place him inside this rapid news cycle rather than at a formally documented meeting where the origin of every paper could be recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[ramey-memo.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">TH E PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">THE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

## The conflicting later recollections

The clearest surviving statement from Johnson about the dispute comes from a 2001 interview conducted by Roswell researcher Dennis Balthaser and subsequently reproduced by researcher Barry Greenwood. Asked directly whether he had previously said that he handed Ramey the paper, Johnson acknowledged that he had entertained that idea. He characterised it as an early speculation that he might have given Ramey the copy of the Associated Press “flash” supplied by his editor concerning the Roswell material's journey to Fort Worth. Crucially, Johnson then said that he had been mistaken in making that speculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

That wording is more nuanced than simply saying that Johnson first “remembered” one event and later “remembered” another. In his later formulation, **Johnson himself downgraded the AP-flash version from a memory to a speculation**. That distinction should be retained when assessing his testimony. It means the earlier account cannot safely be treated as a contemporaneous identification of the paper merely because Johnson was the photographer.

However, the correction does not establish a secure replacement history. Researcher Kevin Randle, who interviewed Johnson at length, reported that Johnson initially told him he had brought the flash message to Ramey's office and handed it to the general. Randle later recorded Johnson saying instead that he had taken a document from Ramey's desk and given it to Ramey so that he would have something to hold while being photographed. Randle regarded Johnson's broader recollections as repeatedly changing and argued that this made his unsupported testimony unreliable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/10/ufos-ramey-memo-j-bond-johnson-and-me.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

Johnson's uncertainty was not confined entirely to the memo. Accounts of his Fort Worth visit also varied over such details as how many photographs he had taken and aspects of his access to the base. Randle's published transcript records Johnson initially remembering only a small number of exposures, whereas later research established that he had taken six photographs. A modern sceptical review similarly notes that Johnson's recollection of taking only a couple of pictures was contradicted by the surviving photographic record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/10/ufos-ramey-memo-j-bond-johnson-and-me.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

Those discrepancies do not prove that Johnson deliberately fabricated his memo story. They demonstrate the more limited and historically important point that **his decades-later episodic memory cannot independently fix the document's provenance**. His recollections emerged roughly half a century after a brief assignment that, in 1947, gave him no reason to anticipate that the paper at the edge of one photograph would become an intensely debated artefact.

There is an additional complication. By the late 1990s Johnson was no longer merely a rediscovered witness. He became actively involved in Roswell photographic research, helping organise the Roswell Photo Interpretation Team (RPIT), which attempted to extract information from the photographs and argued against conventional interpretations of the photographed debris. A contemporary sceptical newsletter described Johnson as heading the team, while Greenwood later noted that RPIT researchers were also attempting to interpret the document itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2022/09/26195432/SUN-60-Nov-1999.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com">[Center for Inquiry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for Inquiry SUN #60</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">S KEPTICS uco NEWSLETTER By Philip J. KlJuly 27, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2013</span></span></span>

That does not invalidate Johnson's testimony, but it changes how it should be weighted. Once a witness becomes an investigator and advocate within the controversy, later recollection can become difficult to separate from subsequent discussion, interpretation and reconstruction. Greenwood also recorded that Balthaser — himself sympathetic to treating Roswell as a continuing mystery — believed Johnson had embellished aspects of his involvement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span> The criticism therefore did not arise solely from investigators committed to dismissing Roswell.

## Why provenance changes the memo's meaning

The competing Johnson accounts produce two substantially different evidential scenarios.

**In the newsroom-origin scenario**, Johnson carried the AP flash or another newswire sheet from the Star-Telegram and handed it to Ramey. The document would then be expected to discuss Roswell because Roswell was precisely the story Johnson had been sent to cover. Words resembling “disc”, “Fort Worth” or references to balloons would consequently reveal little about secret military knowledge: they could simply reproduce information already circulating through news services.

**In the Ramey-office scenario**, the document was already on the general's desk or otherwise in his possession before Johnson posed him. That would strengthen the possibility that it originated within military communications. But it still would not establish that it was classified, written by Ramey, addressed to senior headquarters or concerned with anything beyond the public handling of the Roswell story. A paper found in a commander's office during a major press story could have been military, journalistic or administrative.

This is where the physical appearance of the document becomes relevant. Greenwood compared the photographed sheet with examples of military and journalistic teletypes. He argued that its apparent formatting and punctuation were compatible with a newswire message and noted that newspaper teletypes commonly used punctuation more freely than military traffic. He also pointed to Johnson's original AP-flash account as an independently plausible route by which such a document could have reached Ramey's hand. Greenwood therefore judged a newswire origin to be at least as plausible as the claim that the sheet was a secret military message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

That argument is suggestive rather than conclusive. The photograph records only a small, angled, partly folded sheet, and its characters sit close to the resolution limit of the image. Greenwood himself stressed that most of the roughly eight visible lines are effectively unreadable, with different investigators producing substantially different transcriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span> A peer-reviewed examination in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration* likewise described substantial gaps and ambiguities in attempted readings even after researchers applied photographic enhancement and computer processing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The disagreement over provenance therefore compounds the disagreement over transcription. Suppose an enhancement appears to yield language resembling ordinary press reporting. That becomes particularly unsurprising if Johnson carried a newswire into the office. Conversely, suppose a researcher believes a fragment resembles operational military wording. Its significance rises if the paper demonstrably originated within [Eighth Air Force]({{ 'command-control/' | relative_url }}) communications. **Without knowing which kind of document is being read, interpretation of uncertain characters risks becoming circular: an assumed provenance influences the transcription, while the resulting transcription is then used to argue for that provenance.**

## Why Johnson cannot settle the memo question

Johnson remains indispensable because without his photographs there would be no Ramey memo controversy. But his status as the photographer should not be confused with reliable knowledge of everything captured incidentally by his camera. The surviving negative establishes the existence and appearance of the document far more securely than Johnson's later testimony establishes its origin.

The most defensible reconstruction is therefore deliberately narrow. Johnson went to Fort Worth to photograph the Roswell material; Ramey was photographed holding a folded, typed document; Johnson at one point associated that document with an AP flash he had brought from the newsroom; he later rejected that identification as mistaken speculation; and other reported recollections had him retrieving a paper from Ramey's desk as part of posing the photograph. No surviving contemporary record has been identified that decisively chooses between those alternatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

This leaves both strong interpretations with a provenance problem. Claims that the photograph captures a secret military Roswell message cannot simply assume that the paper belonged to Ramey. But claims that it was merely Johnson's AP copy cannot present his earliest account as settled eyewitness testimony when Johnson himself subsequently disavowed it. The responsible conclusion is not that one of his stories has been proved correct, but that his conflicting recollections **reduce the evidential weight that can safely be placed on either provenance theory**.

For understanding the Army's 8 July Roswell reversal, the memo consequently remains an intriguing contemporary object rather than a decoded documentary key. The photograph securely places a typed sheet in Ramey's hand at the centre of the Fort Worth episode. Johnson's changing story prevents researchers from securely identifying who produced it, how it reached him or whether its contents represented military communications rather than the rapidly moving news coverage surrounding Roswell. Until provenance can be established independently of Johnson's late recollections — or the text can be recovered by a method that does not depend on subjective reconstruction — the memo cannot bear the historical weight often placed upon it.
