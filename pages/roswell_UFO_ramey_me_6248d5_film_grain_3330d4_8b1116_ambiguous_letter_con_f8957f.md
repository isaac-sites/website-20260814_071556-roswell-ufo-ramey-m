## Introduction

The [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) does not contain one uniformly readable set of typewritten characters. Some marks retain enough structure to suggest a letter, while others preserve only a stem, a curve, a dark patch or a gap. In those weak positions, the easiest letters to confuse are the ones that differ mainly in the very features most likely to disappear in the photograph. **H, N and M** can collapse towards similar arrangements of vertical strokes; **C, G, O and S** can converge when their curves and openings blur; and other characters become ambiguous when adjoining letters merge into them.

That is not merely a theoretical concern. Research on human letter recognition finds recurring visual confusions such as **N versus H** and **C versus O** near the [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) of visibility. More importantly for the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo, published attempts to decipher the photograph have produced markedly different words at the same locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2935333/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Using geometric moments to explain human letter recognition near the acuity limit - PMCJanuary 21, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2009</span></span></span> The sensible question is therefore not simply “What letter does this resemble?”, but “Which competing letters could reasonably produce the same surviving photographic pattern?”

## Why H, N and M can share the same weak uprights

Capital **H, N and M** are particularly vulnerable because much of their identity depends on the relationship between strong vertical strokes and weaker connecting strokes. In a clean typewritten impression, the distinction is straightforward: H has two uprights joined by a crossbar, N has two uprights connected diagonally, and M normally adds inward diagonal structure. Once only the strongest dark portions survive, however, all three can leave conspicuous upright fragments while the distinguishing centre becomes uncertain.

There is experimental evidence that this is a genuine perceptual problem rather than an imagined one. A study of human letter recognition near the acuity limit found that letter confusions were systematic rather than random and specifically reproduced **N–H confusion**. Some visually similar pairs were confused substantially more often than others, showing that when letter information approaches the visibility threshold, observers tend to substitute structurally similar characters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2935333/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Using geometric moments to explain human letter recognition near the acuity limit - PMCJanuary 21, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2009</span></span></span>

The Ramey image presents additional reasons for caution. It is not a purpose-made document scan: the characters have been extracted from a small sheet appearing within a much larger press photograph. The US Air Force's 1994 investigation reported that a national-level organisation digitised the photographs in an attempt to recover sought details but found their quality insufficient for that purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span> In such circumstances, the absence of an apparent diagonal or crossbar cannot safely be treated as evidence that the original typewritten character lacked it.

That creates several practical traps:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* An **H** can resemble an **N** if noise supplies an apparent diagonal while its real crossbar is poorly represented.
* An **N** can move towards **H** if its diagonal becomes fragmented and a central grain cluster looks horizontal.
* An **M** can resemble a simpler two-stem letter when its inner diagonals are too weak to separate reliably.
* Conversely, grain between two strong uprights can manufacture apparent internal structure, encouraging an observer to choose the more complicated character.

</div>

This does **not** establish that any particular disputed Ramey character must be H, N or M. It establishes something more limited but important: a reading that depends on distinguishing those letters from weak central strokes deserves less [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) than one supported by several independently visible features.

## Why C, G, O and S become ambiguous when curves degrade

Rounded letters pose a different problem. **C, G, O and S** depend heavily on the continuity, opening and direction of curved strokes. When those curves are represented by only a handful of distinguishable dark patches, their defining differences can disappear.

Again, laboratory evidence supplies a useful comparison. Human recognition experiments near the acuity limit have identified **C versus O** as a prominent confusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2935333/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Using geometric moments to explain human letter recognition near the acuity limit - PMCJanuary 21, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2009</span></span></span> A C becomes an O if its opening appears closed; an O becomes a C if part of its perimeter disappears. In a degraded photographic image, either event can arise without any change to the underlying typed character.

**G** introduces an additional difficulty because much of the information distinguishing it from C or O lies in relatively small internal or terminal structure. Lose that feature and the remaining outline may support C; join an opening through blur or a dark grain cluster and it can move towards O. An **S**, meanwhile, depends on recognising the opposing curvature of its upper and lower portions. With enough blur, a few separated pieces of that curve need not uniquely specify S at all.

This is precisely the class of problem documented in degraded-document research. Schenkel and Jabri found that low resolution, noise, blur and skew substantially impair character recognition and that characters in poor-quality documents can either break into separate pieces or merge with neighbours. Their point is especially relevant to the Ramey memo because deciding where one character ends can become part of the recognition problem itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167865597001761" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low resolution, degraded document recognition using neural networks and hidden Markov models - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

NIST testing likewise demonstrated how sharply machine-print recognition can deteriorate as print and image quality decline: across deliberately varied material, reported character-recognition error rates ranged from about 1 per cent to as high as 74 per cent. That experiment is not a measurement of the Ramey photograph, but it demonstrates why apparently minor degradation cannot be assumed to have minor consequences for character identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/publications/impact-image-quality-machine-print-optical-character-recognition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">impact image quality machine print optical character recognition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Image Quality in Machine Print Optical Character Recognition &#124; NISTDecember 1, 1997...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 1997</span></span></span>

The Library of Congress makes the same point in practical archival terms. Its guidance for digitised historical newspapers warns that OCR is not completely accurate and that very small type, unusual styles and extraneous markings generate errors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/collections/chronicling-america/about-this-collection/technical-information/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on loc.gov.</span></span></span> The Ramey problem is more severe than ordinary OCR of a scanned newspaper page because the memo was incidental detail in a photograph rather than the primary object being digitised.

## The disputed readings show the ambiguity in practice

The most revealing evidence is not a theoretical list of possible letter pairs but the history of attempts to read the Ramey memo itself. Houran and Randle's 2002 paper reproduced interpretations by several investigators, including Neil Morris, David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}), Don Burleson and Tom Carey. The readings sometimes converge, but in other places they diverge radically despite referring to the same photographic marks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

One striking example comes near the bottom of the memo. Different investigators rendered the final name or word as **“Temple”** and **“RAMEY”**. Elsewhere, interpretations of another line included variants involving “land”, “Denver”, “crews”, “demo” and “surveyor”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> These disagreements cannot be reduced to a single H/N or C/O [substitution]({{ 'substitution/' | relative_url }}). They show what happens when uncertainty accumulates across several adjacent characters: different choices at the letter level produce entirely different candidate words.

There were nevertheless islands of stronger agreement. In Houran and Randle's experiment, participants independently converged to some degree on phrases including **“Fort Worth TX”**, **“story”** and **“weather balloons”** at corresponding positions. The authors therefore did not conclude that the entire memo was meaningless visual noise. Instead, they argued that some portions appeared genuinely legible while the interpretation and context remained vulnerable to suggestion and showed weak agreement among earlier analysts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction is essential. The presence of a few comparatively stable words does not automatically validate every neighbouring character. It instead suggests a spectrum of confidence: some character groups retain enough information for several readers to converge, while other groups permit multiple incompatible reconstructions.

## How neighbouring characters can falsely stabilise a guess

A weak character rarely gets judged in isolation. Once an observer thinks the letters around it form the beginning or ending of a familiar word, the uncertain middle character becomes easier to “see”. Context is normally an enormous advantage in reading. In the Ramey memo, however, it can also disguise the weakness of the underlying visual evidence.

Suppose a fragment could plausibly be either **C or O**. If neighbouring marks already seem to spell most of a familiar word requiring O, the observer has a powerful reason to close the incomplete curve mentally. Likewise, ambiguous upright fragments that could accommodate H or N may suddenly seem decisive after the surrounding characters suggest a particular word. The resulting word can feel much clearer than any individual uncertain glyph actually is.

This interaction between visual information and contextual expectation is why the Houran and Randle experiment is particularly relevant. Participants were divided into groups given different descriptions of what the document supposedly concerned: Roswell, atomic-bomb testing, or no suggested subject. The study found evidence that suggestion affected interpretation, even though participants also showed genuine convergence on certain portions of the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The problem can compound across a line. One tentative letter helps suggest a word; the candidate word then influences interpretation of the next uncertain letter; that apparent confirmation strengthens confidence in the original guess. By the end, a coherent phrase may emerge even though several of its characters were never independently secure.

This is also why disagreements among experienced Ramey-memo interpreters matter. Earlier researchers exchanged information while working on the document, and Houran and Randle explicitly noted that their published interpretations displayed weak inter-rater reliability despite the sophisticated processing applied to the photograph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Agreement reached after contextual reconstruction is therefore different evidentially from agreement produced by independent observers who identify the same character before being told the proposed word.

## Which letter identifications deserve the least confidence?

There is no defensible universal ranking in which, for example, every H in the Ramey memo is less trustworthy than every O. Confidence has to depend on what information survives at each particular position. But the evidence does support a useful hierarchy of risk.

The weakest identifications are those in which the proposed alternatives share the same surviving large-scale structure and differ only through a faint feature. **H/N/M-type ambiguities** become serious when the uprights are visible but the internal connecting strokes are not. **C/G/O/S-type ambiguities** become serious when a curved outline survives but its opening, terminal or internal stroke does not. Characters adjoining other dark marks deserve additional caution because merging can alter both their apparent width and shape. Degraded-document research specifically identifies breaking and merging as major recognition and segmentation problems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167865597001761" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low resolution, degraded document recognition using neural networks and hidden Markov models - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

The safest way to express such evidence is therefore in alternatives rather than false precision. A mark might support “H or N”, or “C/O-like rounded character”, without supporting a unique transcription. That may seem less satisfying than producing a complete sentence, but it preserves the distinction between what the photograph contains and what linguistic context supplies.

Archival practice provides a useful parallel. The US National Archives warns that OCR text is not always accurate and keeps machine-generated transcription conceptually separate from the underlying document image; its preservation guidance specifically rejects processes that substitute interpreted OCR text for the original bitmap when doing so risks loss through imprecise character interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/lcdrg/contribution/ocr-transcription" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesContribution Type: Optical Character Recognition (OCR) Transcription &#124; National ArchivesSeptember 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 2024</span></span></span> For the Ramey memo, the analogous discipline is to retain the ambiguous photographic mark as the evidence and treat the chosen letter as an interpretation of it.

## The key test is whether the distinguishing stroke survives

For the most ambiguous Ramey memo letters, the decisive question is not whether an enlarged pattern can be made to resemble a chosen character. It is whether the **feature that distinguishes that character from its nearest competitors** is genuinely present.

For an H versus N decision, that means asking whether the evidence really resolves a horizontal crossbar or diagonal. For C versus O, it means establishing whether the apparent opening is a stable feature rather than missing image information. For G versus C, the small stroke that makes the G distinctive matters more than the broad curve common to both. For M and neighbouring upright-heavy letters, the internal geometry must survive rather than be inferred from scattered dark patches.

That standard explains why some Ramey memo readings remain inherently less secure than others. The photograph can preserve enough information to indicate that a typewritten character occupied a position without preserving enough to identify that character uniquely. The published disagreement among memo interpreters, the experimental evidence for systematic human letter confusions, and the documented behaviour of degraded text all point in the same direction: **where competing letters differ mainly by weak diagonals, crossbars, openings or short internal strokes, the photograph may support a family of possible letters rather than a single one.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For that reason, H/N/M and C/G/O/S are best understood not as a definitive catalogue of “misread letters”, but as high-risk visual families. Recognising those families makes the Ramey memo easier to assess critically: a coherent-looking word is strongest when its individual distinguishing strokes survive independently, and weakest when the word itself is doing most of the work of deciding what its letters must be.
