## Introduction

A higher-resolution scan could not restore the missing detail in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) because scanning resolution and source-image resolution are different things. The memo does not survive as a readable original document waiting to be scanned. It survives as a very small, blurred part of a 1947 press photograph. By the time that photograph was made, some distinctions between individual typewritten strokes had already been weakened or lost.

Russ Estes’s examination is a particularly useful demonstration. According to James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 account, Estes worked from a 16 × 20-inch University of Texas Library print, used professional imaging equipment and software, and scanned it at 9,000 dots per inch (dpi), producing a 1.7-gigabit file. Yet he still said he could not reliably decipher the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The reason is not that 9,000 dpi was somehow too low. Once a scan samples all the meaningful information present in its photographic source, adding more samples mainly describes the same blur, grain and tonal variations more precisely. It cannot recreate letter strokes that the original camera-and-film image never resolved. That distinction is central to understanding both the value and the limit of the Estes scan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/thesignal/2013/07/you-say-you-want-a-resolution-how-many-dpippi-is-too-much/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[Blog of the Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blog of the Library of CongressYou Say You Want a Resolution: How Much DPI/PPI is Too Much? &#124; The SignalJuly 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2013</span></span></span>

## Scan resolution is not memo resolution

The apparently impressive number attached to a scan — 600, 2,400 or 9,000 dpi — describes how densely the scanner samples the physical object placed before it. It does not say that the subject originally photographed contains an equivalent amount of useful detail.

The Library of Congress makes this distinction explicitly. What is commonly called scanner “resolution” in pixels per inch is more accurately the device's sampling rate. Actual resolved detail depends both on the imaging system and on the detail available in the original being digitised. Once the [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) of the device and the original have been reached, further enlargement does not reveal additional information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/thesignal/2013/07/you-say-you-want-a-resolution-how-many-dpippi-is-too-much/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[Blog of the Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blog of the Library of CongressYou Say You Want a Resolution: How Much DPI/PPI is Too Much? &#124; The SignalJuly 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2013</span></span></span>

That matters enormously for the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo because Estes was not scanning the memo itself. Houran and Randle report that he was examining a 16 × 20-inch photographic print. The characters he wanted to identify had therefore already passed through an earlier photographic process before his scanner ever encountered them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A simple thought experiment shows the difference. Suppose one tiny feature on the print is a fuzzy grey patch that might represent the vertical stroke of an **I**, part of an **H**, or merely the merged edge of neighbouring characters. A coarse scan might represent that patch with a handful of pixels. A much finer scan could represent its shape with hundreds. That is genuinely better sampling. But if the photographic patch itself contains no preserved boundary distinguishing an I from an H, the extra pixels cannot discover that missing boundary. They merely provide a more exact digital description of the ambiguous patch.

This is why file size is a poor proxy for evidential content. Estes's 9,000-dpi image could be enlarged and manipulated extensively, but the enormous digital representation did not make the underlying photographic information equally enormous. His reported inability to read the text reliably is precisely what would be expected when scanner sampling has overtaken the useful resolving power of its source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The information had already passed through several limits

The crucial loss occurred upstream of the later scan. The Ramey photograph was an imaging system in its own right. Light reflected from the typed sheet had to travel through the camera lens and be recorded on photographic film. The memo occupied only part of the frame, was held at an angle rather than presented squarely to the camera, and its tiny characters consequently received only a limited share of the photograph's resolving capacity.

Houran and Randle describe the document as tilted relative to the camera and note that earlier investigator Richard Haines could perceive vague words but could not make out individual letters reliably. Haines thought a better-quality enlargement might reveal more, but was doubtful that it would prove very useful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Several stages can therefore constrain what eventually reaches a modern scan:

* **The original typography.** Thin typewriter strokes and the spaces separating similar characters had to be large enough in the scene to survive photography.
* **Lens and focus.** The camera could only transmit spatial detail within the resolving ability of its optics and focus at the memo's position.
* **Subject scale.** The sheet occupied a relatively small region of a photograph intended as a press picture, not a close-up document reproduction.
* **Film recording.** The photographic emulsion had finite resolving power and granularity, so increasingly small variations in the scene could not remain indefinitely distinguishable.
* **Geometry.** A tilted or curved sheet distributes detail unevenly across the image and can leave different portions at different effective scales or focus conditions.
* **Photographic reproduction.** If analysis is performed from a print rather than directly from the [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}), the print becomes another imaging generation with its own tonal and resolving characteristics.
* **Digital capture.** Only after all those stages does the scanner sample whatever information survived them.

The final scanner cannot reverse losses earlier in that chain merely by placing pixels closer together. Modern archival-imaging standards themselves distinguish sampling frequency from actual imaging performance. The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative (FADGI), for example, evaluates spatial frequency response and “sampling efficiency” as well as nominal pixels per inch; its guidelines therefore do not treat the pixel count alone as a complete measure of resolved image quality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/HIGHLIGHTED_Technical%20Guidelines%20for%20Digitizing%20Cultural%20Heritage%20Materials_3rd%20Edition_05092023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization Guidelines</span></span></span>

That distinction helps explain why a scan can contain enormous numbers of pixels while the text represented by those pixels remains uncertain.

## More pixels can capture grain and blur more faithfully

Oversampling is not useless. If an earlier scan is too coarse, increasing the sampling rate can preserve real source detail that the earlier digital version missed. It can also reduce the risk that the scanner itself becomes the limiting stage. That is why archives sometimes digitise unusually detailed originals at higher sampling frequencies. FADGI notes, for example, that very fine engravings may justify 800 ppi or more.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/HIGHLIGHTED_Technical%20Guidelines%20for%20Digitizing%20Cultural%20Heritage%20Materials_3rd%20Edition_05092023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization Guidelines</span></span></span>

But this benefit has a ceiling set by the object being scanned. The Library of Congress warns that beyond the available detail in an original photograph, higher sampling may increasingly record features such as [photographic grain]({{ 'grain-ceiling/' | relative_url }}) rather than reveal additional subject detail. It also notes that apparent sharpening produced by processing is not the same thing as obtaining new detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/thesignal/2013/07/you-say-you-want-a-resolution-how-many-dpippi-is-too-much/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[Blog of the Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blog of the Library of CongressYou Say You Want a Resolution: How Much DPI/PPI is Too Much? &#124; The SignalJuly 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2013</span></span></span>

Applied to the Ramey memo, this creates an important distinction:

**Undersampling** means useful photographic information exists but the scanner has failed to capture it adequately.

**Source limitation** means the photograph itself no longer contains sufficiently distinct information to answer the question being asked.

A high-resolution scan can solve the first problem. It cannot, by itself, solve the second.

Estes's work is significant because he pushed hard against the first explanation. His reported procedure involved a professional camera with a macro lens, specialised software, a jeweller's loupe, magnifying glass, microscope and the 9,000-dpi scan. Nevertheless, Houran and Randle report that he could not reliably decipher the text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That does not mathematically prove that every character in the photograph is unrecoverable. It does show why “scan it at still higher dpi” is not an adequate answer to the problem. Once sampling is already substantially finer than the meaningful photographic structure, further sampling produces progressively more measurements of essentially the same uncertain structure.

## Enlargement changes visibility, not necessarily evidence

Digital enlargement can make faint shapes easier for a human observer to inspect. Contrast adjustments, sharpening and other processing can likewise make boundaries more conspicuous. Those operations may be useful when a real but subtle signal is present.

They create a danger, however, when the source lies near the threshold of recognition. A smudge can be made larger and its contrast increased without becoming less ambiguous. Processing may make one possible interpretation look visually persuasive while failing to establish that the corresponding letter strokes actually existed in the original scene.

The history summarised by Houran and Randle illustrates this problem unusually well. Different investigators proposed materially different readings from the same photographic region. Neil Morris's transcription differed substantially from the earlier Johnson team's version; John Kirby reported still another reading of an important line. The authors noted that investigators often were not seeing the same things in the image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Estes provided an especially revealing example. Where competing readers proposed recognisable words, his inspection sometimes yielded only fragmentary shapes. Houran and Randle report that he tentatively perceived “Fort Work, Tex” with limited confidence; elsewhere he saw the meaningless fragment “ELA*” where other interpretations had supplied words such as “Disk” or “ELSE”. He regarded the disputed shapes as sufficiently ambiguous to compare the process to seeing familiar forms in clouds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The issue therefore was no longer simply whether pixels could be made visible. They plainly could. The harder question was whether those pixels contained enough independent structure to discriminate reliably among competing letters and words.

## The 9,000-dpi result is a test of the bottleneck

The most useful way to interpret the Estes episode is as a bottleneck problem.

Imagine the imaging chain as a series of filters. The original typed sheet contains the highest-information version. Photography reduces that information according to subject size, optics, focus, film and exposure. Making a print can impose another limit. Scanning then converts the surviving analogue variations into digital samples.

If the scanner is the narrowest bottleneck, a better scan can reveal more.

If the 1947 photograph is the narrowest bottleneck, a better scan can only reproduce that bottleneck more accurately.

The available evidence strongly suggests that the second situation became increasingly important in attempts to read the memo. An earlier US government effort had already reported in July 1994 that, after digitisation and photo interpretation, the photographs were of insufficient quality to visualise the requested details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span> Estes later approached the material with far more aggressive enlargement and sampling yet still declined to endorse a reliable transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Later work directly from the negative could avoid whatever additional degradation a photographic print introduced. Indeed, Randle reported that a team made new scans of the negative in 2015 using specialists in forensic photography, producing digital files approaching 250 MB. He also reported that the hoped-for immediate breakthrough did not occur and that the analysis had not produced universal conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey MemoOctober 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2015</span></span></span> This reinforces the central mechanism: removing a reproduction stage may preserve more of what survives, but it still cannot guarantee that the original photograph recorded enough character-level information.

## Why oversampling does not create new evidence

A useful high-resolution scan may contain vastly more pixels than a lower-resolution version without containing proportionately more independent information. Neighbouring samples increasingly describe the same underlying photographic transitions. In imaging science, this is why sampling frequency and effective spatial resolution are treated separately rather than assumed to be interchangeable. FADGI's current technical guidelines explicitly label ppi as “Resolution (Sampling Frequency)” while separately measuring spatial frequency response and sampling efficiency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/HIGHLIGHTED_Technical%20Guidelines%20for%20Digitizing%20Cultural%20Heritage%20Materials_3rd%20Edition_05092023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization Guidelines</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, oversampling can therefore do three useful things: preserve the surviving photographic information with less digital loss, permit closer inspection without immediately exposing a coarse pixel grid, and provide better material for careful processing or quantitative comparison.

What it cannot do is supply measurements of the 1947 sheet that were never encoded in the photograph.

If two possible typewriter characters produced effectively the same blurred photographic pattern at the scale recorded on the negative, no amount of later dpi can tell the scanner which character generated it. A reconstruction algorithm might eventually *infer* a likely letter from surrounding language or learned patterns, but that would introduce assumptions beyond the direct photographic signal. For historical evidence, such inference must not be confused with recovering an objectively recorded stroke.

That is the central lesson of the Estes scan. Its failure to yield a secure transcription was not paradoxical despite its extraordinary nominal resolution. In one sense, the 9,000-dpi scan did exactly what a finer scan should do: it sampled the available source extremely densely. What it exposed was the deeper limitation — that the decisive missing information was not necessarily hiding between the scanner's pixels. Much of it had already been blurred, merged or never distinctly recorded when the press photograph was exposed.

## The practical limit of the Ramey memo image

The right question is therefore not “How many dpi are needed to read the Ramey memo?” There is no universal dpi threshold at which an indistinct photographed document suddenly becomes legible. The meaningful question is whether the surviving negative or print contains spatial and tonal differences that correspond reliably to particular character strokes.

Some portions may preserve enough information for cautious identification. Houran and Randle themselves found that a small number of perceived words occurred in consistent locations across experimental groups, even while their broader experiment showed that suggestion could influence attempted readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That makes the problem more nuanced than simply declaring the whole sheet unreadable.

But the same evidence argues against treating extreme scan resolution as a way around [ambiguity]({{ 'ambiguity/' | relative_url }}). Estes's 9,000-dpi examination produced a much finer digital representation, not a new photograph of the memo. The Library of Congress's general guidance captures the underlying principle: once the useful resolution of the imaging device and the detail in the original have been reached, additional enlargement does not reveal additional detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/thesignal/2013/07/you-say-you-want-a-resolution-how-many-dpippi-is-too-much/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[Blog of the Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blog of the Library of CongressYou Say You Want a Resolution: How Much DPI/PPI is Too Much? &#124; The SignalJuly 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2013</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, that is the fundamental resolution limit. More pixels can preserve surviving evidence better. They can expose grain, blur and faint tonal structure with greater precision. They can make existing strokes easier to study. But they cannot travel backwards through the 1947 photographic process and restore distinctions between letters that the original camera-and-film image failed to preserve.
