## Introduction

Yes. Sharpening can make an uncertain mark in the Ramey memo look more like a deliberate letter stroke without establishing that such a stroke was actually resolved by the 1947 photograph. The reason is mechanical rather than mysterious: sharpening emphasises rapid changes in brightness — the same high-frequency image content produced not only by genuine typewritten edges, but also by [film grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}), tiny specks, scanning noise and other irregularities. If a line appears, disappears, joins another mark or changes direction as sharpening parameters change, its crisp appearance is evidence about the enhancement, not necessarily about the original memo.

## How sharpening strengthens weak boundaries

Sharpening does not work like bringing a camera closer to the document. It does not acquire another measurement of Ramey's paper. Instead, it operates on brightness values already recorded in the image. SWGDE defines sharpening as emphasising edge detail by enhancing high-frequency components, while federal digitisation guidance describes sharpening as a form of image-edge enhancement, commonly implemented with techniques such as unsharp masking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/05-f-001-swgde-digital-multimedia-evidence-glossary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE - SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SWGDE Digital &amp; Multimedia Evidence Glossary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDESWGDE Digital &amp; Multimedia Evidence GlossaryJune 23, 2016 — Sharpening. (i,v) A process used to emphasize edge detail in an...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 23, 2016</span></span></span>

An edge is simply a region where brightness changes rapidly. Imagine a faint, blurred typewritten vertical stroke. Rather than a neat transition from white paper to black ink, the photograph may record several gradually darkening pixels followed by several gradually lightening ones. A sharpening operation can increase those local differences. The darker side becomes more conspicuous against the lighter side, making the transition appear steeper and the putative stroke better defined.

That is useful when the underlying edge genuinely exists. The problem is that the algorithm has no semantic knowledge that one edge belongs to an "R" while another comes from grain in the photographic emulsion. Digital images contain noise unrelated to the original subject, just as photographic film grain does not correspond directly to objects in the photographed scene; US Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative guidance explicitly distinguishes such noise from original image content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/digitization/fadgi-2016-rev-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesTechnical Guidelines for Digitizing Cultural Heritage...Sharpening is usually implemented through image edge enhancemen...</span></span></span>

This produces an important asymmetry in a document such as the Ramey memo. A large, well-resolved character contains enough spatial structure that modest sharpening mainly improves its presentation. A barely resolved character may consist of only weak, neighbouring tonal variations. There, deciding which variations belong to the character is precisely the unresolved question. Sharpening strengthens those variations before the viewer has established what caused them.

Older forensic-imaging guidance makes the resulting limitation explicit. Linear filters including sharpening, edge enhancement, deblurring and deconvolution increase the contrast of small detail, but at high enhancement levels the result may cease to represent the overall scene accurately. Restoration is also limited by noise and by a basic constraint that matters greatly here: information that has been completely lost cannot simply be replaced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Section_5_Guidelines_for_Image_Processing.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE - SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDEDisclaimer:Linear filtering techniques (see Figure 1) include sharpening, deblurring, edge... Limitations are imposed on th...</span></span></span>

So when an enhanced Ramey image suddenly reveals a beautifully straight segment where the unprocessed scan showed only an uncertain grey variation, the appropriate question is not "What letter has appeared?" It is "What recorded pixels caused the filter to construct such a strong-looking boundary?"

## When noise begins to resemble letter strokes

The most troublesome false-looking strokes are not necessarily invented from nothing. More often, sharpening reorganises the visual importance of genuine recorded variations whose causes are uncertain.

Suppose, for example, that a faint vertical remnant of ink lies near a dark grain cluster. In a minimally processed image the two may appear as disconnected grey marks. Edge enhancement can strengthen both. Once enlarged for inspection, the eye may perceive the resulting dark components as one continuous vertical stem. If another speck lies nearby, it can become an apparent crossbar. Three individually ambiguous features have now acquired the familiar geometry of a letter.

The opposite can happen too. Sharpening can strengthen the light-dark boundaries *within* a broad blurred feature, visually splitting what was one uncertain patch into apparently separate strokes. Strong processing can therefore change not merely how visible a supposed character is but its apparent topology: whether components touch, whether a loop is open or closed, whether a vertical stem continues through a noisy region, or whether a short patch looks like a crossbar.

These are disproportionately important changes when reading letters. The difference between several possible characters may depend on exactly such tiny structures — a short horizontal arm, the closure of a loop, a diagonal connection or a gap between adjacent strokes. At the resolution limit, a processing artefact only a few pixels across can therefore change the perceived character rather than merely improve its appearance.

Sharpening can also produce conspicuous light or dark structures adjacent to strong transitions. More aggressive restoration techniques have related problems. For example, documentation for Richardson-Lucy deconvolution warns that restored images can exhibit ringing, while research on the method notes that fitting noise can generate high-frequency artefacts whose structure depends on processing choices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mathworks.com/help/images/ref/deconvlucy.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mathworks.com">[MathWorks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mathworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Math Worksdeconvlucy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">deconvlucy - Deblur image using Lucy-Richardson method - MATLAB...</span></span></span> Such methods are not equivalent to ordinary sharpening, but they illustrate the broader problem: attempts to recover high-frequency detail from degraded imagery can produce visually persuasive fine structure that is partly algorithm-dependent.

The Ramey memo is unusually vulnerable because this ambiguity occurs at the scale of the information investigators most want to recover. The UTA material derives from J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})'s photograph rather than from the memo itself; the paper occupies only part of the original frame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span> No amount of enlargement changes that historical sampling geometry. More pixels in a displayed enlargement can make the boundaries smoother to look at, but they do not automatically create additional independently recorded letter detail.

This distinction between **display size** and **resolved information** is central. A character can be enlarged until it fills a computer screen and still contain insufficient source information to decide whether a tiny projection is ink, grain or a processing effect.

## Crisp letters can still be unstable letters

The Ramey memo provides an unusually useful warning against equating visual confidence with evidential confidence because people have repeatedly extracted different readings from the same underlying material.

In a 2002 study, James Houran and Kevin Randle divided 176 participants into three groups. Fifty-nine were told that the document concerned Roswell, 58 that it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing, and 59 received no suggested context. Participants reported numerous apparent words, but their readings were affected by the contextual suggestion they had received. The researchers nevertheless found a smaller number of features that were perceived consistently across conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That experiment concerned interpretation rather than sharpening specifically, but it exposes why sharpening-dependent strokes are hazardous. Once processing converts a vague tonal pattern into something resembling a familiar letter component, human expectation can complete the remaining structure. A short dark segment becomes easier to interpret as the crossbar one expects; disconnected patches are more readily grouped into the anticipated word.

The study also reviewed earlier attempts to decipher the memo and found weak agreement between investigators. It recommended standardised computer enhancement of the best available raw data and analysis by independent, blind laboratories specialising in archival-document transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> A later review of the Roswell evidence similarly concluded that attempts to decipher the memo had not produced definitive results.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationA Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; Journal of Scientific ExplorationAugust 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 20, 2022</span></span></span>

This does not mean that every apparent letter in an enhanced Ramey memo is false. Houran and Randle's experiment actually points towards the more useful distinction: some features appear sufficiently stable to be recognised by different observers, while other interpretations depend heavily on context. The same principle can be applied to processing. A feature that survives different reasonable enhancement methods deserves more weight than one that becomes letter-like only under one particularly aggressive setting.

## Tests that expose sharpening-created strokes

For the Ramey memo, the most informative test is therefore not a contest to produce the clearest-looking enhancement. It is a **stability test**: does the proposed stroke remain supported when the processing conditions are deliberately varied?

A strong examination would preserve the original image and generate controlled working versions rather than repeatedly processing an already enhanced derivative. That matches SWGDE guidance: the original should be preserved, processing should occur on working copies, and the steps should be documented sufficiently for another trained examiner to understand and reproduce the procedure. SWGDE explicitly cautions against introducing artefacts that add misleading information or losing detail in ways that could produce erroneous interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/15-m-002-swgde-image-processing-guidelines/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE - SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SWGDE Image Processing Guidelines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDE Image Processing Guidelines</span></span></span>

Several checks are especially revealing:

* **Return to the minimally processed source.** Identify exactly which tonal variations support each proposed stem, crossbar, curve or gap. A feature that cannot be related back to recorded source structure should not acquire evidential status merely because it looks persuasive after enhancement.
* **Sweep the sharpening strength.** Compare no sharpening, weak sharpening and progressively stronger settings. Genuine boundaries should have some continuity across sensible settings. A supposed stroke that suddenly materialises at one threshold is much less secure.
* **Change the method.** Compare, for example, conservative unsharp masking with another documented edge-enhancement approach. If a claimed letter component changes substantially with the algorithm, its shape is partly method-dependent.
* **Inspect neighbouring blank or non-text regions.** If the same processing routinely turns grain and specks around the memo into short bars, hooks and junctions, similar structures inside a supposed word require caution.
* **Test connections rather than merely darkness.** Ask whether two components are genuinely connected in the source data. Sharpening can make nearby marks appear to form a continuous stem even when the underlying connection is uncertain.
* **Keep competing readings visible.** Where the pixels permit several plausible character shapes, the enhancement should not be allowed to hide that ambiguity behind one clean transcription.
* **Use blind comparison where possible.** Analysts who do not know the proposed word are less likely to search the enhanced grain for precisely the stroke needed to complete it. The suggestion effects demonstrated in the Ramey-memo experiment make this particularly relevant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The decisive concept is **persistence**. A genuine recorded feature need not look identical under every transformation, but its location and basic geometry should remain reasonably stable. A vertical component that persists through minimally processed, lightly sharpened and independently processed versions is much stronger evidence than a vertical component that exists only after aggressive edge enhancement.

The same applies at word level. If one processing recipe produces a distinctive letter while another equally defensible recipe turns its crucial stroke into two specks, the source has not securely resolved that letter. Choosing whichever version produces the most meaningful English word would reverse the proper direction of inference: the desired transcription would be selecting the image-processing parameters.

## What a convincing Ramey reading would require

The central risk is therefore not simply that software can "fake letters". Conventional sharpening usually acts on something already present in the pixel values. The deeper problem is that it can give **equal visual authority to unequal kinds of information**. A faint trace produced by typewritten ink, an emulsion-grain boundary and a tiny digitisation irregularity can all become crisp-looking high-contrast structures.

For well-resolved material, that distinction may matter little. For the Ramey memo, it can determine whether the viewer sees one letter or another.

The strongest forensic principle is consequently conservative. SWGDE's image-processing guidance recognises that enhancement can reveal information not readily apparent in an original, but it simultaneously requires caution because processing can introduce misleading artefacts. Its guidance also stresses controlled, predictable and repeatable processing rather than treating the most visually impressive result as intrinsically superior.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/15-m-002-swgde-image-processing-guidelines/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE - SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SWGDE Image Processing Guidelines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDE Image Processing Guidelines</span></span></span>

A persuasive claimed Ramey-memo stroke should therefore be traceable to the least-processed source available, remain geometrically stable across a reasonable range of sharpening settings, survive comparison with alternative enhancement methods and be independently recognised without prompting the reader with the desired word. Those tests do not guarantee that a character has been identified correctly, but failure of them is highly informative.

That is the key distinction sharpening can obscure. **A letter can become clearer-looking without becoming better established.** In the Ramey memo, where individual character features sit close to the [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) imposed by the original photograph, the most impressive-looking enhancement may therefore be less informative than a duller image that honestly preserves the uncertainty of the recorded strokes.

      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {

      } catch (err) {}

    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
