## Introduction

In April 2015, a small group of Ramey memo researchers returned to the University of Texas at Arlington, where the original Fort Worth Star-Telegram negatives from 8 July 1947 are preserved, to try something that earlier enlargements and scans had failed to achieve: capture enough additional information from the archival film to make the memo reliably readable. The effort used direct inspection, microscopy, digital re-photography, bracketed exposures and high-dynamic-range processing. It produced substantially better working material than many researchers had previously possessed, but not the hoped-for breakthrough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The reason matters. The 2015 project did not fail because the archive held a badly ruined [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}). Investigators reported that the relevant film was reasonably well preserved, adequately exposed and in focus. The harder limit was scale: the writing on the sheet occupied such a tiny part of the original photograph that individual characters were recorded at or near the granularity of the photographic emulsion itself. The new scans could reveal that grain more clearly; they could not reliably turn ambiguous grain patterns into agreed letters and words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

## Returning to the archival source

By 2015 the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo had already been subjected to repeated enlargement and digital [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) for well over a decade. Earlier investigators had used large photographic prints, professional video equipment, microscopy and high-resolution scans. A 2002 study described four then state-of-the-art source images assembled by Stanton Friedman, while another investigator had scanned an enlargement at 9,000 dpi without obtaining a reliable transcription. The recurring problem was that different readers could extract different words from the same blurred character patterns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

The 2015 initiative therefore went back to the archival photographic source rather than simply applying another software filter to an existing web image. According to the later published account incorporating Simon Schollum’s 2015 technical report, work at UTA began on 21 April. The stated goals included inspecting the physical condition of the negative, assessing definition and resolution, distinguishing genuine image density from film-base fog, identifying the silver particles forming letter shapes and separating those from interfering grain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The researchers credited with the scanning work were Kevin Randle, David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}), Simon Schollum and Jerry Morelock. Contemporary accounts say forensic-photography expertise was involved and that several days were spent preparing and recording the material. UTA later described itself as having partnered with the researchers to document and disseminate the Roswell photographs and their effort to determine the memo’s contents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-ramey-memo-scans.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Scans</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo ScansNovember 30, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 30, 2015</span></span></span>

This distinction is important. The 2015 work was not merely a new transcription attempt. It was an attempt to improve the evidential substrate on which all later transcription arguments rested.

## What the 2015 imaging actually improved

The archival examination gave researchers some useful answers about the negative itself. Schollum’s report described the emulsion as sufficiently well fixed, with no significant degradation specifically destroying the memo area. There were minor stains, dust and scratches consistent with age and normal newspaper use, but the exposure was judged adequate, there was no evident camera or subject shake, and the memo lay within the photographer’s focus zone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That finding ruled out one tempting explanation for the poor readability: the decisive information had not simply vanished because the negative had deteriorated in storage. Instead, the report identified scale as the central constraint. In one digital representation of the full 4-by-5-inch negative, the frame measured about 3,663 by 2,743 pixels, while the message itself spanned only about 148.5 pixels in width. In other words, a high-quality photograph contained a very small photographed document inside it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The team then attacked that limitation from several directions. The memo was examined through a Nikon SMZ1500 biological microscope; UTA’s digital microfiche equipment was used to record bracketed exposures; the negatives were photographed with Canon digital equipment using macro and micro lenses; and additional recordings were made on ultra-high-resolution Kodak film for processing in New Zealand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

High Dynamic Range, or HDR, processing was also used. Multiple image states could be stacked and manipulated in an attempt to suppress [film grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}), separate the paper tone from the darker type and “lift” character tones away from the background. The later published description says this process reached a sampling resolution high enough to show individual silver-halide grains in the negative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That sounds dramatic, but it exposes the central paradox of the 2015 project. Once the recording system is resolving the grains that constitute the photographic image, further enlargement increasingly describes the grain structure rather than recovering finer detail from the original sheet of paper. The investigators had become much better at seeing what survived on the film. What survived was still insufficiently explicit to force one transcription.

## Why the critical lines remained unresolved

The hoped-for outcome was not simply a sharper-looking memo. It was independent agreement on the controversial wording. On that test, the project fell short.

Randle reported in October 2015 that some individual files approached 250 MB and that the new imaging had not produced the anticipated immediate result. By the end of November, he wrote that the complete collection of scans, using different techniques, lighting and filters, amounted to roughly 27 GB. He explicitly described the new scans as an improvement over previous material while conceding that they had not yielded the desired clarity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

The researchers then broadened access. Copies were given to people outside the core group, including sceptical investigator Lance Moody, and the image set was placed online with help from Isaac Koi and others. This mattered because a genuine breakthrough should survive independent inspection: researchers approaching the files with different expectations ought to converge on the same letters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

They did not. A contemporaneous sceptical review by Tim Printy reported that members of the Roswell Slides Research Group tried different software and methods on the new scans but could not reach agreement about much of the text. Printy’s own conclusion was that the scans had not materially resolved the letter shapes and that subjective interpretation remained unavoidable. His analysis was openly sceptical of pro-Roswell readings, but on the narrow technical point — no consensual transcription emerged from the 2015 data — it matches Randle’s own published assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/SUNlite8_1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOShedding some light on UFOlogy and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 4, 2025</span></span></span>

That outcome also echoed an older experimental warning. In 2002, James Houran and Kevin Randle had shown different groups the ambiguous memo while giving them different contextual suggestions. Participants often reported seeing words consistent with the story they had been told — Roswell, atomic-bomb testing or no supplied context — although a smaller number of features appeared consistently across groups. The study did not prove that every proposed word was imaginary, but it demonstrated that [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) could influence readings when the visual signal was weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The 2015 scans improved the signal without eliminating that weakness. Consequently, disputed phrases such as readings involving “victims”, “wreck”, “disc” or alternative mundane wording could still be argued over rather than demonstrated character by character to the satisfaction of opposing readers.

## The real limit was in the 1947 exposure

The failed breakthrough illustrates a basic distinction in image analysis: better digitisation is not the same thing as recovering lost information.

A modern scanner can sample an old negative at extremely fine intervals. HDR processing can make tonal differences easier to see. Grain-suppression techniques can reduce distracting visual noise. Geometric correction can enlarge and straighten a tilted document. None of these methods can guarantee recovery of letter strokes that were never independently resolved by the camera and film in 1947.

Schollum’s observation that individual film grains were about 3–10 microns across captures the problem particularly well. Once a photographed typewriter character is represented by only a limited and irregular cluster of such grains, there may be several plausible ways to connect those grains into letter forms. Sharpening can make one interpretation look more convincing, but it cannot establish that interpretation if the underlying exposure does not contain enough separable spatial information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

This is why file size is a poor proxy for evidential improvement. A 250 MB or multi-gigabyte image can preserve every measurable feature of a small section of film and still leave the original subject ambiguous. The data set may be enormous because it samples the negative very densely, not because the 1947 camera recorded equally dense information about the type on Ramey’s paper.

The 2015 work therefore changed the nature of the debate more than it changed its answer. It made it harder to argue that a simple rescan with competent equipment would obviously settle the memo. Competent equipment had been brought directly to the archival negative, and the result remained interpretively unstable.

## What the failed breakthrough established

The most defensible legacy of the 2015 rescanning effort is methodological rather than sensational.

First, it confirmed that the surviving negative was worth examining directly. The relevant area had not been obliterated by catastrophic deterioration, and the original photographic material retained more tonal and structural information than ordinary reproductions conveyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

Second, it produced a richer set of research images and made them available beyond the original investigators. Randle’s November 2015 release explicitly invited others to examine the material and disclose both their results and their methods. UTA subsequently continued to present the Ramey image as an archival object whose text had been intensively studied but not definitively deciphered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-ramey-memo-scans.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Scans</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo ScansNovember 30, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 30, 2015</span></span></span>

Third, it supplied unusually concrete evidence for why the memo remains unresolved. The obstacle is not simply “blur” in the everyday sense. It is the relationship between the minute photographed type, film grain, optical scale, document angle, tonal overlap and the human tendency to impose familiar character shapes on incomplete patterns.

Most importantly, 2015 did not validate a single full transcription. Advocates continued to defend readings they believed were strengthened by the new images, while sceptics continued to see different words or no dependable words at all. Later published summaries still characterised attempts to decipher the memo as non-definitive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/12/jan-aldrich-barry-greenwood-and-ramey.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Jan Aldrich, Barry Greenwood and the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Jan Aldrich, Barry Greenwood and the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

## Why 2015 remains a useful dividing line

Before the archival rescanning, it was reasonable to hope that the Ramey memo controversy was mainly waiting for better access to the original negative and more modern imaging. After 2015, that explanation became much harder to sustain.

The archival source was inspected. Multiple recording methods were applied. Tonal range was exploited. Microscopic structure was examined. Large digital files were produced and distributed to researchers with sharply different views of Roswell. Yet the core test — independent convergence on the disputed wording — was not met.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

That does not prove that no additional word can ever be recovered. Nor does it mean every proposed reading is equally poor. Some character groups may be more stable than others, and future methods can still be tested against the archived data or the physical negatives.

But the 2015 episode sharply reduced the plausibility of the simplest promised breakthrough: scan the negative at higher quality and the memo will become plainly readable. The project did scan the archival material far more intensively, and what emerged was a somewhat better image of an intrinsically under-resolved text — not a document that opposing readers could finally transcribe in the same way.
