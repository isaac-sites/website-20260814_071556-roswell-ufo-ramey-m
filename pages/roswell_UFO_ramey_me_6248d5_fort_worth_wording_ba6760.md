## The recurring location fragment

Attempts to read the memo have produced striking disagreements. Researchers have variously proposed “victims”, “remains”, “disc”, “crash”, “weather balloons” and much longer reconstructed sentences. Against that unstable background, “Fort Worth” stands out because several early interpretations retained essentially the same location while disagreeing over surrounding text.

The history reviewed by James Houran and Kevin Randle in their 2002 study illustrates this clearly. A team assembled by photographer J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}) reported a passage containing “AT FORT WORTH, TEX”. Neil Morris later produced a substantially different transcription, yet “Fort Worth, Texas” remained. Computer specialist John Kirby said he could decipher little of the memo but nevertheless agreed that the third line read “At Fort Worth, Tex.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That sort of convergence is more informative than agreement reached after researchers have repeatedly compared notes. It suggests that the underlying letter shapes in this particular part of the photograph contain a relatively distinctive signal.

The strongest quantitative evidence came from Houran and Randle’s experiment. They divided 176 participants into three conditions. One group was primed with a Roswell-UFO explanation, another was told that the document related to secret atomic-bomb testing, while a third received no proposed subject. The researchers were explicitly testing whether suggestion could make people perceive expected words in an ambiguous image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Suggestion did have a substantial effect. Participants often produced readings compatible with whatever story they had been given. Yet several fragments appeared across the conditions in matching locations. “Fort Worth TX” was identified by 24 participants in the UFO group, 17 in the atomic-bomb group and 11 in the blind group: 52 readers altogether. “Story” and “weather balloons” also showed cross-group agreement, although at different frequencies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The finding is important precisely because it came from a study designed to expose false confidence. Houran and Randle did not conclude that the full memo had been deciphered. They argued instead that the combination of strong suggestion effects and localised agreement meant that isolated portions could be genuinely legible while the document’s broader meaning remained uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why Fort Worth fits the photographed setting

The location reading has an unusual advantage over phrases such as “victims of the wreck”: it can be checked against independently established circumstances without first accepting any theory about what crashed near Roswell.

The photograph was taken in Ramey’s office at Fort Worth Army Air Field on 8 July 1947. A recent historical account from the US Army Intelligence Center of Excellence likewise describes Ramey, then commander of the Eighth Air Force, holding his press conference there after the Roswell material reached Fort Worth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dvidshub.net/news/475677/intelligence-agents-investigate-ufos-roswell-7-jul-1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dvidshub.net">[DVIDS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dvidshub.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jesse Marcel with debris at Fo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DVIDSDVIDS - News - Intelligence Agents Investigate UFOs in Roswell (7 JUL 1947)July 8, 2024 — DVIDS - News - Intelligence Agents Investi...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 2024</span></span></span> Contemporary press coverage was also centred on the base. The surviving photograph belongs to the Fort Worth Star-Telegram collection preserved by the University of Texas at Arlington, a provenance noted in the academic analysis of the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Fort Worth therefore belongs naturally in several plausible types of document that Ramey might have been holding. It could have appeared in military traffic identifying the headquarters, in a message describing where material had been sent, or in a civilian wire report describing developments at Fort Worth. That last possibility is significant because the precise origin of the sheet itself has never been settled beyond doubt. Houran and Randle noted differing accounts about whether photographer J. Bond Johnson might have carried an Associated Press wire story into the office or whether the paper was already there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That ambiguity [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) what the place name can prove. Reading “Fort Worth” does not establish that the document was a classified message from Ramey to Washington, nor does it tell us who wrote it. A newspaper wire story produced during the same unfolding event could also have mentioned Fort Worth.

What the fragment can establish, more modestly, is consistency between the photographed marks and a place unquestionably central to the scene. This makes it a useful calibration point rather than a solution to the memo’s authorship.

## How a known anchor can test broader transcriptions

A reliable place name can function as an anchor when evaluating disputed readings. The principle is straightforward: start with a fragment for which both visual agreement and external context are relatively strong, then ask whether a proposed transcription of neighbouring text fits the same physical evidence without requiring increasingly speculative substitutions.

“Fort Worth” is useful for this purpose because its approximate character sequence, spacing and position can be compared across scans and [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) methods. Researchers can ask whether different treatments of the photograph preserve the same apparent word boundaries and letter structures around that location. If an image-processing technique makes an accepted anchor disappear while revealing dramatic new phrases elsewhere, that should reduce confidence in the technique rather than increase it.

The 2002 experiment supports this cautious approach. Its participants agreed on “Fort Worth TX” in a common location even though their larger interpretations diverged sharply according to suggestion. The authors therefore distinguished between local recognition and narrative reconstruction: some characters may carry enough information to support repeated identification, while connecting those characters into complete sentences invites much greater cognitive influence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction is especially important in the Ramey memo debate. Once a reader accepts a proposed sentence such as “victims of the wreck”, nearby smudges can become easier to interpret in ways that complete the expected story. Houran and Randle demonstrated experimentally that contextual suggestion affects what readers think they can see. The safer practice is consequently to score short fragments independently before attempting to infer sentences or document purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The historical record also provides a useful warning against claims that digital enlargement automatically resolves the problem. During the Air Force’s 1994 Roswell investigation, first-generation Fort Worth Star-Telegram prints and negatives were obtained from the University of Texas at Arlington and sent to what the report called a “national-level” organisation for digitisation and photo interpretation. That organisation reported on 20 July 1994 that the photographs remained of insufficient quality to recover the sought details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span>

Later civilian researchers have claimed greater success, but even analyses sympathetic to continued study acknowledge substantial disagreement. A 2022 review in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration* stated that attempts to decipher the memo had not produced definitive results capable of ruling out competing Roswell explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

The presence of a relatively stable anchor such as “Fort Worth” therefore demonstrates recoverability, not complete recoverability.

## What the clue can safely tell us

The strongest interpretation of the Fort Worth fragment is deliberately limited. Three conclusions are reasonably supported.

First, the Ramey memo is not simply featureless photographic noise. Independent readers have identified at least a few recurring words at matching positions, with “Fort Worth TX” producing particularly notable agreement. Houran and Randle themselves regarded this cross-reader consistency as evidence that portions of the document were genuinely legible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Second, the location is contextually appropriate. Ramey commanded the Eighth Air Force at Fort Worth Army Air Field, and the photographed Roswell debris and press activity were physically present there on 8 July 1947.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dvidshub.net/news/475677/intelligence-agents-investigate-ufos-roswell-7-jul-1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dvidshub.net">[DVIDS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dvidshub.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jesse Marcel with debris at Fo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DVIDSDVIDS - News - Intelligence Agents Investigate UFOs in Roswell (7 JUL 1947)July 8, 2024 — DVIDS - News - Intelligence Agents Investi...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 2024</span></span></span> A recovered “Fort Worth” reading therefore aligns with independently documented circumstances rather than introducing an unexplained place.

Third, the fragment provides no automatic support for the more consequential proposed readings beside it. “Fort Worth” can be correct even if “victims”, “disc”, “wreck” or proposed transportation instructions are wrong. Indeed, the experimental evidence points towards exactly that mixed situation: some words recur reliably, while larger interpretations change according to the expectations supplied to the reader.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For that reason, “Fort Worth” is best treated as a calibration clue. It shows that the photographed document contains at least some context that can survive enlargement and independent inspection. Its evidential value lies less in proving a particular Roswell theory than in marking the boundary between comparatively reproducible reading and speculative reconstruction.

## Why “Fort Worth” is one of the safer readings

Among proposed Ramey-memo fragments, “Fort Worth” earns a comparatively high confidence not because the letters are perfectly sharp, but because several independent tests point in the same direction. Researchers with otherwise incompatible [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) retained the phrase; readers exposed to different explanatory stories identified it in the same part of the document; and the place itself corresponds exactly to Ramey’s headquarters and the location of the photographed press event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The qualification “comparatively” remains essential. The source photograph does not offer the clarity of an original typed document, and official photo analysis in 1994 judged the available imagery inadequate for a definitive reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span> The later experimental work likewise found that suggestion could strongly reshape interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That combination makes the Fort Worth wording valuable in a very specific way. It is evidence that recognisable linguistic information survives in parts of the Ramey memo, and it gives researchers a known contextual anchor against which enhancement methods and wider transcriptions can be tested. It is not, by itself, evidence for bodies, extraterrestrial technology or a deliberate cover story. The distinction is what makes “Fort Worth” one of the safer—and more useful—readings in an otherwise highly contested document.
