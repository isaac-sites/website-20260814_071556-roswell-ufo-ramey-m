## Introduction

The debris displayed in Brigadier General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }})’s Fort Worth office on 8 July 1947 resembled a radar target for a simple mechanical reason: the visible materials were the same basic materials from which contemporary balloon-borne radar reflectors were made. Reporters described foil, broken wooden members and balloon rubber. Army technical documentation for the ML-307 family of pilot-balloon targets describes lightweight, paper-backed aluminium reflecting surfaces supported by balsa-wood sticks and carried beneath a balloon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology.patrickgross.org/rw/p/rdr9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology.patrickgross.org">[Ufology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, Roswell Daily Record, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

That resemblance is central to understanding Ramey’s explanation. A RAWIN target was not a solid metal instrument or a sophisticated electronic box. It was deliberately flimsy: essentially a collapsible geometric reflector whose broad metallic surfaces made an otherwise difficult-to-track balloon conspicuous to radar. Once broken and crumpled on the ground, such a device naturally resolved into exactly the sort of apparently unimpressive pile photographed at Fort Worth — shiny sheet material, snapped sticks and associated balloon remnants. Contemporary weather officer Irving [Newton]({{ 'newton/' | relative_url }}) therefore identified the displayed material as a balloon and RAWIN target, while Ramey presented that identification as the mundane explanation for Roswell’s supposed “flying disc”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/chicagotribune9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, Chicago Tribune, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

## The foil, sticks and rubber described by reporters

The most important point is that the [Fort Worth debris]({{ 'substitution/' | relative_url }}) was not merely called a weather device after the fact; contemporary descriptions recorded a distinctive combination of materials. Associated Press coverage described what arrived at Fort Worth as a bundle of “tinfoil, broken wood beams and rubber remnants of a balloon”. Another contemporary account characterised it as a mass of broken wooden stays wrapped in foil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology.patrickgross.org/rw/p/rdr9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology.patrickgross.org">[Ufology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, Roswell Daily Record, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

The account attributed to rancher W. W. “Mac” Brazel contained a remarkably similar inventory. The *Roswell Daily Record* reported a large area of bright wreckage consisting of rubber strips, tinfoil, tough paper and sticks. When gathered, Brazel said, the foil, paper, tape and sticks could be compressed into a bundle roughly three feet long and only seven or eight inches thick, with the rubber forming a separate small bundle. He estimated the whole collection at about five pounds. The same account said there was no sign of an engine or propeller, while at least one paper fin appeared to have been attached to foil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rr0.org/time/1/9/4/7/07/09/RoswellDailyRecord_2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rr0.org">[rr0.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rr0.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Harassed Rancher Who Located &#x27;Saucer&#x27; Sorry He Told About It</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

Those details matter because a radar reflector did not resemble an aircraft after it had collapsed. Its functional shape existed only while its lightweight frame held the reflecting panels in their designed geometry. Break the frame, tear or fold the panels and separate it from its lifting balloon, and the recognisable object disappears. What remains is precisely a mixture of sheets, paper-like backing, narrow wooden pieces, tape or fastenings and balloon material.

The bright appearance is particularly unsurprising. The Army’s technical description of the ML-307 family specifies triangular surfaces made from light, paper-backed aluminium foil supported by balsa sticks. A later Air Force examination of an example likewise described aluminium-coloured foil-like material over stronger paper-like material, attached to balsa sticks with tape, glue and twine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radionerds.com/images/2/26/TM_11-487G.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radionerds.com">[RadioNerds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radionerds.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span></span></span>

This is one reason the shorthand phrase “weather balloon” can obscure rather than clarify [the Fort Worth photographs]({{ 'the-fort-worth-photographs/' | relative_url }}). The shiny material was not supposed to be the balloon itself. The balloon provided lift; the metallic structure hanging beneath it provided a radar echo.

## How a balloon-borne radar reflector was assembled

A pilot balloon could be followed visually to calculate winds aloft, but visual tracking became impossible once it rose beyond useful sight or entered conditions in which observers could no longer follow it. Radar offered another method — provided that the balloon carried something capable of producing a sufficiently useful radar return. Contemporary reporting quoted Newton explaining that these targets were used because balloons could rise higher than the eye could see.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology.patrickgross.org/rw/p/rdr9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology.patrickgross.org">[Ufology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, Roswell Daily Record, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

The ML-307 was designed for that job. An Army technical manual describes the ML-307/AP, ML-307A/AP and ML-307B/AP as reflectors attached to 100- or 350-gram pilot balloons to assist radar tracking. The reflector consisted of multiple triangular surfaces of paper-backed aluminium foil supported by balsa sticks. It weighed only about 100 grams and folded into a flat triangle for shipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radionerds.com/images/2/26/TM_11-487G.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radionerds.com">[RadioNerds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radionerds.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span></span></span>

The construction makes sense once the competing requirements are understood. A balloon payload had to remain extremely light, so heavy sheet metal and a substantial frame were undesirable. At the same time, the target needed broad conducting surfaces arranged in a geometry that efficiently returned radar energy. Thin metallic reflecting panels supplied the radar-visible surface, while balsa supplied enough stiffness to hold those panels in shape without imposing much weight.

Later Army documentation describes the ML-307/AP as a collapsible radar reflector whose aluminium-foil surfaces were reinforced with wood to prevent collapse while airborne. It gives an assembled size of approximately 50 inches long, 50 inches wide and 37 inches high, again with a weight of about 100 grams.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.analogweather.com/uploads/7/7/7/5/77750690/tm-750-5-3_meteorological_equipment_data_sheets_1977.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: analogweather.com">[Analog Weather]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">analogweather.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analog Weather TM</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TECHNICAL MANUAL METEOROLOGICAL...</span></span></span>

The result was therefore much larger in flight than its mass or wreckage might suggest. An intact reflector presented an angular, three-dimensional collection of shiny planes. The same object after impact could be flattened into a surprisingly small heap because the reflecting panels were thin and the supporting structure was merely lightweight wood. That distinction between **assembled geometry** and **collapsed materials** is the key to understanding why the Fort Worth photographs look the way they do.

It also explains the apparently kite-like quality noted in 1947 reporting. One newspaper described the Roswell wreckage as a “kite-like” wind target suspended beneath a balloon, while contemporary newspaper imagery elsewhere showed foil-covered radar targets being launched with balloons for wind observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/cincinattienquirer9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and the Ohio Roswell, July 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span> The resemblance was functional rather than accidental: both a kite and a RAWIN reflector use lightweight members to maintain a much larger surface or framework.

## Why the broken target looked so unremarkable

Seen without its original shape, a radar reflector can look less like a piece of military equipment than like packaging material. That is an important feature of the Roswell photographs rather than a contradiction within Ramey’s account.

Consider what happens to each component when the target comes down. The balsa framework can snap into narrow sticks. The large reflecting surfaces can tear, fold and crumple into sheets of foil-backed material. Tape, glue, twine and other attachment points cease to make obvious sense once the geometry has collapsed. The lifting balloon can tear into pieces of rubber. Army documentation confirms that these were all ordinary structural ingredients of the radar target and balloon system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.users.globalnet.co.uk/~hex/ufo/text/pages026-050/page33.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: users.globalnet.co.uk">[Globalnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">users.globalnet.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air Force Roswell Report...</span></span></span>

Brazel’s reported difficulty reconstructing the wreckage is consistent with this problem of lost geometry, although it does not by itself prove the identification. The *Roswell Daily Record* said that Brazel and the military personnel tried to put the material back together but could not reconstruct it or make a workable “kite” from it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/roswelldailychronicle9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Roswell Daily Chronicle, July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span> A broken reflector would not necessarily advertise its original three-dimensional arrangement to someone examining disconnected sticks and panels.

This distinction also helps explain why descriptions such as “tinfoil” can be misleading to a modern reader. The technical target was not simply a loose sheet of household foil. The Army manual specifies **paper-backed aluminium foil**, supported on balsa members.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radionerds.com/images/2/26/TM_11-487G.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radionerds.com">[RadioNerds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radionerds.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span></span></span> The later Air Force investigation, working from recovered specifications and an example of the equipment, likewise described a foil-like aluminium surface bonded to a stronger paper-like backing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.users.globalnet.co.uk/~hex/ufo/text/pages026-050/page33.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: users.globalnet.co.uk">[Globalnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">users.globalnet.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air Force Roswell Report...</span></span></span> The combination closely matches the otherwise slightly odd pairing of “tinfoil” and “rather tough paper” in Brazel’s 1947 description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rr0.org/time/1/9/4/7/07/09/RoswellDailyRecord_2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rr0.org">[rr0.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rr0.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Harassed Rancher Who Located &#x27;Saucer&#x27; Sorry He Told About It</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

## Why the geometry made it a radar target

The foil was not there because the device needed to be shiny. Its important property was electrical conductivity. Radar works by transmitting radio waves and detecting energy reflected back towards the receiving system. A small rubber balloon by itself is a poor radar target compared with a deliberately constructed metallic reflector. The ML-307 supplied reflective surfaces arranged so that the balloon could be tracked at altitude.

Army documentation explicitly calls the device a **reflector** and states that its purpose was to assist radar tracking of a pilot balloon. The manual associates the ML-307 family with contemporary radar sets including the SCR-584 and SCR-614.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radionerds.com/images/2/26/TM_11-487G.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radionerds.com">[RadioNerds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radionerds.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span></span></span> Later military documentation similarly describes its primary use as assisting radar tracking so that upper-wind direction and velocity could be determined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.analogweather.com/uploads/7/7/7/5/77750690/tm-750-5-3_meteorological_equipment_data_sheets_1977.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: analogweather.com">[Analog Weather]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">analogweather.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analog Weather TM</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TECHNICAL MANUAL METEOROLOGICAL...</span></span></span>

This makes the three principal materials intelligible as parts of one mechanism rather than miscellaneous debris:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Aluminium-covered panels** provided the radar-reflecting surfaces.
* **Balsa sticks** held those surfaces in the required geometric arrangement while keeping the payload light.
* **The rubber balloon** supplied the lift that carried the target high enough for meteorological wind tracking.

</div>

The wreckage shown at Fort Worth therefore contained materials with complementary functions in a known balloon-and-radar system. The foil was not merely decorative; the sticks were not arbitrary splinters; and the rubber was not an unrelated scrap. Together they formed the material signature expected from a lightweight radar reflector carried by a balloon.

## Why those components mattered to Ramey’s explanation

Ramey’s explanation gained plausibility from this physical fit. He did not need the displayed debris to resemble a conventional weather instrument with gauges, dials or electronics. His claim was that it included a RAWIN target — a device whose useful feature was its large reflective geometry when assembled, not sophisticated machinery. Contemporary reports consequently described the object as the crushed remains of a target used to measure wind direction and velocity at high altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/santafenewmexican9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell debris explained, July 9July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

Newton’s role was especially important for that reason. He was a forecaster at the Fort Worth Army weather station rather than simply another officer in Ramey’s command chain. Contemporary reports say that he recognised the material as a RAWIN target and noted that roughly 80 US weather stations were using this type of balloon equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/cincinattienquirer9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and the Ohio Roswell, July 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span> In other words, the official identification was tied to equipment that a weather specialist was expected to recognise.

Decades later, Newton again maintained that the material he had inspected in Ramey’s office was the remains of a balloon and RAWIN target. That retrospective testimony is less immediate than the July 1947 newspaper reports and should therefore be treated separately, but it is consistent with the identification recorded at the time. The Air Force’s later Roswell investigation also located technical drawings for the ML-307 series and compared its foil, paper, balsa, tape and other construction materials with descriptions of the recovered wreckage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.users.globalnet.co.uk/~hex/ufo/text/pages026-050/page33.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: users.globalnet.co.uk">[Globalnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">users.globalnet.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air Force Roswell Report...</span></span></span>

There is an important limit to what this resemblance establishes. Demonstrating that the **displayed Fort Worth debris** matches the construction of a balloon-borne radar reflector does not, by itself, settle every later dispute over whether those photographs show all of the material originally recovered near Roswell. Some later Roswell witnesses and researchers alleged that ordinary balloon equipment had been substituted for more unusual debris before the photographs were taken. That is a separate evidential dispute. The narrower mechanical question addressed here is much clearer: the material visible and described at Fort Worth genuinely had the expected physical ingredients of a RAWIN-type radar target and balloon.

## The simplest way to read the Fort Worth debris

The Fort Worth display becomes easier to understand when viewed from function backwards. Start with the Army’s need to track a small balloon by radar. That requires a lightweight reflector. Making one from paper-backed aluminium surfaces produces the necessary radar-reflective area without much mass. Holding those surfaces in the correct three-dimensional shape requires a very light framework, hence balsa sticks. Carrying the assembly aloft requires a balloon, hence the rubber remnants. Army technical manuals describe essentially that construction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radionerds.com/images/2/26/TM_11-487G.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radionerds.com">[RadioNerds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radionerds.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radio Nerds Technical Manual</span></span></span>

Now reverse the process. A balloon descends or ruptures; its rubber collapses. The reflector hits the ground; its balsa frame breaks and its broad panels crumple. Instead of an angular radar target, the observer finds scattered rubber, shiny foil-like sheets, paper, tape and sticks. That is strikingly close to both the contemporary descriptions of the Roswell material and the debris reporters saw in Ramey’s office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/roswelldailychronicle9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Roswell Daily Chronicle, July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

That material correspondence is the strongest reason the displayed debris resembled a radar target. Whatever conclusions are drawn about the wider Roswell controversy or disputed readings of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), Ramey’s public explanation was attached to a physically coherent piece of known 1940s technology: a fragile balloon-borne radar reflector whose construction naturally produced the foil, narrow wooden members and rubber visible in the Fort Worth presentation.

      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
