## Introduction

Barry [Greenwood]({{ 'greenwood/' | relative_url }})’s news-teletype interpretation of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) drew part of its force from punctuation. In his 2004 argument, Greenwood pointed to what he regarded as ordinary commas, full stops and quotation marks in the photographed sheet and contrasted them with military traffic in which punctuation could be written out as abbreviations such as “PD” and “CMA”. If those marks really were present, he argued, the document looked more like journalistic copy than a formal military transmission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://id.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: id.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">id.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young AdultMarch 1, 2004...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2004</span></span></span>

Brad Sparks challenged that distinction at its weakest point: [military communications]({{ 'military-communication/' | relative_url }}) were not uniform enough to make ordinary punctuation a civilian fingerprint. Later summaries of the dispute record Sparks finding period military messages that used actual periods and commas, while surviving communications material confirms that military teletypewriters were technically capable of transmitting punctuation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span> The result is important but limited. Sparks's objection substantially weakens punctuation as a discriminator; it does not, by itself, establish that the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) sheet was military.

## The formatting clues Greenwood treated as journalistic

Greenwood was not simply attempting to recognise isolated words in the famously blurred photograph. He tried to identify the *kind of document* Roger Ramey was holding. That distinction matters because a civilian newswire and a formal military message would create different expectations about layout, addressing, signatures and punctuation.

His punctuation argument concentrated on two portions of the disputed text on which several interpreters had reported at least partial agreement. One appeared to end with something resembling “AT FORT WORTH, TEX.”; another appeared to contain “disc” or “disk” in quotation marks. Greenwood regarded the apparent comma, full stop and quotation marks as significant because the journalistic teletype examples he examined freely used conventional punctuation. He contrasted that appearance with military examples in which punctuation was represented textually rather than by the marks themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://id.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: id.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">id.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young AdultMarch 1, 2004...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2004</span></span></span>

There was a genuine historical basis for noticing that difference. Military communications did employ abbreviations for punctuation. A declassified US communications reference lists “CMA” for comma and “PD” for period, alongside other specialised abbreviations. An original Army teletype order concerning Japan's cessation of hostilities in 1945 likewise rendered commas as “CMA” and periods as “PD”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_223/41760759079988.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">APPENDIX 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">APPENDIX 4 - SERVICE TERMINOLOGY &amp; STEREOTYPES; MAIN DOCUMENT NOT SPECIFIED...</span></span></span>

So Greenwood had identified a real military convention. The problem was the stronger inference that followed from it. Evidence that *some* military traffic encoded punctuation as words does not establish that *all relevant military traffic* did so, still less that a sheet carrying ordinary punctuation must therefore be journalistic. That is the opening Sparks exploited.

## Brad Sparks's military-message objection

Sparks's response was essentially a counterexample argument. As later summarised in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration*, he maintained that examination of military messages from the relevant era showed periods and commas appearing as ordinary punctuation rather than invariably being rendered as abbreviations. That meant Greenwood's proposed diagnostic was not exclusive: a feature offered as evidence of civilian news copy could also occur in military traffic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

This is more than a technical quibble because the Ramey memo contains so little confidently recoverable information. When only a handful of marks can be discussed at all, assigning strong evidential weight to punctuation risks magnifying a convention that was variable in actual use.

The broader technological evidence also favours caution. US Army doctrine described teletypewriter communication as capable of transmitting “punctuation, symbols, and text arrangement”, while technical documentation for military teletypewriters shows standard communications keyboards equipped with comma and period characters. In other words, ordinary punctuation was not inherently incompatible with the military transmission equipment surrounding this period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/913248360/FM100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Army Signal Communications Doctrine &#124; PDF &#124; Communication &#124; Staff (Military</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Army Signal Communications Doctrine &#124; PDF &#124; Communication &#124; Staff (Military)...</span></span></span>

That does not mean “PD” and “CMA” were imaginary conventions. Rather, the form depended partly on the communications system and procedure involved. One contemporary debate over the Ramey document, summarised by Tim Printy, records the argument that spelled-out punctuation was particularly associated with voice and radiotelegraph traffic, where [ambiguity]({{ 'ambiguity/' | relative_url }}) in transmitting punctuation presented different problems, and was not necessarily characteristic of teletype or telex traffic. Printy judged the evidence mixed: punctuation written as words appeared common in examples close to the period, but Sparks had demonstrated that conventional punctuation could occur as well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

This distinction is crucial. “Military message” was not a single physical format produced by one universal machine under one invariant set of conventions. Messages could move through different communications channels, be prepared or reproduced at different stages, and vary according to purpose and command practice. Treating a formatting habit from one class of military traffic as a universal rule therefore creates a classification risk.

Sparks went further by proposing that the Ramey document might represent relatively informal communication between senior officers — sometimes described in accounts of the debate as a “general-to-general” or back-channel message. Such traffic, he argued, could also explain another feature Greenwood considered suspicious: an originating officer's name appearing as a signature rather than the document following the format expected of routine command traffic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

Independent historical material makes the general point about signatures plausible. A declassified US military-communications reference states that the originator's identity could appear as a signature at the end of a military message and could consist of a name and rank or command designation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_223/41760759079988.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">APPENDIX 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">APPENDIX 4 - SERVICE TERMINOLOGY &amp; STEREOTYPES; MAIN DOCUMENT NOT SPECIFIED...</span></span></span> This does not prove Sparks's specific reconstruction of the Ramey sheet, but it shows why neither punctuation nor a terminal name can safely be treated as uniquely journalistic features.

## How much document format can actually decide

Sparks's challenge does not simply reverse Greenwood's conclusion. It changes the evidential question. The relevant test is no longer “Does this sheet contain punctuation that looks journalistic?” but “Are its observable formatting features sufficiently exclusive to journalism to rule out plausible military formats?” On the evidence available, punctuation fails that stronger test.

A useful way to separate the claims is:

* **Greenwood was right that “PD” and similar punctuation abbreviations existed in military communications.** Surviving military material documents exactly such conventions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_223/41760759079988.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">APPENDIX 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">APPENDIX 4 - SERVICE TERMINOLOGY &amp; STEREOTYPES; MAIN DOCUMENT NOT SPECIFIED...</span></span></span>
* **Sparks was right that ordinary punctuation was not forbidden or unknown in military traffic.** Contemporary-era examples and military teletypewriter capabilities undermine any categorical rule that commas or full stops identify civilian copy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>
* **Neither observation identifies the Ramey document by itself.** The photographed text is too degraded, and military message practice too varied, for punctuation alone to distinguish confidently between a news teletype and every possible military communication format.

This leaves Greenwood's larger formatting case in a more nuanced position. Tim Printy's detailed review, while acknowledging that Sparks had weakened the punctuation objection, still found problems with attempts to reconstruct the sheet as a standard military message: the proposed originator/addressee arrangement, absence of a recognisable date-time group and message numbering, and other layout features did not fit comfortably with the examples he examined. Significantly, Printy noted that the 1947 senior-officer messages supplied by Sparks could contain an officer's name at the end yet still retained recognisable military addressing conventions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

That is why punctuation should not become a proxy for the whole Greenwood-Sparks dispute. Sparks successfully attacks one discriminator without necessarily validating an alternative document identification. A comma can be compatible with military traffic while the rest of a proposed military reconstruction remains problematic.

The surviving historical record reinforces this caution about rigid formatting rules. US military communications documentation shows both specialised punctuation abbreviations and considerable variation in how identifying information could appear. The declassified reference preserved by the National Security Agency, for example, describes several possible positions for an originator's or addressee's identity rather than prescribing a single visual arrangement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_223/41760759079988.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">APPENDIX 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">APPENDIX 4 - SERVICE TERMINOLOGY &amp; STEREOTYPES; MAIN DOCUMENT NOT SPECIFIED...</span></span></span> Older US Signal Corps regulations likewise show that military telegraph practice was capable of preserving punctuation as supplied: operators were instructed that when punctuation marks occurred, they should be transmitted precisely as contained in the original message. Although those regulations considerably pre-date 1947 and cannot establish the specific Army Air Forces procedure in Ramey's headquarters, they demonstrate why sweeping claims that conventional punctuation was alien to American military telegraphy are historically unsafe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/49/Regulations_for_the_operation_and_maintenance_of_United_States_military_telegraph_lines_-_and_general_regulations_of_the_Signal_Corps%2C_United_States_Army_%28IA_cu31924030766475%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsRegulations for the operation and maintenance of United States military telegraph lines: and general regulations of the...</span></span></span>

## What the punctuation test can really tell us

The most defensible conclusion is narrower than either side's strongest formulation. Apparent commas, periods or quotation marks in the Ramey memo cannot reliably classify it as a newspaper teletype. Sparks's counterexamples and military communications documentation show that ordinary punctuation was possible in military traffic, so Greenwood's punctuation clue cannot carry the exclusionary weight originally placed upon it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

But the reverse proposition is equally unwarranted. Demonstrating that military messages *could* contain conventional punctuation does not show that this particular document was a military message, much less a secret Roswell report. Printy's comparison found no clean match between the disputed reconstruction and the standard military formats he examined, even after taking Sparks's examples into account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

That leaves punctuation as supporting evidence rather than a decisive test. It may contribute to a document identification when combined with securely readable headers, date-time groups, routing information, signatures, line lengths and [provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}). On the Ramey photograph, however, precisely those higher-value identifiers remain uncertain or contested.

The punctuation challenge therefore produces a useful methodological correction within Barry Greenwood's competing interpretation. His newswire hypothesis remains a possible explanation of the sheet, but ordinary punctuation is not the strong newswire fingerprint it initially appeared to be. Sparks did not prove a military origin; he showed that one of the supposed boundaries between military and journalistic teletypes was porous. Given a photograph in which even individual letters are disputed, that distinction matters: format can narrow possibilities only when the convention being used is genuinely diagnostic, and in this case punctuation is not.
