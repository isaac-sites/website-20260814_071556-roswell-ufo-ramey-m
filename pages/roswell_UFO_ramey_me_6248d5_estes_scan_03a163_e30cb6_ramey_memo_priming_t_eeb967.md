## Introduction

A 2002 experiment by psychologist James Houran and Roswell researcher Kevin D. Randle tested a deceptively simple question about the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}): if different people looked at the same blurred writing but were told different stories about what it concerned, would they read different words? The answer was largely yes. Participants primed with a Roswell UFO scenario reported Roswell-compatible terms; participants told the document concerned secret atomic-bomb testing produced words such as “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”; people given no subject at all produced far fewer interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_estes_scan_03a163_e30cb6_ramey_memo_priming_t_eeb967-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Priming Test illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_estes_scan_03a163_e30cb6_ramey_memo_priming_t_eeb967-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_estes_scan_03a163_e30cb6_ramey_memo_priming_t_eeb967-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

Yet the experiment did not show that *everything* supposedly visible in the memo was imaginary. Several fragments appeared repeatedly across different groups, notably “Fort Worth TX”, “[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})” and “weather balloons”. That combination — context-sensitive readings alongside a smaller core of cross-group agreement — is the study's most important finding. It helps explain why ever sharper scans, including the high-resolution work associated with Russ Estes, do not by themselves settle disputes over transcription. Once letter shapes become sufficiently ambiguous, the problem is no longer purely one of magnification. It is also one of interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## How three groups were given three different memos

Houran and Randle recruited 176 participants, aged 18 to 68, and randomly assigned them to three conditions. The physical evidence shown to them was essentially the same; what changed was the introductory frame. Fifty-nine participants were told that the military memo might contain information about the crash, retrieval and subsequent cover-up of an extraterrestrial craft near Roswell in July 1947. Fifty-eight were told it might concern secret testing of the atomic bomb. The remaining 59 were simply asked to read a photograph of a document, without being told what it might discuss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This was more substantial than asking people to squint at a small reproduction on a page. Participants could inspect an 8-by-10-inch enlargement of the memo area and computer displays derived from four high-resolution images then available through Stanton Friedman's CD-ROM collection. They were also supplied with a magnifying glass, and a research assistant who did not know the researchers' hypotheses could help them manipulate the displayed images. There was no imposed time limit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The design therefore isolated an especially relevant variable: **[expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }})**. The Roswell group and atomic group were looking at the same ambiguous marks, but each had been handed a different semantic map before beginning. The blind group had no such map.

That matters because ambiguous visual information does not necessarily force a single interpretation. Modern research on ambiguous vision more generally shows that contextual information and prior expectations can bias which interpretation becomes perceptually dominant when the sensory evidence permits several possibilities. That does not mean observers deliberately invent what they report seeing. Rather, context can help the visual system resolve uncertain input in one direction instead of another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3282309/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)United we sense, divided we fail: context-driven perception of ambiguous visual stimuli - PMCApril 5, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 5, 2012</span></span></span>

## The words changed when the story changed

The clearest result was not simply that people disagreed. Their disagreements tended to follow the scenario they had been given.

Participants in the Roswell condition reported such readings as “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”, along with “Fort Worth TX”, “story”, “weather balloons” and “land”. In the atomic-bomb condition, different possibilities emerged: “glasses”, “morning”, “flash”, “atomic”, “laboratory” and “meaning”, as well as some of the words also reported elsewhere. The blind participants generated a much thinner set, including “Fort Worth TX”, “flew”, “story” and “balloons”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The contrast is particularly revealing because the atomic-bomb frame was deliberately unrelated to the usual Roswell transcription dispute. Once participants were told to expect atomic testing, ambiguous character shapes suddenly became capable of supporting a vocabulary appropriate to *that* narrative. Houran and Randle specifically noted the appearance of “flash”, “glasses” and “atomic” after the context was changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The effect also appeared quantitatively. The Roswell group averaged 4.6 deciphered words and the atomic group 4.8, whereas the blind group averaged only 1.6. The difference between the contextualised groups and the blind condition was statistically significant in the authors' analysis. In other words, giving people a meaningful story did not merely change *which* words they reported; it was associated with their finding considerably more words in the noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There was an important complication. The Roswell participants spent an average of about 20 minutes on the task, compared with 16 minutes for the atomic group and 14 for the blind group. Houran and Randle therefore recognised that priming and motivation were intertwined: an intriguing story could make somebody search harder as well as direct what they expected to find. The experiment consequently cannot cleanly reduce every difference to a single psychological mechanism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## A few fragments survived the change of context

The experiment becomes more useful when its [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) and positive findings are considered together. If contextual suggestion had generated essentially everything participants reported, there would be little reason to trust any transcription. That was not what happened.

Across the three conditions, participants repeatedly identified several words in the same positions. Houran and Randle reported cross-group agreement on “Fort Worth TX” from 52 participants, “story” from 61 and “weather balloons” from 27. “Land” was also reported in the same location by 47 people across the two primed conditions, although it did not have the same three-way support. Participants in every condition also perceived punctuation-like marks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction is central to interpreting the experiment. It separates two categories of claimed transcription:

**Relatively robust fragments** are readings that recur at the same location when observers have been given different expectations. Their persistence makes a purely scenario-driven explanation less persuasive, although it still does not guarantee that every letter has been identified correctly.

**Context-sensitive fragments** appear primarily when observers have been told a story that makes those words meaningful. “UFO” under a Roswell frame and “atomic” under an atomic-testing frame are the obvious examples. Such readings demand much more caution because the experiment demonstrates that the underlying marks can accommodate different narratives.

The authors therefore did not conclude that the memo was completely unreadable. They argued instead that *some* portions appeared legible while the larger meaning remained uncertain. That is a more consequential finding than either extreme — “the memo has been decoded” or “nothing can be read” — because it explains how genuine-looking fragments and unreliable reconstruction can coexist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why this changes the standard for a convincing transcription

The priming experiment directly challenges a common method of deciphering the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo: begin with partially recognisable characters, consult what is known about Roswell in July 1947, and then search for historically plausible words that fit the remaining shapes.

That procedure can feel rigorous because historical context normally *is* valuable when reading damaged documents. David Rudiak, one of the memo's best-known interpreters, advocated using letter spacing, probable word lengths, grammar, syntax and historical knowledge to constrain possible readings. Houran and Randle agreed that structural checks such as letter spacing and word length could be useful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Their experiment exposed the danger in allowing historical context to become the deciding factor, however. If an observer decides that a blurred sequence probably concerns Roswell and then searches Roswell vocabulary for a plausible fit, the context used to validate the word may also have helped generate the word in the first place. The atomic group demonstrated the problem neatly: change the presumed historical story and some of the perceived vocabulary changes with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This creates a form of circular reasoning that is particularly difficult to detect. A proposed word may appear to confirm a Roswell interpretation because it fits Roswell history, while Roswell history was itself one of the reasons the ambiguous shapes were interpreted as that word. The observer may be entirely sincere; the methodological problem remains.

The study also found associations between the number of words reported and participants' characteristics, including their previous exposure to UFO and Roswell material and measures concerning tolerance of ambiguity. That reinforced the authors' broader concern that transcription was not being determined by image information alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## The experiment had limits of its own

The Houran-Randle study should not be treated as a definitive psychological demolition of every Ramey memo transcription. Its design had weaknesses, and critics within the Roswell debate challenged it.

The participants were a convenience sample rather than professional document examiners. They also spent much less time on the images than dedicated memo researchers who had studied particular character shapes repeatedly. David Rudiak subsequently argued, among other criticisms, that participants had not devoted enough time to the material and lacked the contextual knowledge necessary for a proper reconstruction. A later review by Randle records those objections while maintaining that the experiment remained relevant as a test of context effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

There is a more concrete limitation in the original data. When a referee asked for the number of participants in each experimental condition who had reported each individual word, the researchers discovered that the original scoring sheets had been discarded after responses were entered into a computer database and notes on common words were prepared. They consequently lacked detailed participant-by-participant records for the full set of reported words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That loss restricts how thoroughly later researchers can reanalyse the most interesting part of the experiment. The published lists show striking scenario-specific differences, but the surviving dataset is not as complete as one would want for an experiment carrying substantial weight in a disputed historical case.

There were also later corrections and criticisms concerning reporting and statistics. Houran and Randle themselves published a correction after accidentally printing “Fort Work, Tex” instead of Russ Estes's tentative “Fort Worth, Tex”, using the error as an example of how transcription mistakes can enter even careful work. Later discussions acknowledged that Rudiak had identified some statistical typographical problems while arguing that these did not change the experiment's central conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/501208456/jse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; Falsifiability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; FalsifiabilityJanuary 1, 2002...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2002</span></span></span>

The safest reading, therefore, is not that the experiment scientifically established which proposed Ramey words are false. It established something narrower but still important: **the same low-information image was demonstrably capable of producing different, thematically appropriate readings when observers' expectations were changed**.

## What the test means for high-resolution scans


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

This is where the priming experiment connects directly with the limits exposed by the high-resolution examinations associated with Russ Estes. Estes's difficulty in reaching confident readings suggests that the photographic record contains too little clean character information in many places for magnification alone to settle the text. The Houran-Randle experiment examined what can happen psychologically once an observer encounters precisely that sort of uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

A sharper scan can make genuine recorded detail easier to inspect. It cannot guarantee that an apparent stroke belongs to one letter rather than another when the original photographic information does not discriminate between them. At that point, knowledge of Roswell, military terminology, expected sentence structure and proposed earlier transcriptions can begin supplying constraints. Some constraints are legitimate; others can quietly become prompts.

That is why Houran and Randle ultimately proposed a much stricter standard than another individual transcription. They recommended that the best available raw images be processed using standardised procedures and examined by at least three independent, blind laboratories experienced in reading archival material. Agreement would need to concern particular words **in particular positions**, permitting inter-rater reliability to be assessed rather than merely collecting plausible-looking reconstructions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Later technical work reinforces the value of that caution. A 2022 review reported blind or independent image-analysis efforts by researchers at the University of Ioannina, the Institut d'Optique in France and the City University of Hong Kong. Techniques including blind deconvolution, Fourier processing, homomorphic filtering, wavelets and deblurring could manipulate or modestly improve aspects of the images, but the reported analyses did not produce clearly interpretable text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

The priming experiment therefore changes the burden of proof surrounding the Ramey memo. A proposed transcription is strongest not when it produces an impressive coherent sentence after extensive [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) and historical reconstruction, but when readers who have **not** been told what they are supposed to find independently identify the same letters and words in the same places.

## What can be said with confidence

The experiment leaves the Ramey memo in an awkward but informative middle ground. It provides evidence that the photograph contains some recurring textual information: “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” proved substantially more resistant to changes in expectation than many other proposed readings. At the same time, it demonstrated that contextual framing could generate dramatically different vocabulary from the same ambiguous visual material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That makes controversial phrases carrying the greatest historical consequences precisely the ones that require the strongest independent validation. A reading such as “victims”, “remains”, “UFO”, “disc” or any comparable phrase cannot become reliable merely because it fits a reconstruction of Roswell events. The experiment showed why contextual fit is not independent evidence when context itself can influence perception.

Its lasting contribution is therefore methodological rather than a rival decipherment. The question is no longer simply, “Can somebody make these marks look like a particular word?” It is: **do independent readers, protected from knowledge of the desired answer, repeatedly recover that word from the image itself?**

For the most disputed portions of the Ramey memo, that standard has proved much harder to meet. And that is the important bridge between the limits of high-resolution scanning and the limits of human interpretation: when the photograph stops supplying enough unambiguous information, expectations can begin doing part of the reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
