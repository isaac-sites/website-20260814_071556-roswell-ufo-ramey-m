## Introduction

A fair independent-reading test of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) should control not only what readers are told, but **how long they are allowed to inspect the image**. That requirement emerges directly from James Houran and Kevin Randle’s published experiment: the Roswell-primed group spent an average of **20 minutes** on the memo, the atomic-bomb group **16 minutes**, and the blind group **14 minutes**. The study imposed no time limit. The groups therefore differed simultaneously in prior suggestion and in exposure time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_controlled_memo_view_1790b7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Viewing Time illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_controlled_memo_view_1790b7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_controlled_memo_view_1790b7-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That does not erase the experiment’s evidence that suggestion affected readings. It identifies an additional variable that future tests can remove. With such degraded text, six extra minutes represents roughly **43 per cent more inspection time than the blind group received on average**. A cleaner experiment would give [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) identical images, identical tools, identical instructions and an identical viewing period. Only then can differences in transcription be attributed more confidently to the factor being tested rather than to unequal opportunity to search the visual noise.

## How Viewing Time Differed Between Reader Groups

Houran and Randle randomly assigned 176 participants to three conditions. Fifty-nine were told that the photographed document might concern the Roswell crash and a government cover-up; 58 were told that it might concern secret atomic-bomb testing; and 59 were simply asked to read a photographed document, without being given a subject. All were shown an enlarged print and high-resolution computer images, could use a magnifying glass, and recorded what they thought they could perceive line by line. Crucially, the researchers stated explicitly that **no time limit was imposed**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The resulting viewing times formed a striking progression:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Roswell condition:** average 20 minutes.
* **Atomic-bomb condition:** average 16 minutes.
* **Blind condition:** average 14 minutes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

The number of reported words also differed. Roswell-primed participants averaged 4.6 deciphered words and atomic-bomb participants 4.8, while blind participants averaged only 1.6. The suggestion-condition effect on the number of words reported was statistically significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those two patterns cannot simply be treated as independent observations. The experiment manipulated contextual information, but because participants chose when to stop, that manipulation was also followed by different amounts of engagement with the image. The researchers themselves drew attention to this point. They argued that motivation was probably influencing performance, noting that the Roswell group examined the images longer and that an alleged UFO crash and cover-up might encourage harder searching than an unidentified old document. They offered a similar explanation for the greater output of the atomic-bomb group compared with the blind group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

In other words, the experiment uncovered something more complicated than a simple “primed versus blind” comparison. **The contextual [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) appears to have changed both what participants expected to find and how much effort they invested in finding it.**

## Why Longer Inspection Becomes a Hidden Confound

Extra viewing time is not inherently a source of error. For difficult visual material, more time can improve perception. Research outside the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) case confirms the broader point that exposure duration can materially affect visual performance. For example, an experimental eyewitness study found significantly better face-identification accuracy after 45 seconds of exposure than after 12 seconds. Research on ambiguous images likewise shows that recognisability changes substantially as presentation duration increases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14511547/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Exposure duration: effects on eyewitness accuracy and confidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exposure duration: effects on eyewitness accuracy and confidence - PubMed...</span></span></span>

That is precisely why unequal time is troublesome in the Ramey experiment. Suppose a Roswell-primed reader reports more words than a blind reader. At least three mechanisms could contribute:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Suggestion:** knowing the Roswell story makes particular interpretations more readily available.
2. **Motivation:** the dramatic context encourages the participant to search harder.
3. **Exposure:** searching longer provides more opportunities to detect genuine structure — or to construct plausible patterns from ambiguous marks.

</div>

The 2002 design was principally intended to test suggestion, but its self-paced procedure allowed the latter two mechanisms to vary along with it. Houran and Randle did not claim that viewing time alone explained their findings. Rather, their own discussion explicitly connected longer examination in the Roswell group with motivation and the number of perceived words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The distinction matters especially for this particular document because its photographic information is severely constrained. Earlier investigators had reached sharply different readings despite extensive [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) work. One professional documentarian described in the Houran-Randle paper scanned the material at 9,000 dpi and still said that he could not reliably decipher the text. A US government photo-analysis effort cited in the paper had likewise concluded that the available photographs were of insufficient quality to visualise the details sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

With a clear document, additional minutes might simply let a reader finish reading. With the Ramey image, the task is different: readers repeatedly inspect indistinct strokes, grain, gaps and partial character shapes while deciding whether they constitute letters. More search time therefore gives additional opportunities for both **real discrimination and interpretative fitting**.

That makes viewing time a particularly important control rather than a procedural nicety.

## The Time Difference Does Not Explain Everything

Equalising exposure should not be confused with dismissing the original experiment. Some of its findings are difficult to reduce to viewing time alone.

The atomic-bomb participants, for example, averaged slightly *more* reported words than the Roswell participants — 4.8 against 4.6 — despite spending less time on the task, 16 minutes against 20. More importantly, the *content* of readings changed with the supplied story. Roswell-primed participants produced Roswell-compatible terms, while atomic-bomb participants produced such interpretations as “flash”, “glasses”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. Blind participants produced comparatively few interpretations and no similarly strong thematic pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

So the original experiment still supplies evidence consistent with contextual suggestion affecting ambiguous readings. Equal viewing time would answer a narrower but important question: **how much of the difference survives once unequal persistence is removed?**

There was also genuine cross-group convergence. Participants in different conditions independently reported “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” at matching positions; the researchers regarded that agreement as evidence that at least portions of the photographed document may carry recoverable information. “Land” also received considerable agreement in the two suggested conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This is useful for designing a stronger follow-up. A controlled-time experiment should not merely count how many words each participant supplies. It should distinguish between **location-specific agreement across independent readers** and isolated guesses. If a proposed word is genuinely encoded strongly enough in the photograph, independent readers should tend to place it in the same location even when their inspection time and prior information are controlled.

## Equal Time Makes the Independent-Reader Test Cleaner

A stronger Ramey memo reader experiment could retain the best feature of the 2002 work — independent comparison — while removing self-selected exposure as a competing explanation.

The simplest design would give every reader the same fixed interval. The exact duration is less important than applying it consistently. Twenty minutes might be chosen because it matches the mean of the most persistent group in the original study; alternatively, researchers could pilot the task and select a period long enough for careful inspection without turning the exercise into an open-ended search. What matters is that one group cannot compensate for greater curiosity or prior interest simply by examining the document longer.

The procedure should also standardise the rest of the visual environment. Houran and Randle already recognised the importance of comparable raw data and computer enhancement when they recommended analyses by multiple independent, blind laboratories using standardised procedures. They argued that agreement should concern **specific words in precise locations**, rather than a general impression that different transcriptions sounded similar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For a reader experiment, that principle translates into several practical controls: identical image files and resolution; identical zoom and enhancement options; the same screen conditions; the same written instructions; no discussion between readers; no access to published Ramey transcriptions during the test; and a common countdown or automatic cut-off.

There is also a useful alternative to imposing only one duration. Researchers could randomly assign blind readers to predetermined exposure periods — for example, short, medium and long — while withholding all Roswell context from everyone. That would directly measure whether additional inspection increases agreement on the *same* words or merely increases the number and diversity of proposed readings. Such a design would separate the viewing-time question from the suggestion question instead of allowing the two to move together.

## Why “Let Experts Look as Long as They Need” Is a Different Test

A criticism of short reader experiments is that serious Ramey researchers have sometimes spent months or even longer studying the photographs. One historical example described in the Houran-Randle paper is Don Burleson, who reported spending a year working on his transcription. Other investigators used sophisticated cameras, microscopes, magnification and image-processing software. Yet their proposed readings still differed substantially.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This highlights an important distinction. **Unlimited expert decipherment and controlled independent reading answer different questions.**

An unlimited investigation asks: *How much information can a motivated analyst eventually extract using every available technique and source of contextual knowledge?*

A controlled reader experiment asks: *When observers receive equivalent evidence under equivalent conditions, do they independently perceive the same thing?*

For testing disputed wording, the second question is indispensable. Allowing every participant unlimited time sounds fair because nobody is formally restricted, but it does not produce equal exposure. A Roswell enthusiast might spend an hour while a genuinely blind participant decides after ten minutes that no further characters can be resolved. The resulting transcriptions would then compare different levels of effort as well as different perceptions.

Fixed exposure sacrifices some freedom in exchange for experimental comparability. If researchers want to preserve prolonged analysis, they can instead require a **minimum and maximum session of identical length**, or record intermediate transcriptions at fixed checkpoints. For example, every reader could submit what they see after 10, 20 and 40 minutes. That would reveal whether apparent words emerge quickly, gain independent support with further inspection, or appear only after extended searching.

## What a Fair Result Would Look Like

The strongest outcome would not be the longest transcription. It would be **independent convergence under controlled conditions**.

If equally timed, blind readers repeatedly identify the same character sequence at the same coordinates, [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) in that sequence should increase. If longer exposure increases agreement on a disputed word across independent observers, that would suggest that additional inspection is extracting useful signal. If longer exposure instead produces more words but less agreement, the extra output would be much harder to treat as reliable decipherment.

That distinction is already foreshadowed by the 2002 findings. The study found both suggestion-dependent readings and a small set of cross-condition agreements. Its authors therefore did not conclude simply that the memo was unreadable. They argued for independent, blind analysis and inter-rater agreement — essentially asking whether separate readers or laboratories converge on the same words without being led towards them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Equal viewing time is a logical extension of that standard. The original experiment showed that the Roswell narrative was associated not merely with different interpretations but with **six additional minutes of average inspection compared with the blind condition**. Future independent-reader tests should therefore blind the readers to disputed interpretations and put them on the same clock. Only then does “independent” mean that the principal differences between readers come from what they can actually extract from the photograph, rather than from how compelling a story made them keep looking.
