## Introduction

Barry Greenwood and David Rudiak offer a particularly revealing test of the Ramey memo because, in one disputed passage, they do not merely disagree over a letter or two. They reconstruct two substantially different sentences from roughly the same blurred marks. Greenwood reads the passage as a reference to Roswell public-information officer Walter Haut under the contemporary press misspelling “[Warren Haught]({{ 'warren-haught/' | relative_url }})”, while Rudiak reconstructs language about B-29 or C-47 transport, [Wright Field]({{ 'wright-field/' | relative_url }}) and activity at Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greenwood UFO ArchiveFROM OUT OF THE PAST - Barry Greenwood UFO ArchiveSeptember 6, 2009 — titled, “An Observation on the Ramey Memo.” (h...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2009</span></span></span>

## Greenwood's public-relations-officer reconstruction

[Greenwood]({{ 'greenwood/' | relative_url }})'s disputed passage is usually rendered:

&gt; “WARREN HAUGHT, PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER AT ROSWELL, SAID.”<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[greenwoodufoarchive.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greenwood UFO ArchiveFROM OUT OF THE PAST - Barry Greenwood UFO ArchiveSeptember 6, 2009 — titled, “An Observation on the Ramey Memo.” (h...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2009</span></span></span>

The important point is that Greenwood did not arrive at “Haught” simply by looking for Walter Haut's real name. In his 2009 discussion he explained that he had returned to contemporary Roswell newspaper coverage and encountered the erroneous name “Warren Haught”. Seeing a six-letter surname ending in “GHT” in genuine July 1947 reporting gave him a concrete alternative to readings that treated those marks as part of military terminology. He then argued that the neighbouring shapes could accommodate “PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER AT ROSWELL, SAID”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greenwood UFO ArchiveFROM OUT OF THE PAST - Barry Greenwood UFO ArchiveSeptember 6, 2009 — titled, “An Observation on the Ramey Memo.” (h...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2009</span></span></span>

There is a real historical anchor for this interpretation. Contemporary press coverage did call the Roswell officer “Warren Haught”. Surviving [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) of Associated Press coverage identify “Lieut. Warren Haught” as the public-information officer at Roswell Army Air Field, while United Press material used wording identifying Haught as the public-relations officer. The name was wrong — the officer was Walter Haut — but that error is precisely what makes Greenwood's proposal interesting. “Warren Haught” was language circulating through the news system during the Roswell [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}), rather than a phrase invented decades later to fit the photograph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paradigmresearchgroup.org/News_Items-3.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paradigmresearchgroup.org">[Paradigm Research Group]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paradigmresearchgroup.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paradigmresearchgroup.org.</span></span></span>

Greenwood consequently treated the passage as support for his wider proposal that Ramey might have been holding a journalistic teletype or news copy. In that framework, “Warren Haught” and a description of him as Roswell's public-information or public-relations officer would be entirely ordinary. Greenwood also argued that other comparatively recognisable material in the photographed sheet — including “FORT WORTH, TEX.” and “DISC” in quotation marks — could be found in contemporary news reporting and that the document's punctuation looked compatible with journalistic teletype practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greenwood UFO ArchiveFROM OUT OF THE PAST - Barry Greenwood UFO ArchiveSeptember 6, 2009 — titled, “An Observation on the Ramey Memo.” (h...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2009</span></span></span>

But the strength of the historical match must be separated from the strength of the photographic identification. Finding a genuine 1947 phrase that fits a proposed reading demonstrates that Greenwood's reconstruction has contextual plausibility. It does not independently demonstrate that those particular blurred marks actually encode that phrase. That distinction becomes crucial when Rudiak's competing reconstruction is placed alongside it.

## Rudiak's military-language reconstruction

Rudiak reads essentially the same region very differently. Versions of his reconstruction give the passage approximately as:

> “BY B29-ST OR C47. WRIGHT AF ASSESS AIRFOIL AT ROSWELL. ASSURE”

with variants including “ASSIST” and “FLY-OUT” or “FLIGHTS” for some uncertain groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This is not a minor alternative spelling of Greenwood's sentence. Where Greenwood sees a person's name and occupational title, Rudiak sees aircraft designations and a reference to Wright Field. The opening area that becomes “WARREN HAUGHT” in Greenwood's reconstruction is incorporated into Rudiak's “B29-ST OR C47. WRIGHT AF” sequence; material Greenwood takes as “PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER” becomes language Rudiak connects with assessment or flight activity. Both retain Roswell towards the end of the disputed region, but they reach it through radically different sentences. A recent specialist comparison reproduces the two readings side by side for precisely this reason.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/the-missing-u/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo The missing U</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoThe missing U - UnlockingTheMysteryWARREN HAUGHT, PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER AT ROSWELL base released a statement in the name of...</span></span></span>

Rudiak's version belongs to a broader reconstruction in which the sheet is interpreted as a military communication concerning the Roswell recovery. His extended transcription includes such proposed phrases as “VICTIMS OF THE WRECK”, transport by “B29-ST OR C47”, “WRIGHT AF”, and later references to “WEATHER BALLOONS”. Rudiak has therefore argued that the memo records military handling of unusual wreckage and an accompanying public explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Context consequently works in opposite directions for the two interpreters. Once Greenwood recognises the marks as “Haught”, contemporary press reports supply an obvious continuation: Haught was Roswell's public-information officer, and newspapers were reporting his statements. Once Rudiak recognises aircraft and Wright Field terminology, surrounding portions of his own reconstruction encourage a logistical reading involving transport and military assessment. In each case, the interpretation of one uncertain cluster helps determine what neighbouring uncertain clusters are expected to say.

Rudiak has directly challenged Greenwood's reconstruction on visual grounds. In a 2015 discussion he argued that Greenwood was “force-fitting” the passage, maintaining that apparent word spaces contradicted combinations such as “HAUGHT” and “RELATIONS”. Greenwood, conversely, regarded the letter count, punctuation and grammatical coherence of his version as evidence in its favour and challenged alternative readers to produce a comparably coherent reading of the line.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">digital image of ramey memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">digital image of ramey memo</span></span></span>

The dispute therefore cannot be reduced to “Greenwood uses context while Rudiak reads the photograph”. Both approaches use visual marks, estimated character positions and contextual expectations. Their disagreement is over which segmentation and contextual reconstruction best explains those marks.

## One image, two incompatible stories

Putting the reconstructions directly against each other exposes the scale of the problem:

GreenwoodRudiakWARREN HAUGHTB29-ST OR C47 / WRIGHT AFPUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICERASSESS/ASSIST AIRFOIL/FLIGHTSAT ROSWELLAT ROSWELLSAIDASSURE/ABOUT

The exact Rudiak alternatives have changed in places, so the table should not be mistaken for a character-by-character alignment. Its value is simpler: it shows that agreement on an occasional anchor such as “AT ROSWELL” does not imply agreement about the sentence containing it. Published comparisons of Ramey-memo interpretations likewise show substantial variation around this line, including earlier attempts quite unlike either Greenwood's or Rudiak's later reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction between **local agreement** and **sentence-level certainty** is easy to miss. Several investigators have independently reported apparently recognisable fragments elsewhere in the document. Houran and Randle's 2002 experiment also found a few words perceived in the same locations across differently primed groups. But their participants produced many additional readings that followed the contextual suggestions they had been given. The researchers concluded that suggestion and [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) affected attempts to decipher the ambiguous image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

Their experiment involved 176 participants divided among three conditions. Fifty-nine were told that the document concerned Roswell, 58 were told that it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing, and 59 received no proposed context. Participants frequently reported readable words, but many of those solutions reflected the supplied story. The number of perceived words was also associated with variables including previous exposure to the UFO field and Roswell case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That does not prove Greenwood wrong, nor does it prove Rudiak wrong. It identifies a mechanism capable of producing sincere but incompatible readings. Once an interpreter believes that a fuzzy group resembles “GHT”, for example, knowledge of the press name “Haught” can make neighbouring marks easier to organise as a journalistic sentence. If an interpreter instead expects military logistics, apparent abbreviations, aircraft types and Wright Field terminology can organise the same low-information region differently.

The underlying image quality makes that danger unusually important. The US Air Force's 1994 Roswell investigation reported sending the photographs for digitisation and interpretation by what it called a “national-level organization”; according to the report, the photographs remained insufficient to visualise the sought details. Later private researchers obtained and processed better scans and continued proposing readings, but disagreement persisted rather than collapsing into a stable transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/national-level-organization-failed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo National-Level Organization FAILED! – Unlocking The Mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey Memo National-Level Organization FAILED! – Unlocking The Mystery</span></span></span>

## What the incompatible readings reveal about certainty

The Greenwood–Rudiak comparison is therefore more informative as a warning about reconstruction than as a contest in which one polished sentence must defeat another. Greenwood possesses an intriguing external corroboration: “Warren Haught” and descriptions of him as Roswell's information or public-relations officer genuinely occur in 1947 reporting. Rudiak, meanwhile, has developed a much larger military reconstruction in which his version of the disputed passage connects with proposed references elsewhere in the memo to wreckage, transport, Wright Field and weather balloons. Each interpretation gains apparent coherence from material outside the tiny group of pixels being decoded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paradigmresearchgroup.org/News_Items-3.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paradigmresearchgroup.org">[Paradigm Research Group]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paradigmresearchgroup.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paradigmresearchgroup.org.</span></span></span>

That is precisely why contextual fit cannot substitute for character-level evidence. A plausible sentence can help test a transcription, but if the image itself does not reliably distinguish the required letters and spaces, plausibility risks becoming circular: uncertain marks suggest a context, and that context is then used to resolve the uncertain marks.

Later assessments have continued to emphasise this problem. Kevin Randle's 2022 review described the memo as an ambiguous stimulus and noted that even researchers who spent extensive time studying it failed to converge on a common transcription. It repeated the earlier recommendation that meaningful progress would require standardised enhancement and independent, blind analysis by multiple laboratories, with agreement assessed at specific word positions rather than by how persuasive an overall narrative sounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span></span></span>

The safest conclusion from the Greenwood–Rudiak passage is consequently narrower than either complete reconstruction. Some portions of the Ramey document may contain recoverable information, and “AT ROSWELL” is one of the stronger points of agreement in this particular region. But the transformation of neighbouring marks into either “WARREN HAUGHT, PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER” or “B29-ST OR C47. WRIGHT AF…” remains interpretation-dependent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/the-missing-u/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo The missing U</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoThe missing U - UnlockingTheMysteryWARREN HAUGHT, PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER AT ROSWELL base released a statement in the name of...</span></span></span>

That makes this single passage unusually valuable in the wider Ramey memo debate. Greenwood and Rudiak begin with the same photograph and produce narratives belonging to different document types: a news account on one side and a military communication on the other. The contrast demonstrates why a reconstruction should not be judged chiefly by whether its completed sentence makes sense. With an image this degraded, the more important question is whether independent readers, deprived of the proposed story, can repeatedly recover the same characters, spaces and words from the same positions. On that stricter test, the Greenwood–Rudiak disagreement remains evidence of uncertainty rather than a settled transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>
