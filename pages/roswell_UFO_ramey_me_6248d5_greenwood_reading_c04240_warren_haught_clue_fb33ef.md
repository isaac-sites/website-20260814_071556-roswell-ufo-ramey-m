## Introduction

Barry [Greenwood]({{ 'greenwood/' | relative_url }})’s “Warren Haught” clue is one of the more concrete pieces of external evidence in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) dispute because it did not originate in the blurred photograph itself. In 2009, Greenwood noticed that a disputed cluster ending in “GHT” resembled a conspicuous error in July 1947 Roswell news coverage: Walter Haut, the Roswell Army Air Field public information officer, had been reported by the Associated Press and other newspapers as “Warren Haught”. The match matters because “Warren Haught” is not merely a plausible-looking name invented to fit indistinct letters. It is a documented period error that circulated in contemporary reporting about the very incident being discussed at Fort Worth. Yet that same fact creates an important limitation: once Greenwood had seen the newspaper wording, it could also have influenced what he perceived in an ambiguous image. The clue is therefore stronger as evidence for a possible journalistic source text than as proof that the memo actually contains those words.

## Where “Warren Haught” appeared in 1947

The real officer behind the name was Walter Haut, public information officer at Roswell Army Air Field. On 8 July 1947, Haut distributed the statement announcing that personnel of the 509th Bomb Group had obtained a “flying disc”. As the [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) entered the wire-service system, however, his identity was repeatedly rendered incorrectly as “Warren Haught”. The mistake appeared in multiple newspapers rather than in a single obscure retrospective source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/losangelesheraldexpress8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, Los Angeles Herald-Express, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

A surviving Associated Press chronology is particularly useful because it shows the erroneous name inside the rapidly developing wire story itself. An AP lead transmitted that evening began by reporting that the Army Air Forces had gained possession of a flying disc and attributed the announcement to “Lt. Warren Haught”, identified as Roswell Army Air Field’s public information officer. That makes the error part of the contemporary news stream, not a spelling introduced decades later by Roswell researchers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/roswell/illini.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - AP Wires Burn With &#x27;Captured Disk&#x27; StoryJuly 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

Newspaper examples reinforce the point. The *Seattle Daily Times* carried “Lieut. Warren Haught” and identified him as Roswell’s public-information officer; the *Sacramento Bee* likewise printed “Lieutenant Warren Haught”. A Reno report used the same erroneous identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span> The error persisted into subsequent coverage: a 9 July account again referred to “Warren Haught”, while an archival discussion by the US National Archives reproduces another period reference to “Lt. Warren Haught” in connection with the Roswell announcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/newyorktimes9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The New York Times, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

This repetition is the crucial feature of Greenwood’s clue. “Haught” was not simply a surname that happened to end with the visible-looking letters “GHT”. The combination of the wrong first name, wrong surname and Roswell press-officer description formed a recognisable piece of 1947 journalistic language.

## How Greenwood connected Haught to the blurred memo

Greenwood did not initially build his interpretation around the name. His 2004 study concentrated principally on the physical and typographical character of the document. He argued that features of the sheet were compatible with a teletype and questioned the common assumption that General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) must have been holding an internal [military communication]({{ 'military-communication/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr11.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Greenwood UFO Archive UHR</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greenwood UFO Archive UHR</span></span></span>

The “Haught” connection emerged during a later examination. Greenwood described returning to contemporary press clippings while studying the photograph and encountering an 8 July *San Mateo Times* report containing the wording “Lt. Warren Haught, public information officer at Roswell said”. The newspaper archive confirms that the 8 July 1947 *San Mateo Times* page exists with the “Warren Haught” wording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Greenwood UFO Archive FROM OUT OF THE PAST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">After a lull in interest, there has been a recent renewal of discussions about the. “Ramey Memo” on Internet lists. What caught Greenwood’s attention was the surname. Researchers examining the same portion of the Ramey document had often interpreted the apparent ending “GHT” as part of another word, particularly “SOUGHT”. Greenwood instead asked whether those marks might be the end of “HAUGHT”. In his account, the space immediately before it appeared capable of holding six letters. Because the contemporary newspaper story supplied the six-letter name “WARREN”, he regarded “WARREN HAUGHT” as a meaningful alternative reconstruction. A later review of the Roswell evidence summarised this reasoning and stressed that Greenwood did not claim that “WARREN” itself was plainly visible; rather, he inferred it from the available space and the independently known press wording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident/fulltext/63a499df097c7832ca5912b6/A-Grounded-Theory-Update-on-the-Roswell-UFO-Incident.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident Greenwood, B</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2009). Ramey memo redux—line 5. Sep- tember 2009. UFO Historical Revue, 13. Haines, G. K. (1999). CIA&#x27;s role in the study of UFOs, 1947–...</span></span></span>

That distinction is essential. Greenwood’s reasoning was not:

**blurred photograph → clearly readable “WARREN HAUGHT”.**

It was closer to:

**apparent “GHT” → possible “HAUGHT” → contemporary Roswell reports contain “WARREN HAUGHT” → preceding space appears compatible with “WARREN”.**

The external source therefore supplied constraints that the photograph alone could not provide.

Greenwood extended the proposed reading beyond the name. In *UFO Historical Revue* No. 13, he argued that the line was best reconstructed as approximately “WARREN HAUGHT, PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER AT ROSWELL, SAID.” He considered the word lengths, punctuation and surrounding context consistent with contemporary journalistic prose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Greenwood UFO Archive FROM OUT OF THE PAST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">After a lull in interest, there has been a recent renewal of discussions about the. “Ramey Memo” on Internet lists. There is another notable historical wrinkle here. Contemporary reports did not always use exactly the same title for Haut. Some called him the “public information officer”, while other newspaper coverage described “Warren Haught” as the “public relations officer”. A 9 July report, for example, referred to “Lieutenant Warren Haught” as the “public relations officer at the Roswell base”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/middletowntimesherald9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on patrickgross.org.</span></span></span> That variation gave Greenwood a period precedent not only for the misspelled name but also for the wording “public relations officer” in his proposed reconstruction.

## Why the clue supports a newswire hypothesis

The unusually specific error gives Greenwood’s interpretation more explanatory power than a free-form attempt to turn fuzzy marks into meaningful English. If the sheet in Ramey’s hand contained “Warren Haught”, its source would almost certainly have been downstream of public reporting, because the real officer was Walter Haut. A document repeating the press’s mistaken version of his name would look much more naturally like copied, quoted or retransmitted news material than like an original military message written by colleagues who knew who Haut was.

That is why the clue became central to Greenwood’s broader alternative to readings that treat the Ramey document as an internal account of recovered material. His 2009 article explicitly contrasted the possibility of a highly secret Roswell message with that of a teletype originating in a newspaper environment. He argued that several proposed fragments — particularly the reference to the “‘DISC’” and the Haught passage — could be accounted for by language circulating in journalistic reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Greenwood UFO Archive FROM OUT OF THE PAST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">After a lull in interest, there has been a recent renewal of discussions about the. “Ramey Memo” on Internet lists. The timing is also meaningful, although not decisive. The Roswell story was moving rapidly through wire services on 8 July, and the AP chronology shows repeated updates, leads and corrections during the afternoon and evening. “Warren Haught” was already embedded in that stream.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/roswell/illini.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - AP Wires Burn With &#x27;Captured Disk&#x27; StoryJuly 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span> The famous photographs of Ramey were likewise taken at Fort Worth amid intense press interest in the Roswell material. A sheet reproducing current wire copy would therefore not be anomalous in that setting.

The hypothesis does not require believing that a journalist somehow independently invented precisely the wording Greenwood later perceived. The documented newspaper record establishes that the distinctive error already existed in circulation. That is the clue’s strongest feature.

## What the external match can and cannot establish

The “Warren Haught” evidence considerably narrows the interpretive question, but it does not eliminate the fundamental problem with the Ramey memo: the original photograph does not contain enough recoverable visual information to make every disputed character objectively readable.

Later scholarly discussion of Roswell has treated Greenwood’s interpretation as a serious alternative rather than a settled transcription. A 2022 review recounts his identification of “GHT”, his discovery of the San Mateo clipping and his inference of “WARREN”, but also notes that other memo researchers rejected the reconstruction. Brad Sparks, for example, challenged parts of Greenwood’s argument about distinguishing newspaper and military message styles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident/fulltext/63a499df097c7832ca5912b6/A-Grounded-Theory-Update-on-the-Roswell-UFO-Incident.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident Greenwood, B</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2009). More importantly, experimental work by James Houran and Kevin Randle demonstrates why an external match must be handled cautiously. They gave different groups the same Ramey image while varying what participants were told about it: one group received Roswell-related suggestions, another was told that it concerned atomic-bomb testing, and a third received no contextual suggestion. Participants often produced readings that reflected the information they had been given. Only a relatively small set of perceived words showed consistency across conditions. The authors concluded that suggestion and [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) could materially affect attempts to decipher the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That result cuts both ways in assessing Greenwood. It weakens claims that competing Roswell-oriented reconstructions can simply be “seen” in the photograph without interpretive influence. But it also means that discovering “Warren Haught” in a newspaper before returning to the memo is itself a potential source of perceptual priming. Greenwood’s historical clue can make “HAUGHT” a more meaningful candidate without making the underlying pixels clearer.

The safest evidential conclusions are therefore narrower:

* **The historical template is real.** “Warren Haught” genuinely appeared in contemporary July 1947 reporting about Roswell and was identified as the base’s press officer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>
* **The template is unusually specific.** The combination of the erroneous name, Roswell and Haut’s press function is substantially more distinctive than matching a common word such as “the” or “said”.
* **Greenwood’s full phrase remains a reconstruction.** The photograph does not independently establish every character in “WARREN HAUGHT, PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER AT ROSWELL, SAID”, and other researchers have proposed radically different readings for the same area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Greenwood UFO Archive FROM OUT OF THE PAST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">After a lull in interest, there has been a recent renewal of discussions about the. “Ramey Memo” on Internet lists. * **The match would favour journalistic transmission if independently verified.** An authentic “Warren Haught” on Ramey’s sheet would strongly suggest dependence on the contemporary press or wire-service version of the Roswell story, because it reproduces the press’s mistaken identity rather than the officer’s actual name.
* **The clue is not evidence by itself for what crashed at Roswell.** At most, it bears on the [provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}) and content of the sheet visible in Ramey’s hand. It cannot establish the nature of the recovered debris.

## The clue’s real significance

Greenwood’s contribution was to change the kind of evidence being brought to the disputed line. Instead of asking only which words could be imagined from enlarged shapes, he searched the documentary environment of 8 July 1947 for language that might have generated them. The result was a striking candidate: a historically attested error, “Warren Haught”, attached in contemporary reports to precisely the Roswell officer and story that a Fort Worth news teletype might have mentioned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greenwoodufoarchive.com/uhr/uhr13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greenwoodufoarchive.com">[Greenwood UFO Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greenwoodufoarchive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Greenwood UFO Archive FROM OUT OF THE PAST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">After a lull in interest, there has been a recent renewal of discussions about the. “Ramey Memo” on Internet lists. That makes the clue genuinely useful, but principally as a hypothesis that can be tested against the image rather than as a key that automatically decodes it. Its strength comes from the independent 1947 newspaper record; its weakness comes from the extreme visual ambiguity of the Ramey document and the demonstrated tendency for expectations to shape attempted readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The most defensible assessment is consequently more interesting than either “Greenwood solved the memo” or “the resemblance means nothing”. He found a rare, documented textual fingerprint that could explain the disputed “GHT” cluster and, if the complete phrase were ever independently established, would strongly support a news-derived document. What remains unproven is the indispensable middle step: that those blurred marks really do say “Warren Haught” in the first place.
