## Introduction

The strongest words in James Houran and Kevin D. Randle’s 2002 [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) experiment were not the dramatic Roswell readings. They were the words that kept appearing when the supposed subject of the document was changed. Across participants told that the blurred paper concerned Roswell, participants told that it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing, and participants given no explanatory [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) at all, three readings survived: **“Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”**. The researchers recorded aggregate totals of 52, 61 and 27 participants respectively for those readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

That cross-condition survival matters because the experiment simultaneously demonstrated how easily ambiguous portions of the photograph could acquire prime-specific meanings. Roswell participants produced “crash” and “UFO”; atomic-test participants produced “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. Against that shifting background, the few readings that persisted under incompatible cover stories deserve a different evidential status. They do not reveal the memo's complete message, but they provide the experiment's best evidence that at least fragments of the photographed text were genuinely recoverable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Words reported across all three conditions

Houran and Randle randomly divided 176 participants among three conditions: 59 received a Roswell/UFO framing, 58 an atomic-testing framing and 59 no proposed context. Participants attempted their own line-by-line readings rather than choosing from a supplied vocabulary. The point was therefore not simply whether people could recognise suggested words, but whether changing the narrative supplied beforehand changed what they spontaneously believed they saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The researchers also reported punctuation being perceived throughout all three groups. “Land” came close to joining the surviving-word set, with 41 Roswell participants and six atomic-test participants reporting it, but it was not reported as a common reading in the blind condition. Houran and Randle therefore treated “land” as a point of agreement between the two primed groups rather than one of the three genuinely cross-condition readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

There is an important detail behind those totals. Agreement meant more than different participants happening to propose the same vocabulary somewhere on the page. Houran and Randle said participants perceived these words in **identical locations** in the photographed document, and those locations also corresponded with places where earlier [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo investigators had reported the same words. That spatial agreement is one reason the authors concluded that some portions of the document were probably legible despite its poor photographic quality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

The distribution is nevertheless uneven. “Story”, for example, was reported by 43 of 59 Roswell-primed readers but only 11 of 58 atomic-test readers and seven of 59 [blind readers]({{ 'blind-readers/' | relative_url }}). Likewise, “weather balloons” fell from 20 Roswell readers to five atomic readers and only two blind readers. Survival across all three conditions therefore should not be confused with equal recognition rates. The important finding is narrower: changing or removing the supplied narrative did not make these readings disappear altogether.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why cross-condition agreement carries more weight

The experiment created an unusually useful contrast because its two cover stories were incompatible. A participant expecting a Roswell document had reasons to interpret indistinct shapes as words such as “crash”. Someone expecting a secret atomic-testing document had an entirely different set of expectations. A blind participant had neither prompt. If all three independently settled on the same word at the same position, the explanation that the word existed solely because one particular narrative suggested it became less persuasive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

The prime-specific results show why that distinction is necessary. The Roswell condition generated exclusive readings including “remains”, “fundamental”, “crash” and “UFO”. The atomic-test condition instead produced “glasses”, “morning”, “flash”, “atomic”, “laboratory” and “meaning”. Those two vocabularies make sense within the stories supplied to their respective participants, even though everybody was looking at the same underlying photograph. The blind group generated comparatively little interpretation and included “flew” and “balloons” among its few readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

This was not a small side effect. Participants in the Roswell condition reported an average of about 4.6 deciphered words and those in the atomic condition about 4.8, while blind participants averaged only about 1.6. Houran and Randle found significant effects associated with suggestion and concluded that interpretation of the memo was strongly influenced by suggestion and cognitive style.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Against that evidence of narrative contamination, a word such as “Fort Worth TX” is qualitatively different from “UFO” or “atomic”. It survived not because it fitted every supplied story particularly well, but despite the fact that the stories differed. “Story” and “weather balloons” meet the same basic test, albeit with substantially lower recognition in the blind group.

The researchers themselves drew this cautious distinction. They regarded the agreement as evidence that **some** of the document was genuinely legible, while warning that the meaning and context of those readable fragments remained ambiguous because suggestion substantially affected interpretation elsewhere. Their experiment therefore supports neither extreme proposition that the entire memo can reliably be read nor that nothing whatsoever can be extracted from it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The surviving words do not reconstruct the memo

It is tempting to join “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” into a miniature sentence and infer what Ramey was discussing. The experiment does not justify that move. The three fragments occur at different positions in the document, and agreement on isolated words does not establish the missing language connecting them. This distinction is especially important because competing full-memo reconstructions have historically agreed on certain islands of text while disagreeing markedly over what surrounds them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Earlier investigators illustrate the problem. Houran and Randle reviewed attempts in which researchers proposed very different surrounding text even when some fragments persisted between readings. One computer specialist consulted during the earlier work reportedly rejected “Magdalena”, for example, while other investigators disagreed over substantial portions of the document. The study was designed precisely because apparent agreement on selected characters had not produced a stable, independently reproducible transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span>

The later debate has retained this split between high-agreement fragments and controversial reconstruction. Kevin Randle, one of the experiment's authors and also a long-standing Roswell researcher, subsequently described “weather balloons” and “Fort Worth, Texas” as nearly universally accepted readings while acknowledging that other apparently obvious words lacked comparable [consensus]({{ 'consensus/' | relative_url }}). Advocates of a Roswell cover-up interpretation, meanwhile, have placed those agreed fragments inside much more ambitious proposed sentences concerning a “story” and a subsequent public-relations account involving weather balloons. Those longer reconstructions go considerably beyond what the three-condition experiment independently established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey MemoOctober 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2015</span></span></span>

That distinction prevents an otherwise easy evidential mistake. **Recognising “weather balloons” is not the same thing as establishing what the memo says about weather balloons.** The phrase might occur in a straightforward description, a proposed explanation, a report of somebody else's statement or some other context hidden in the unreadable material. The experiment provides evidence for the word-shapes, not for one preferred interpretation of the sentences around them.

## How surviving words differ from story-matched guesses

The most useful way to read the experiment is to separate its proposed vocabulary into levels of evidential resilience.

**Prime-specific readings** are the weakest for reconstructing the document. “UFO” and “crash” emerged only from the Roswell condition, while “atomic”, “flash” and “laboratory” belonged to the atomic-test condition. Their neat correspondence with the stories participants had been given is exactly what makes them vulnerable to expectation effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

**Cross-prime but not blind readings** occupy an intermediate position. “Land” appeared in the same location among both groups that had been supplied a story, but not as one of the blind group's common readings. Houran and Randle suggested that the primed participants might have been more motivated or discriminating in their interpretations, but the absence of blind-condition agreement prevents “land” from passing the strongest internal comparison available in the experiment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

**Three-condition readings** are the experiment's most resilient category. “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” survived a Roswell narrative, an atomic-testing narrative and no narrative. They therefore cannot be dismissed in the same way as a word that appeared only when participants had first been encouraged to expect it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

This does not make every survivor equally certain. Only 27 of 176 participants contributed to the aggregate “weather balloons” agreement, for example, compared with 61 for “story”; and the original individual scoring sheets were no longer available when a referee requested fuller word-by-word figures. The research assistant had discarded them after compiling the database and notes, leaving detailed condition counts only for the words identified as common across conditions. That loss limits retrospective checking of the complete response set and is worth bearing in mind when assigning precision to the findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

There is also a useful historical cross-check. Houran and Randle noted that these common readings matched words reported by previous investigators, and “Fort Worth, Tex.” and “weather balloons” continued to be treated as particularly strong readings in later specialist discussion. That does not independently authenticate every letter, because investigators may share source material and expectations, but convergence between earlier analyses and an experiment deliberately manipulating expectations is more informative than agreement among similarly primed Roswell researchers alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## What the surviving words actually establish

The defensible conclusion is modest but useful. The atomic-test prime worked as a control against a simple assumption that any apparently legible Ramey memo word must represent objective text. It showed that readers could manufacture plausible, context-matched vocabulary from ambiguous visual information. Yet the experiment also found a small residue that resisted this manipulation.

That residue was **“Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”**, with “land” surviving the two primed conditions but not the blind one. In Houran and Randle's own interpretation, this pattern suggested that parts of the photographed document really were legible even without computer [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}), while the broader meaning remained uncertain because expectation affected the decipherment process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo debate, this changes the relevant question. The evidence is not well described by either “the memo has been deciphered” or “the photograph contains no readable information”. The experiment instead points towards **small, comparatively stable islands of text surrounded by much more ambiguous material**. Claims built from those islands deserve more consideration than prime-specific readings; claims built from the uncertain material connecting them require substantially more caution.

That is particularly important for “weather balloons”. Its appearance even among participants primed to think about atomic testing, and among two participants given no context whatsoever, makes the phrase harder to explain purely as a Roswell-induced guess. But its survival cannot tell us whether the memo endorsed a weather-balloon explanation, discussed one critically, described its release to the press or used the phrase in some other way. The word-level evidence survives the competing cover stories. The proposed cover-story narrative surrounding those words does not automatically survive with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>
