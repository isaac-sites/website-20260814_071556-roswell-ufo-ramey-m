## Introduction

John Kirby’s reading of the [Ramey Memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) matters because it changes the crucial disputed passage from a statement apparently about casualties into one about recovered material. Kirby, a Roswell researcher working in the computer field, examined the photographed document and reconstructed its second line as “are the remains of the material you commanded we fly”. He also independently recognised the following line as “At Fort Worth, Tex.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_victims_phrase_b3d345_kirby_remains_readin_437ae4-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Remains Reading illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_victims_phrase_b3d345_kirby_remains_readin_437ae4-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_victims_phrase_b3d345_kirby_remains_readin_437ae4-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That is a fundamentally different message from the much-publicised “victims of the wreck” reconstruction. “Victims” invites an inference about injured or dead beings; “remains of the material” describes physical remnants and can naturally refer to debris. Yet Kirby’s version should not simply replace one confident transcription with another. The photograph is so degraded that later experiments found strong effects from [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), while independent image-analysis laboratories could not recover clearly interpretable text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Kirby’s real importance, therefore, is methodological as much as linguistic: his incompatible reading demonstrates how much of the extraordinary meaning attributed to the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) Memo depends on resolving ambiguous marks into particular words.

## Kirby’s reconstruction of the line

The principal published account of Kirby’s work appears in James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 study of attempts to decipher the memo. They describe Kirby as a researcher interested in Roswell who worked for an established company in the computer field. Despite having relevant expertise and equipment, he reported being unable to see much of the document clearly. That qualification is important: Kirby was not claiming that the whole message had suddenly become legible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

He did, however, identify one comparatively stable passage. On what researchers label line three, Kirby read “At Fort Worth, Tex.” Other investigators independently produced substantially the same wording: the comparative table in Houran and Randle’s paper records versions of “AT FORT WORTH, TEX.” from David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}), Don Burleson, Tom Carey and Kirby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Kirby diverged sharply on the preceding and far more consequential line. Where several researchers perceived wording involving “victims” and a “wreck”, his reconstruction was:

> “are the remains of the material you commanded we fly”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The source for this interpretation was a 7 February 1999 letter from Kirby to Kevin Randle, rather than a separately published technical paper by Kirby. Houran and Randle subsequently incorporated it into their comparative analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That provenance places an important limit on claims about Kirby: the available published literature records his conclusion and general background, but does not provide a detailed, independently reproducible technical protocol showing exactly how each disputed character was derived.

Even so, the contrast is unusually valuable because it occurs precisely where the UFO interpretation places some of its greatest evidential weight. The disagreement is not over an inconsequential connective word. It changes the apparent subject of the sentence.

## Why “remains” changes the meaning

Read as “victims of the wreck”, the line seems to refer to beings affected by a crash. That is why the phrase has acquired such prominence in Roswell arguments: if securely established in a contemporary document connected to General Roger Ramey, it would be difficult to reconcile with an ordinary unmanned balloon recovery.

Kirby’s version removes that inference. “Remains of the material” makes the grammatical object physical material rather than victims. In ordinary English, remains can describe what is left of an object after destruction or damage, while “material” reinforces that non-human meaning. The remainder of Kirby’s reconstruction — “you commanded we fly” — would then suggest movement of that material by air. In broad outline, that is compatible with debris being transported to Fort Worth, without itself identifying what the debris was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That last distinction is essential. Kirby’s reading is **debris-compatible**, but it is not a transcription of “weather balloon”, “RAWIN target” or “Project Mogul”. Even if his complete line were correct, “material” could describe mundane wreckage, unusual wreckage or something else entirely. His reconstruction therefore weakens the specific argument that this passage independently establishes casualties; it does not establish the nature of the recovered material.

There is another reason to resist treating the phrase as decisive. The parts of the memo on which readers agree most readily tend to be less sensational. “At Fort Worth, Tex.” is a good example. Houran and Randle’s comparison shows multiple interpreters converging on that wording while disagreeing extensively elsewhere. A later review by Randle noted Barry Greenwood’s observation that essentially the same Fort Worth wording appeared in contemporary news reports, raising further questions about whether the sheet might even have been associated with news-wire material rather than a secret military message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

In other words, recognisable contextual fragments do not automatically authenticate the much less certain words surrounding them.

## How Kirby’s reading compares with “victims”

The comparison is striking because the competing reconstructions generate almost opposite narratives from the same photographic information.

**The “victims” reconstruction** treats the ambiguous character patterns as language about casualties and wreckage. Early interpretation teams reported versions containing “VICTIMS”, and subsequent researchers including Morris, Rudiak, Burleson and Carey produced readings that retained broadly similar wording even while disagreeing substantially about the rest of the memo. Houran and Randle explicitly noted that many investigators were not actually seeing the same message despite apparent areas of agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

**Kirby’s reconstruction** substitutes an entirely different grammatical structure: “remains of the material”. Instead of people associated with a wreck, the sentence concerns material being moved. Houran and Randle themselves highlighted the consequence, writing that changing “victims” to “remains” altered the nature of the entire message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The important evidential point is therefore not that Kirby proved “victims” wrong. It is that a technically experienced examiner looking at the same underlying photograph could derive a coherent alternative that lacked its most extraordinary implication.

That poses a serious reliability problem for arguments based on apparent [consensus]({{ 'consensus/' | relative_url }}). Houran and Randle’s comparison table labelled “VICTIMS OF THE WRECK” as an area of agreement among a majority of contributing interpreters, but the same table preserves Kirby’s contradictory line. The table also notes that participants in the decipherment effort shared information with one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Agreement among interacting researchers is consequently weaker evidence than agreement among genuinely independent, blinded readers would have been.

## The photograph cannot choose confidently between them

The central limitation is information loss. The original sheet is unavailable for direct inspection; researchers are attempting to reconstruct tiny typewritten characters recorded incidentally within a 1947 photograph. Early official efforts already encountered this problem. According to Houran and Randle, a US government analysis in 1994 concluded that even after digitisation the photographs were of insufficient quality to reveal the details being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The difficulty is not simply insufficient magnification. Once fine character information was never adequately recorded on the film, enlarging or digitally processing the image cannot reliably recreate it. Different enhancement methods can make existing structures easier to inspect, but they cannot establish which letters originally occupied areas where the photographic signal is overwhelmed by blur and grain.

That technical limitation was tested more systematically after the early transcription debate. Houran and Randle conducted an experiment in which people were given different contextual suggestions before attempting to decipher the memo. Participants told that the image concerned Roswell, participants given an atomic-bomb context and participants given no explanatory context produced different patterns of perceived words. The suggestion condition significantly affected how many words participants reported, and Roswell-primed readers tended to perceive some terms matching earlier UFO researchers’ reconstructions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Intriguingly, “remains” itself appeared among words susceptible to these contextual effects. That means the experiment does not vindicate Kirby against the “victims” readers. Instead, it points towards a broader warning: when the visual signal is poor enough, meaningful words can emerge through an interaction between genuine traces in the image and the observer’s expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Later technical work strengthens the caution

Houran and Randle proposed a stronger test: give the best available scans to multiple independent, blind laboratories and look for statistically meaningful agreement about particular words in particular positions. Their reasoning was straightforward. If “victims”, “remains” or another expression really survived in the photographic data at adequate resolution, independent specialists should be able to recover it without first being told what they were expected to find.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span></span></span>

A later project reported by Houran and summarised by Randle did broadly follow that approach. Three qualified research groups examined high-quality memo imagery using different restoration techniques. Nikolas Galatsanos at the University of Ioannina applied blind deconvolution; André Marion's laboratory in France used Fourier-based signal processing; and Hong Yan's group at the City University of Hong Kong applied filtering, wavelet and deblurring methods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/667605767/UFO" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; PDF &#124; Unidentified Flying Object &#124; Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; PDF &#124; Unidentified Flying Object &#124; ScienceAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

The result was not a decisive transcription. The laboratories found that processing could improve aspects of the image, but severe blur and film-grain noise remained. Galatsanos and Yan reported no clearly interpretable text, while Marion's analysis found the noise frequencies sufficiently close to those of the text to make recovery problematic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span></span></span>

A further high-resolution effort in 2015 likewise failed to produce an immediate universal reading. New scans were made from the negative at the University of Texas at Arlington with forensic-photography expertise involved. Randle reported that some resulting digital files approached 250 MB, but also stated that the work had not yielded universal conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey MemoOctober 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2015</span></span></span>

Those results are particularly relevant to Kirby. They do not confirm his phrase, but they make it harder to argue that the rival “victims” wording is simply visible fact that competent analysis must recover. The underlying image appears to be close to, or beyond, the point at which some disputed characters can be uniquely reconstructed.

## What Kirby’s reading actually establishes

Kirby’s reconstruction has a narrower but stronger significance than either side of the Roswell debate sometimes gives such readings.

First, it provides a concrete counterexample to the claim that the critical line straightforwardly says “victims of the wreck”. A reader with computer-field experience, working on the same problem and able to recognise the comparatively stable Fort Worth wording, arrived at a materially different sentence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Second, Kirby demonstrates how a small transcription choice can drive a much larger historical conclusion. Once the ambiguous marks are labelled “victims”, the document appears to contain evidence for casualties. Once they become “remains of the material”, that implication disappears and the line sounds like logistics concerning transported debris. The extraordinary conclusion therefore depends on the reliability of the transcription rather than following independently from the photograph.

Third, Kirby does **not** supply affirmative proof for the conventional explanation of Roswell. His wording identifies “material”, not what that material was. Using his reconstruction to prove a balloon would commit essentially the same mistake as using “victims” alone to prove bodies: it would extract more certainty from the image than the transcription can bear.

Finally, the later experimental and laboratory evidence suggests that the safest status for both readings is **hypothesis rather than recovered text**. Houran and Randle concluded that parts of the memo may indeed be legible, but that their meaning remained ambiguous and vulnerable to suggestion effects; subsequent laboratory work found no clearly interpretable text after sophisticated restoration attempts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The key implication for the Ramey Memo debate

John Kirby’s “remains of the material” reading does not solve the Ramey Memo. Its importance is that it prevents the disputed “victims” passage from doing evidential work it has not earned.

If Kirby is right, the line is readily compatible with the transportation of recovered debris and contains no reference to casualties at all. If the “victims” readers are right, the implications are much more dramatic. But the surviving photograph has not permitted independent technical analysis to discriminate confidently between those possibilities.

That asymmetry matters. A claim that the memo **proves victims were recovered** requires “victims” to be securely established in the image. Kirby’s coherent alternative, the substantial disagreement among other transcribers, experimentally demonstrated context effects and the failure of independent laboratories to extract clearly interpretable text all weaken that premise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The most defensible reading of Kirby’s contribution is therefore neither “Kirby decoded the memo” nor “Kirby disproved the [victims phrase]({{ 'victims-phrase/' | relative_url }})”. He showed that the crucial marks support at least one substantially different reconstruction whose meaning points towards material remains rather than casualties. Until the underlying characters can be recovered independently and reproducibly, “victims of the wreck” and “remains of the material” remain competing interpretations — but only the former carries the extraordinary claim about bodies, and consequently it bears the heavier evidential burden.
