## Introduction

“DISC” probably belongs on a list of **plausible and repeatedly reported [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) readings**, but not in the memo’s highest-[confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) tier. The important distinction is methodological. Several experienced Roswell researchers have read “DISC” or “DISK” in roughly the same place, and even Barry Greenwood, who challenged exotic interpretations of the document, thought that reading looked plausible. Yet the strongest controlled test of the memo did **not** reproduce “DISC” among the words independently recognised across differently primed reader groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_weather_balloon_word_20718b_disc_reading_reliabi_fc8f25-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="The DISC Word illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_weather_balloon_word_20718b_disc_reading_reliabi_fc8f25-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_weather_balloon_word_20718b_disc_reading_reliabi_fc8f25-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That puts “DISC” in an awkward middle category. It has more support than many spectacular proposed phrases, but less independent confirmation than “FORT WORTH” and “WEATHER BALLOONS”. Treating all three as equally secure therefore overstates the evidence. The fairest formulation is that “DISC” is a credible candidate reading with substantial researcher agreement, not a firmly established textual anchor.

## Who has reported seeing “DISC”?

The case for “DISC” begins with the history of attempts to read the photographed sheet. Kevin Randle's later account credits Brad Sparks with identifying “BALLOONS” from an enlargement in 1980 and, by 1985, adding “weather balloons”, “Fort Worth, Tex.” and “disc”. Randle's 2022 review describes these later readings as unanimously or almost unanimously accepted among researchers working on the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

Later [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) reinforce the impression of agreement, although not always on spelling or surrounding words. The comparison assembled by James Houran and Kevin Randle in their 2002 *Journal of Scientific Exploration* paper shows several interpretations of line four. David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}) read a phrase containing “DISC”; Don Burleson and Tom Carey rendered the corresponding word as “DISK”; and the table's summary of researcher agreement simply gives “DISK or DISC”. The surrounding sentences diverge substantially even when that four-letter reading survives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is significant because “DISC” is not merely a feature of one famous transcription. Rudiak's much-publicised reconstruction places it in a phrase beginning with “THE ‘DISC’”, while Burleson and Carey independently presented different sentences built around “DISK”. Their disagreement about the rest of the line is actually useful here: it shows that acceptance of the four-letter word did not require acceptance of Rudiak's complete reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Barry Greenwood supplies an especially important data point because his broader interpretation of the memo runs against the extraterrestrial “smoking gun” reading. In a 2004 analysis, Greenwood wrote that Rudiak, Burleson, Neil Morris and Carey/Schmitt were generally in agreement that the relevant line contained “disc” or “disk” in quotation marks, adding that it appeared that way to him as well. At the same time, he stressed that considerable doubt remained over deciphering the rest of the document and argued that its punctuation and structure could indicate a journalistic teletype rather than a secret military communication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

This matters because Greenwood's acceptance demonstrates that seeing “DISC” does not automatically entail accepting a crashed-spacecraft interpretation. A reader can provisionally accept the word while disputing both its surrounding sentence and the nature of the document.

## Why agreement is not independent confirmation

The apparent consensus becomes less impressive once the history of the readings is examined. Houran and Randle explicitly noted that earlier investigators shared information while trying to decipher the memo. Their comparison table says that the contributing researchers exchanged material, and it records substantial differences among their complete transcriptions. In other words, these were not equivalent to several isolated laboratories independently receiving an unlabelled image and all returning “DISC”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction is crucial with an image as degraded as the Ramey photograph. Earlier examination by former NASA researcher Richard Haines found vague indications of words but little ability to resolve individual letters. A US government-sponsored digitisation and photo-interpretation effort likewise reported in 1994 that the photographs were of insufficient quality to visualise the details being sought. Later investigators extracted considerably more text, but Houran and Randle observed that many of them did not see the same things.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Their experiment directly demonstrated why that matters. They randomly assigned 176 participants to three conditions: one was told that the image might concern a Roswell extraterrestrial crash and cover-up, another that it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing, and a third received no proposed context. Participants were given enlargements and high-resolution scans and asked to transcribe the memo line by line.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The framing changed what people reported. Readers given Roswell information produced Roswell-compatible words; those given the atomic-bomb story produced terms such as “flash”, “glasses” and “atomic”. The authors found that suggestion significantly affected the number and character of perceived words, illustrating precisely the danger in treating agreement among context-aware Roswell researchers as straightforward independent corroboration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Most importantly for “DISC”, **it did not emerge in the experiment's cross-group consensus set**. The words reported in matching positions across all three conditions were “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”. “Land” showed relative agreement across the two suggested-context groups but not the blind group. “DISC” is absent from that experimentally derived list.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That absence does not prove that “DISC” is wrong. The experiment was not powerful enough to establish every genuine word in the photograph, and the authors acknowledged limitations in their retained data: the original score sheets had been discarded after responses were aggregated, leaving detailed counts principally for the words common across conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> But it does mean that the strongest available test for context-resistant recognition provides substantially better support for some other memo words than for “DISC”.

## How “DISC” compares with stronger text anchors

The difference can be stated quite precisely. “FORT WORTH” and “WEATHER BALLOONS” possess **two kinds of support**: agreement among specialist interpreters and recognition by participants exposed to different contextual suggestions. “DISC” clearly has the first kind, but the published experiment does not establish the second.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

“FORT WORTH” is particularly strong. In Houran and Randle's experimental data, 24 people in the Roswell condition, 17 in the atomic-bomb condition and 11 in the blind condition reported “Fort Worth TX” in the corresponding location — 52 readers altogether. “Weather balloons” was identified by 20 Roswell-primed readers, five atomic-bomb-primed readers and two blind readers. Those numbers are not proof that every letter has been recovered correctly, but they show recognition crossing the very contextual boundaries the experiment was designed to test.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There is no equivalent published cross-group count for “DISC”. The 2002 paper instead places “DISK or DISC” in its table summarising agreement among previous investigators. This creates two evidential tiers that are sometimes blurred together in discussions of the memo:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Stronger anchors:** “FORT WORTH” and “WEATHER BALLOONS”, because specialist agreement is supplemented by controlled cross-group recognition.
* **Prominent but weaker reading:** “DISC” or “DISK”, because several memo investigators converge on it, including researchers who disagree over the document's wider meaning, but controlled cross-group confirmation is lacking.
* **Still more uncertain material:** longer phrases reconstructed around those words, where competing transcriptions rapidly diverge and contextual interpretation becomes much more important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

This ranking also prevents an important logical mistake. Even if “DISC” were eventually established beyond reasonable doubt, the word itself would not demonstrate that Ramey was describing an extraterrestrial vehicle. “Disc” was already part of the contemporary Roswell vocabulary: the Army's original Roswell announcement concerned recovery of a “flying disc”, and Greenwood noted that contemporary news coverage also used “disc” in reporting the story. His proposed explanation was that the photographed sheet could itself have been newswire material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The quotation marks some interpreters see around the word may even complicate rather than settle its meaning. Greenwood and other critics have pointed out that punctuation of this sort may fit journalistic copy better than the military-[message format]({{ 'message-format/' | relative_url }}) assumed by some reconstructions. Whether that hypothesis is correct is a separate question, but it demonstrates why recovering a noun and determining what the document is asserting about that noun are two different evidential problems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

## The safest verdict on “DISC”

“DISC” should therefore **not be discarded**, but neither should it be presented beside “WEATHER BALLOONS” and “FORT WORTH” without qualification. Its repeated appearance across several specialist transcriptions is genuine evidence in its favour. The fact that Greenwood could accept the visual reading while rejecting the more sensational interpretation strengthens the case that the word is not solely an artefact of one pro-Roswell reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

But the methodological evidence stops short of making it a top-tier anchor. Houran and Randle found weak inter-rater reliability among earlier analysts and experimentally showed that prior suggestion changes what readers extract from the memo. Their most valuable result was that a small number of words survived those changes in context. “DISC” was not one of the words they reported surviving across all three groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The most defensible description is consequently **“probable or plausible, but not independently secured to the same standard as the strongest readings”**. Randle and Sparks have argued for a stronger judgement, describing “disc” as nearly unanimous among experienced readers, and that historical consensus deserves to be recorded rather than dismissed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/05/the-ramey-memo-negative.html?showComment=1462541425906" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span></span></span> Yet consensus among interacting specialists and convergence among differently primed or blind readers answer different questions.

For interpreting the readable weather-balloon wording of the Ramey memo, that distinction is the useful takeaway. “DISC” strengthens the impression that the photographed text concerns the Roswell flying-disc story, but it should carry less evidential weight than “FORT WORTH” and “WEATHER BALLOONS”. And even if the four letters are correct, the word cannot by itself tell us whether the memo was describing an object as a disc, quoting somebody else's description, reproducing a news report, or discussing the public “flying disc” story. The surviving photograph simply does not preserve enough secure surrounding syntax to choose among those possibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
