## Introduction

The disputed four-letter group in the photographed [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) cannot safely be reduced to a choice between **DISC** and **DISK**. The strongest published comparison of rival [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) shows something more fundamental: David Rudiak read **“DISC”**, Don Burleson and Tom Carey read **“DISK”**, Neil Morris at one stage read something resembling **“ELSE”**, and photographic analyst Russ Estes thought the marks looked more like **“ELA*”**. In other words, analysts were not merely arguing about whether the final character was a C or a K. They disagreed about several of the basic letter shapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. If [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) consistently saw D-I-S followed by an uncertain final character, “DISC/DISK” would have relatively strong visual support. The actual record is less tidy. Some readers did converge on DISC or DISK, but other readings diverged much earlier in the sequence, and a controlled experiment later failed to recover “disc” among the memo's cross-group [consensus]({{ 'consensus/' | relative_url }}) words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What the rival analysts actually read

James Houran and Kevin Randle's 2002 study is particularly useful because it preserves several earlier transcriptions side by side rather than presenting one reconstruction as though it were settled. Their comparison identifies Neil Morris, David Rudiak, Don Burleson, Tom Carey and John Kirby, with revised versions for Morris and Rudiak. The researchers also warned that these interpreters had shared information during their work, so their apparent agreement cannot automatically be treated as fully independent confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That last entry is easy to overinterpret. The table's “Agreement” category meant that a majority saw the same *basic structure*. It did not mean every analyst independently identified the same four letters. Indeed, Morris's ELSE reading demonstrates precisely the opposite. The table also explains that capital letters represented characters an interpreter considered accurate, whereas lower-case letters represented a best guess — another reminder that these were confidence-weighted visual interpretations, not straightforward transcriptions of crisp type.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The disagreement becomes still clearer when Russ Estes's separate examination is added. Estes used photographic and computer techniques, including a scan reported at 9,000 dots per inch, yet said he could not reliably decipher the memo. When pressed for his best guess at the disputed area, he reported **“ELA*”** rather than DISC or DISK and explicitly acknowledged that the result made no semantic sense to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

That is valuable evidence precisely because it is inconvenient to every neat reading. Estes was describing the shapes he thought he saw rather than selecting a meaningful Roswell-related word to fit them.

## The disagreement starts before C versus K

The rival readings can be thought of as competing character sequences rather than competing words. That makes the scale of the problem easier to see.

**DISC versus DISK** is a relatively narrow disagreement:

**D – I – S – C**

**D – I – S – K**

Those alternatives agree on three positions and dispute the fourth. If this were the whole controversy, one could reasonably describe the image as strongly supporting **DIS-**, with only the last letter unresolved.

But **ELSE** changes the picture:

**D – I – S – C/K**

**E – L – S – E**

Now only the third character, **S**, clearly survives the comparison. The first could be D or E; the second I or L; and the fourth C, K or E. Estes's **ELA*** pushes the disagreement further still because even the third position ceases to be secure under his interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There is another revealing detail. Morris later reconsidered his own transcription and produced a substantially altered version of the same line, no longer retaining his earlier ELSE reading. Rudiak's revised transcription, by contrast, continued to identify DISC.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That contrast illustrates two different kinds of uncertainty: disagreement **between observers**, and instability **within an observer's own reading over time**.

This does not prove that DISC is wrong. It does mean that the evidential question cannot be answered simply by counting published transcriptions containing the word. The relevant test is whether the underlying character forms independently and reproducibly constrain observers towards D-I-S-C. The historical transcription record does not demonstrate that level of constraint.

## Where the readings genuinely converge

The memo is not uniformly unreadable. That point is important because it provides an internal benchmark against which the supposed DISC can be judged.

Houran and Randle recruited 176 participants and randomly assigned them to three conditions. Fifty-nine were told that the document might concern Roswell and an extraterrestrial crash or cover-up; 58 were told that it might concern secret atomic-bomb testing; and 59 were given no suggested subject. Participants examined an enlarged print plus four high-resolution versions of the memo and recorded whatever characters or words they believed they could see.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Suggestion made a substantial difference. Participants primed with either the Roswell or atomic-bomb scenario reported significantly more words than the blind group. The content also followed the prompt: the Roswell group produced readings such as “UFO” and “crash”, while the atomic-bomb group produced “atomic”, “flash”, “glasses” and “laboratory”. The authors therefore found direct evidence that expectations could influence attempts to extract words from the blurred photograph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Yet some strings survived those changing expectations. Across the three groups, participants repeatedly identified **“Fort Worth TX”** in the same location, with 52 identifications in total; **“[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})”** received 61; and **“weather balloons”** 27. “Land” also showed substantial agreement between the two primed groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That gives the DISC controversy a useful control. When a word was sufficiently supported by the photograph, people given radically different contextual suggestions could still converge on it.

**DISC was not one of those cross-condition consensus words.** Nor was DISK. The study's table of shared and condition-specific readings records the genuinely recurring items but does not establish the disputed four-letter group as one of them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The study had an important limitation: the original individual score sheets were inadvertently discarded after the responses had been compiled, so the authors could not later provide a participant-by-participant frequency for every proposed word. They retained detailed information for the words common across conditions. Consequently, the experiment cannot tell us exactly how many of its 176 participants independently wrote DISC at the disputed location. What it can show is that DISC did **not** emerge into the preserved category of words demonstrating cross-condition agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why image enhancement did not settle it

A natural response to disagreement over a blurred photograph is to obtain a better scan. The history of the Ramey memo shows why that solution has proved less decisive than it sounds.

Richard Haines examined the message under a microscope in 1991 and reportedly saw vague word-like forms but could not reliably identify individual letters. During the US Air Force's later Roswell investigation, first-generation photographic material was sent to what its report called a “national level organization” for digitisation and interpretation. According to the account reproduced by Houran and Randle, the organisation concluded in 1994 that the photographic quality remained insufficient to visualise the details being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Estes's later effort illustrates the same underlying limitation. Very high-resolution scanning can represent the available photographic information with enormous numerical precision; it cannot necessarily recreate fine letter strokes that the original photograph never resolved. His 9,000-dpi work therefore ended not with a definitive word but with the tentative ELA* interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

The same problem persisted when researchers returned to the original negative at the University of Texas at Arlington in 2015. Kevin Randle reported that specialists produced new scans, some approaching 250 MB, but that the improved digital material did not yield the hoped-for universal solution. He wrote that the analysis had reached no universal conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey MemoOctober 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2015</span></span></span> The University of Texas at Arlington's Special Collections has likewise described the memo as having been closely scrutinised but **not definitively deciphered**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span>

Those later efforts do not make every proposed reading equally plausible. They do, however, weaken the idea that DISC has become secure merely because better digital copies exist.

## Why apparent agreement needs careful handling

There is genuine agreement among several experienced Roswell researchers that the disputed group resembles DISC or DISK. Rudiak argued strongly for DISC, while Burleson and Carey independently published DISK readings in the comparison assembled by Houran and Randle. Randle later wrote that “THE ‘DISC’” seemed to stand out in newer imagery, citing Barry Greenwood as another observer who considered that phrase capable of attracting general agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is evidence in favour of taking DISC/DISK seriously. It would be too dismissive to pretend that nobody looking closely at the photograph has perceived those letters.

But “independent agreement” has to be qualified. Houran and Randle's table explicitly notes that the principal interpreters **shared information while attempting to decipher the message**. Burleson and Carey also worked together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Once readers know that another investigator sees DISC at a particular coordinate, subsequent recognition is no longer equivalent to a fully blind identification.

The controlled experiment demonstrates why this matters. Simply telling people that the document concerned Roswell increased the amount of Roswell-compatible material they reported seeing; telling another group that it concerned atomic testing generated atomic-testing vocabulary instead. Participants in the blind condition produced far fewer supposed words overall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This is not proof that every Roswell-related word is an artefact of [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}). “Weather balloons”, for example, appeared across all three experimental conditions, showing that a contextually relevant expression can also possess genuine visual support. Rather, the experiment supplies a more demanding standard: the strongest reading is one that survives changes in expectation and appears at the same position among observers who have not been told what to find.

On the published evidence, DISC does not clear that bar as convincingly as “Fort Worth”, “story” or “weather balloons”.

## What can reasonably be said about DISC

The evidence supports a middle position more precise than either “the memo clearly says DISC” or “DISC is imaginary”.

There is a recognisable patch of photographed text at the relevant location. Several investigators have interpreted that patch as **DISC** or **DISK**, and some continued to do so after repeated examination and improved scanning. That repeated recognition gives the proposal more weight than a reading invented by one observer on one occasion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

But the same marks have also produced **ELSE**, **ELA*** and a later Morris reconstruction that abandoned ELSE without converging on DISC. The disagreement therefore reaches into the fundamental identity of the letters. Moreover, DISC/DISK did not appear among the experimentally demonstrated words that readers recognised across differently primed groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction is decisive for interpreting the Ramey memo responsibly. **DISC is a proposed transcription with multiple supporters, not an established reading of the photograph.** “DISK or DISC” fairly captures the convergence among one cluster of specialist interpreters, but it should not be mistaken for the full range of observations. “ELSE” and “ELA*” show that the photographic information is ambiguous enough for competent readers to disagree before they ever reach the final C-versus-K question.

The most useful comparison is therefore not DISC against DISK. It is **DISC/DISK against the complete rival-reading dataset**. Viewed that way, the photographed marks contain enough structure to suggest a four-letter word to some observers, but not enough recoverable detail to establish its letters with the reliability seen in the memo's better-supported passages. That is why the proposed DISC remains intriguing — and why the image itself does not yet justify treating it as settled text.
