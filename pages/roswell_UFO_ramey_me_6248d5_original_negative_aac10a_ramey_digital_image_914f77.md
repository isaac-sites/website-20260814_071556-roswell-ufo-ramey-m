## Introduction

A crisp digital image of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) is not automatically a good evidential image. Two online copies can have the same pixel dimensions, look equally sharp at normal viewing size and even carry similar filenames while descending from quite different physical sources: one may be a direct scan of the surviving 4-by-5-inch [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}), another a scan of an enlargement, and another a derivative that has been resized, sharpened or compressed several times. For attempts to read the tiny paper held by Brigadier General Roger Ramey in the July 1947 photograph, that distinction is fundamental.

The safest rule is therefore simple: **establish the physical and digital [provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}) before judging apparent letters**. The strongest digital copy is not necessarily the largest file or the one with the most visible-looking text. It is the copy whose route from the archived photographic object to the pixels on screen can be documented, whose processing is disclosed and whose relationship to other copies can be checked. Modern archival-imaging guidance treats the original object, capture specifications, master file, derivatives and metadata as separate parts of that chain for precisely this reason.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/still-image/charter.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Still Image Digitization Working Group Charter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesStill Image Digitization Working Group Charter - Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines InitiativeJuly 17, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 17, 2008</span></span></span>

## Why pixel dimensions do not reveal source quality

Pixel dimensions describe a digital file, not the amount of photographic information that existed before digitisation. A 10,000-pixel-wide JPEG made by enlarging a smaller scan still contains only the information inherited from that smaller scan. Likewise, a huge TIFF created from an enlarged photographic print cannot recover detail that was lost when the negative was printed. File size is similarly ambiguous: bit depth, compression, cropping and metadata can all change the number of megabytes without establishing how much genuine memo detail was captured.

This is particularly important because unusually large [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) files really do exist. Kevin Randle reported that an April 2015 project at the University of Texas at Arlington (UTA) produced scans directly from the negative, with some resulting digital files approaching 250 MB. He also reported that dozens of captures were made under differing conditions with assistance from photographic and forensic specialists. The size of those files is useful contextual information, but it is the claimed direct connection to the archived negative—not the 250 MB figure—that gives them their evidential significance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theufochronicles.com/2015/11/digital-image-of-roswell-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theufochronicles.com">[The UFO Chronicles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theufochronicles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The UFO Chronicles Digital Image of the Roswell Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The UFO ChroniclesDigital Image of the Roswell Ramey MemoNovember 14, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2015</span></span></span>

The same distinction appears in professional archival practice. The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative (FADGI), developed collaboratively by US federal cultural-heritage institutions, treats photographic negatives and prints as defined source categories and evaluates digital masters through technical imaging parameters, image-performance measurements, workflow and metadata. Its current guidance does not reduce quality to a single resolution figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/still-image/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Still Image Working Group</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesStill Image Working Group - Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines InitiativeNovember 15, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 15, 2023</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, this produces a useful hierarchy of questions. Before asking whether an apparent stroke resembles an **R**, **V** or **K**, ask:

* What physical object was digitised?

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was it the surviving camera negative, a photographic enlargement, another print or a microform reproduction?
* Was the whole photographic frame captured, including edges, or only the memo region?
* Who performed the digitisation, where and when?
* Is the available file the capture/master or a later derivative?
* What cropping, rotation, contrast adjustment, sharpening, noise reduction or resampling occurred?
* Has JPEG or another lossy compression intervened?
* Can the claimed source be independently connected to the UTA collection or another identified archive?

</div>

A digital copy for which those questions cannot be answered may still be useful for illustration. It is much weaker as evidence for character-level transcription.

## Negatives, prints and microfilm are not interchangeable

The Ramey research history itself demonstrates why source generation needs to be recorded. A 2002 study by James Houran and Kevin Randle described a deliberately mixed set of source material rather than treating every high-resolution Ramey image as equivalent. Participants were supplied with an enlarged print and digital material associated with Stanton Friedman. The reported digital set included an image from the negative, a UTA-made 8-by-10 enlargement of the memo area, an older 8-by-10 enlargement and an 11-by-14 enlargement made in 1998.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is an unusually useful provenance lesson. All four could potentially yield impressive-looking digital images, but they are not four equivalent observations of the original scene. A scan from the camera negative samples the photographic record produced by [Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})'s camera. A scan of an 8-by-10 or 11-by-14 enlargement samples a **photographic interpretation of that negative**: enlarger optics, focus, exposure, paper response and chemical processing have already intervened.

An older print is not automatically worse in every respect. Houran and Randle specifically noted an older enlargement made before the original negative had accumulated as much handling-related dirt and debris. That creates a genuine archival trade-off: a derivative print contains an additional reproduction generation, but it can preserve a historical state of the negative before later physical changes occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Microfilm or microfiche introduces a different chain again. A microform image is normally a photographic reproduction of another document or photograph; digitising the microform adds a further capture stage. Such a source may have substantial [historical value]({{ 'historical-value/' | relative_url }}), particularly when it preserves an early reproduction unavailable elsewhere, but its high-resolution digital scan should not be described simply as equivalent to a direct scan of the Ramey negative. Contemporary Ramey research pages themselves distinguish high-resolution microfiche material from photographs attributed to the Fort Worth Star-Telegram collection at UTA.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/the-missing-u/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span></span></span>

This is why the phrase **“high-resolution Ramey memo scan” is incomplete provenance**. High resolution tells the reader something about the final sampling operation. It does not say what was sampled.

## The 2015 scans show what good provenance looks like

The 2015 UTA scanning project provides one of the clearest publicly documented examples against which unidentified internet copies can be judged. Contemporary accounts identify the physical source as the negative held in the Fort Worth Star-Telegram Photograph Collection at UTA Special Collections; identify the scanning participants as Kevin Randle, David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}), Simon Schollum and Jerry Morelock; place the work at UTA in April 2015; and subsequently provide an explicit attribution formula for distributed copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

Randle later addressed doubts about whether the object scanned was actually the original negative. His account says UTA staff, whose collection had received the negatives from the Fort Worth Star-Telegram, identified them as originals. He also published views showing the complete negative and its edge markings rather than presenting only an isolated rectangle around the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/05/the-ramey-memo-negative.html?showComment=1462541425906" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span></span></span>

Those details do not prove any proposed reading of the memo. They establish something narrower but essential: a comparatively strong provenance claim for a particular family of digital images. That distinction should be maintained. **Authentication of the source object is not authentication of a transcription.**

The full-frame material is particularly valuable for provenance checking because an uncropped image preserves evidence that disappears from memo-only extracts. Frame boundaries, orientation, edge markings, surrounding scene detail and characteristic defects can help researchers determine whether two files descend from the same physical negative or whether one is merely a transformed version of another. Once an internet copy has been tightly cropped to the paper, much of that comparison evidence is gone.

The 2015 history also demonstrates another useful principle: published derivatives should retain attribution. The scans were subsequently described as available for non-commercial sharing with credit to the scanning team and to the Fort Worth Star-Telegram Collection at UTA. A copy that retains that information offers a starting point for verification; an otherwise identical image reposted without it forces the next researcher to reconstruct the chain retrospectively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

## Processing history matters as much as the download

Even a genuinely negative-derived file can become problematic after capture. A useful distinction is between a **master** and a **derivative**. The master aims to preserve the capture faithfully; derivatives are produced for access, publication or analysis and may be cropped, resized, compressed or otherwise processed. The US National Archives similarly distinguishes long-term master-type files from distribution copies and notes that reproduction masters can undergo more processing, with further derivatives then made for online access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/products/definitions/reproduction-master" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Reproduction Master &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Reproduction Master &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

For memo analysis, common transformations have very different consequences. Rotation and perspective correction can make the sheet easier to inspect but require resampling. Contrast stretching may expose faint tonal differences while simultaneously making grain and dirt appear more letter-like. Sharpening increases local edge contrast without creating genuine optical resolution. Noise reduction may erase weak character strokes. Enlargement changes the number of pixels without adding new recorded detail. JPEG compression can introduce small block and edge artefacts precisely where researchers are trying to distinguish tiny marks.

None of those operations makes an image useless. The problem arises when the operation is undisclosed and the processed result is treated as though it were the archival capture.

FADGI's framework is instructive here because it explicitly covers the progression from image capture through sensor transfer and file creation, including the benefits and risks of processing, while its metadata guidance emphasises information that travels with digital objects throughout their life cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/still-image/charter.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Still Image Digitization Working Group Charter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesStill Image Digitization Working Group Charter - Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines InitiativeJuly 17, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 17, 2008</span></span></span> For a disputed image such as the Ramey memo, the practical implication is that processing should be reproducible: a researcher ought to be able to identify the input file and explain the operations that produced the displayed version.

That standard also guards against a subtle problem in online comparisons. Two supposedly independent “readings” may actually use files descended from the same earlier enhancement. Similar-looking letter forms then cease to be independent confirmation. They may simply reproduce the same sharpening artefact, contrast choice or interpolation pattern.

## What provenance information a serious comparison needs

A defensible Ramey image comparison should identify three layers separately: **physical source, digital capture and analytical derivative**. Mixing them together under a label such as “high-res scan” conceals exactly the information needed to judge reliability.

For the physical source, the record should give the holding institution or owner, collection where applicable, object type and generation. “Fort Worth Star-Telegram Collection, UTA Special Collections, camera negative” is meaningfully stronger than “Ramey photo from the internet”. If a print is involved, its size, approximate date and known relationship to the negative should be recorded. The same principle applies to microfilm or microfiche.

For the capture, useful information includes the date, operator or project, equipment where known, sampling resolution, bit depth, colour or greyscale mode, file format and whether the scan represents the complete source object. FADGI's cultural-heritage guidance treats technical parameters, image-quality evaluation, best practice and qualified imaging work as components of a reliable digitisation programme rather than assuming that a large raster file is self-authenticating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/digitize-technical.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesGuidelines: Technical Guidelines for Digitizing Cultural Heritage Materials - Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines...</span></span></span>

For the analytical derivative, the essential information is the parent file and every material transformation applied after capture. Ideally, researchers should retain an untouched master and create new working files from it rather than repeatedly saving edited derivatives. A published enhancement can then be accompanied by the unprocessed source crop so that readers can see which structures were already present.

File metadata can help, although it should not be treated as infallible proof. Embedded TIFF, IPTC or XMP metadata can preserve descriptive and technical information with an image; FADGI specifically recommends embedded metadata because it can remain associated with the digital object as it moves between systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/digitize-tiff.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesGuidelines: TIFF Image Metadata - Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines Initiative...</span></span></span> Metadata can also be stripped or altered, however, so it is strongest when it agrees with archive records, published scanning documentation and the image's physical characteristics.

Checksums or cryptographic hashes add another useful layer. They cannot prove that the original capture was authentic, but once a trusted master has been identified, a published hash allows researchers to establish whether they possess exactly the same file. That prevents silent recompression or modification from masquerading as the original scan.

## A practical trust test for an online Ramey image

The most useful way to assess a newly encountered copy is to work backwards rather than beginning with whatever words seem visible.

First, locate its claimed source. If the page says “from the original negative”, look for documentation connecting the file to the UTA-held negative and, ideally, to a named scanning project. The documented 2015 material provides recognisable attribution information against which such claims can be checked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

Second, seek a larger contextual version. A full-frame negative scan with edges is substantially easier to place in a known image family than an anonymous memo crop. Randle's publication of the complete negative and edge markings illustrates the kind of contextual evidence that makes source claims more testable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/05/the-ramey-memo-negative.html?showComment=1462541425906" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span></span></span>

Third, distinguish capture from enhancement. If the image has unusually crisp letters, ask whether an unsharpened or otherwise minimally processed version exists. Apparent readability should never substitute for provenance; indeed, a dramatically “cleaner” derivative deserves more scrutiny because its appearance may depend heavily on processing choices.

Fourth, compare independent source generations where possible. The 2002 research record shows that negative-derived material and several photographic enlargements were simultaneously available. Agreement across genuinely different source paths is more informative than agreement among several resized copies of the same web image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Finally, record uncertainty explicitly. A sensible description might be “apparently derived from the 2015 UTA negative scan, but downloaded as a resized JPEG with unknown intervening processing”. That is far more informative than calling the file either “authentic” or “fake”. Provenance is usually a chain with stronger and weaker documented links, not a binary label.

## Crisp does not mean close to the original

The central provenance problem surrounding digital Ramey memo copies is therefore less glamorous than decipherment but more fundamental. Researchers cannot meaningfully compare alleged words until they know what images they are comparing.

The surviving research record contains several distinguishable families of source material: archived negatives, enlargements produced from them at different times, later direct digital captures and further online derivatives. The 2002 study explicitly documented multiple physical generations, while the 2015 UTA project created a later set of negative-derived scans whose origin, participants and archival location were publicly identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Those histories make it unsafe to rank internet copies merely by apparent sharpness or pixel count.

Professional archival practice points in the same direction. Source-object identification, master-versus-derivative status, technical capture information, metadata and processing history all contribute to the reliability of a digital surrogate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/still-image/charter.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Still Image Digitization Working Group Charter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesStill Image Digitization Working Group Charter - Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines InitiativeJuly 17, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 17, 2008</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, provenance cannot make unresolved lettering readable. What it can do is prevent a more basic error: mistaking a polished descendant of a print, microform image or earlier web file for new information extracted from the original photographic negative. In a controversy where the disputed evidence may come down to minute grain-sized structures, knowing **where the pixels came from** is a prerequisite for deciding what, if anything, they say.
