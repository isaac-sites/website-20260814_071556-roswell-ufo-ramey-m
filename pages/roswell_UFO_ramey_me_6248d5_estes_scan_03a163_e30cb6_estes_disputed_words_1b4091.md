## Introduction

Russ Estes’s contribution to the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) debate is valuable less for the words he thought he could see than for the words he refused to certify. After intensive enlargement and image processing, he still said he could not reliably decipher the document. When pressed for tentative impressions, he offered only limited readings: most notably “Fort Worth, Tex.” and, on a more disputed line, the non-word-like sequence “ELA*”. The published literature originally misprinted his first reading as “Fort Work, Tex.”, an error later corrected by the authors themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction matters because other investigators were assigning consequential words to the same blurred character groups. Where competing readings included “DISK” or “ELSE”, Estes did not select whichever option produced the most coherent Roswell narrative. He reported the shapes he thought he saw even when they produced nonsense. His restraint exposes the central evidential problem: recognising several plausible character shapes is not the same thing as recovering a reliable transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The tentative “Fort Worth, Tex.” reading

The best-known account of Estes’s examination appears in James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 *Journal of Scientific Exploration* paper. Estes, a professional documentarian, examined enlarged photographic material using substantial imaging equipment and processing techniques. Yet the authors report that he could not reliably decipher the memo. Only after being pressed for a “best guess” did he identify a small number of shapes with any [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

One passage seemed stronger than most. Estes thought he could perceive “Fort Worth, Tex.”, but even here his confidence was explicitly limited. This is significant because Fort Worth was not an arbitrary phrase imposed on an otherwise context-free photograph: the photographs themselves were taken in General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }})’s office at Fort Worth. Agreement on those words therefore demonstrates that some photographed character information may survive without establishing that the surrounding lines are equally readable. Houran and Randle’s later experiment also found “Fort Worth TX” among the relatively few readings on which participants showed notable agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There is an unusually instructive complication. In the original Houran–Randle article, Estes’s reading appeared as “Fort Work, Tex.” The authors subsequently published a correction stating that Estes had actually read **“Fort Worth, Tex.”** They described their own mistake as an object lesson in how consequential errors can enter transcription even during careful research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/297491207_A_significant_typo_and_a_corresponding_object_lesson_7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That correction changes how the oft-repeated “Fort Work” phrase should be understood. It was **not** evidence that Estes saw an anomalous final word which happened to resemble “Work”. It was a publication error. The corrected “Fort Worth” reading is therefore the appropriate version to attribute to Estes. At the same time, the episode reinforces his broader caution: even comparatively clear language can be corrupted one step later when somebody copies, types or publishes what another observer reported seeing.

More importantly, success with “Fort Worth” cannot simply be transferred to harder passages. A recognisable place name provides unusually strong linguistic constraints. Once enough shapes suggest “FORT W…”, the known location makes “WORTH” a natural completion. An isolated blurred sequence with no comparable anchor offers many more possible solutions. That difference between a constrained identification and an unconstrained one is central to evaluating the memo.

## DISK, ELSE and the unresolved “ELA*” shapes

The sharper test of Estes’s approach came on the line beneath “Fort Worth”. Houran and Randle reported that different investigators had read a disputed character group as “Disk” and “ELSE”. Estes instead thought it looked like **“ELA*”**, with the asterisk representing an unresolved character rather than a confidently identified letter. He acknowledged that the result made no sense to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That apparently unhelpful result is evidentially useful. “DISK” is a meaningful Roswell word. “ELSE” is an ordinary English word. “ELA*” is neither an obvious completion nor a convenient narrative clue. By reporting the visually suggested sequence rather than forcing it into meaningful English, Estes effectively separated two questions that are easily conflated: **What do these marks resemble?** and **What word would make sense here?**

The disagreement is particularly important because “disk” or “disc” carries far greater interpretive weight than most ordinary vocabulary in the photograph. David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }})’s pro-Roswell reconstruction, for example, treats variants of “DISK” or “DISC” as central evidence that the memo concerned the recovered object and therefore supports a reading sharply at odds with the mundane explanation of the incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span></span></span> Estes’s “ELA*” observation does not prove that such readings are wrong. It shows something narrower but essential: the underlying shapes did not compel a trained examiner to recognise that particular word.

The competing [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) collected by Houran and Randle show how large the divergence could become. Different investigators constructed substantially different sentences from the same lines, including disagreements over such consequential terms as “victims”, “remains”, “disk” and other surrounding words. The dispute was therefore not merely about punctuation or a single damaged character; alternative character assignments could change the apparent subject and meaning of an entire sentence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Estes also resisted claims about the apparent signature block. Houran and Randle report that he could see nothing clearly resembling either of the proposed identifications. At most, he thought there might be an “M” somewhere within the sequence and perhaps “LE” near its end. Although those fragments could be made compatible with one suggested name, Estes compared the broader interpretive process to finding “faces in the clouds”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The point was not that every proposed character must therefore be imaginary. It was that weak visual fragments can support multiple coherent completions once an observer knows what kinds of words or names are being sought. The disputed “ELA*” group provides an unusually compact example: identical marks were capable of sustaining sharply different reported readings.

## Why isolated character guesses are not a transcription

A transcription makes a stronger claim than a collection of visual impressions. To transcribe the Ramey memo reliably, an investigator would need enough surviving character information to distinguish one sequence of letters from reasonable alternatives, and those identifications would need to remain stable when examined independently rather than becoming persuasive mainly after a proposed sentence is supplied.

The history of the memo shows why that threshold matters. The US Air Force reported that a national-level organisation digitised the photographs in 1994 but found them of insufficient quality to visualise the details sought. Estes later applied extensive enlargement and processing yet likewise declined to endorse a reliable transcription. Other investigators nevertheless produced considerably fuller readings. These outcomes are not logically incompatible — observers can extract different amounts of apparent information from the same degraded material — but the disagreement means that claimed words need validation beyond their seeming plausibility to one reader.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.users.globalnet.co.uk/~hex/ufo/text/pages026-050/page33.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: users.globalnet.co.uk">[Globalnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">users.globalnet.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air Force Roswell Report...</span></span></span>

Houran and Randle subsequently tested the role of [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) more directly. Participants were assigned different contextual conditions before attempting to interpret the memo. The study found that suggestion and cognitive factors affected what people reported, although participants also showed agreement on several comparatively recognisable items, including “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span></span></span>

That mixed result is more informative than saying either “nothing can be read” or “the memo has been decoded”. Some portions may contain enough signal to generate cross-reader agreement, while other regions remain sufficiently ambiguous for expectations and linguistic completion to influence the result. The existence of readable fragments therefore does not validate every neighbouring word.

Estes’s own disputed readings demonstrate three distinct confidence levels that should not be collapsed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A constrained, tentative identification:** “Fort Worth, Tex.”, which is compatible with the known setting and later attracted substantial cross-reader agreement.
* **An unresolved character sequence:** “ELA*”, where Estes reported apparent shapes without turning them into a meaningful word.
* **Insufficient information:** passages and the signature area for which he would not endorse the proposed readings at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

That hierarchy is more defensible than treating every enlarged mark as an equally valid letter. A blurred vertical stroke might be compatible with several characters; several ambiguous characters may fit numerous words; and once one plausible word is selected, grammar and knowledge of Roswell can make surrounding guesses seem more obvious than the photographic evidence itself warrants.

The problem becomes especially serious when the disputed word determines the historical conclusion. “DISK”, “victims” or a particular personal name is not evidentially equivalent to an ordinary connective word. If the interpretation of Roswell changes depending on whether four blurred shapes constitute “DISK”, then the certainty attached to each of those letters matters more than whether the completed word makes narrative sense.

## Estes’s caution changes what the memo can prove

Estes’s examination does not establish that every ambitious Ramey memo transcription is false. Nor does it establish what the disputed characters really say. Its importance is that an intensive examination produced **persistent [ambiguity]({{ 'ambiguity/' | relative_url }}) precisely where later interpretations can become historically consequential**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That leaves the strongest memo claims with a demanding burden of proof. A proposed reading should ideally survive independent examination by readers who have not been shown the expected phrase; alternative letter combinations should be tested rather than dismissed because they produce awkward language; and uncertainty should be retained at character level instead of disappearing when fragments are assembled into sentences. Houran and Randle themselves advocated independent checking, and their correction of the accidental “Fort Work” transcription vividly demonstrated why such safeguards matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/501208456/jse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; Falsifiability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; Falsifiability...</span></span></span>

This is also why Estes’s seemingly useless “ELA*” may be one of the most revealing observations in the entire episode. A nonsensical reading does not tell us what the memo says, but it preserves information about what the photograph **fails to determine**. Replacing that uncertainty with “DISK”, “ELSE” or any other completed word requires additional evidence that distinguishes the chosen letters from their competitors.

The most defensible lesson, then, is not that Estes decoded a different Ramey memo. He did something more limited: he showed how quickly a few ambiguous strokes can become words once an interpreter demands that they mean something. His corrected “Fort Worth, Tex.” demonstrates that portions of the image may support useful readings. His unresolved “ELA*”, rejected signature claims and refusal to certify a complete transcription show why that success cannot simply be extended across the page. For the memo’s most famous disputed words, the unresolved characters remain part of the evidence rather than an inconvenience to be edited out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
