## Introduction

Yes — the best direct experiment on the question found that people with greater prior exposure to UFOs and Roswell reported deciphering more words from the blurred [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}). But the important qualification is that the experiment measured the **quantity of words people believed they could read, not whether those readings were more accurate**. Greater [familiarity]({{ 'familiarity/' | relative_url }}) therefore appears to have made the memo seem more interpretable; it was not shown to make participants better decoders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That distinction matters unusually strongly for the Ramey memo because its photographed lettering is severely degraded. When the visual evidence does not uniquely determine a word, [prior knowledge]({{ 'prior-knowledge/' | relative_url }}) can provide plausible completions. Someone immersed in Roswell has more names, phrases and expectations available with which to complete uncertain shapes. The 2002 experiment by James Houran and Kevin Randle found exactly the pattern that this mechanism would predict: Roswell exposure correlated with more reported words, while experimentally supplied context also changed what participants thought they saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What the UFO–Roswell scale actually measured

The crucial variable in the Houran–Randle study is easy to misdescribe as “Roswell knowledge”. The researchers called it a study-specific index of prior exposure or knowledge concerning Roswell and the Ramey memo, but its eight questions actually mixed **knowledge, media exposure, belief and claimed personal experience**. The authors say explicitly that the lead author constructed the items on the basis of “face validity”; it was not a validated examination of expertise in photographic analysis, document restoration or historical transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The questionnaire asked participants about eight progressively more Roswell-specific matters. These included belief in intelligent extraterrestrial life and UFOs, claimed personal UFO experience, enjoyment of UFO television or radio programmes, exposure to books, films or programmes about Roswell, belief that Roswell involved an extraterrestrial craft, previous awareness of the Ramey memo, and previous reading about attempts to interpret the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span>

That composition changes how the study should be understood. A high score did **not** necessarily identify somebody who possessed unusually good visual discrimination skills. Nor did it cleanly distinguish a knowledgeable Roswell sceptic from a strongly committed believer. Instead, higher scores broadly represented greater involvement with the UFO–Roswell subject matter.

Calling the result an “expertise effect” would therefore go beyond the evidence. A more defensible description is a **familiarity-and-involvement effect**: people with more UFO/Roswell exposure, beliefs and related experience tended to produce more candidate words when confronted with the ambiguous photograph.

## Why Roswell familiarity was linked to more reported words

Across the 176 participants, Houran and Randle reported a Spearman rank-order correlation of **0.35** between scores on their UFO–Roswell scale and the number of words deciphered, statistically significant at *p*<.05. In plain terms, participants scoring higher on the combined Roswell/UFO measure tended to say that they could extract more words from the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

The result becomes more revealing when combined with the experiment's manipulation of context. Participants had been randomly divided into three groups. Fifty-nine were told that the document concerned the Roswell UFO case, 58 were told that it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing, and 59 received no explanation of what the document might contain. The resulting interpretations tended to follow the supplied scenario.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For example, the researchers reported Roswell-related interpretations in the Roswell condition, while people given the atomic-testing explanation produced candidate words such as “flash”, “glasses” and “atomic”. The blind group produced fewer interpretations and no comparably strong thematic pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This provides a useful mechanism for understanding the familiarity correlation. Knowledge does not have to make somebody consciously invent a word. Instead, it can narrow the field of possibilities considered plausible. Faced with a faint sequence of strokes, a Roswell-aware observer already possesses a vocabulary of potentially relevant terms. Once one of those possibilities occurs to the observer, ambiguous marks may appear to fit it.

The experiment therefore points to two related routes by which context can influence decipherment. **Immediate suggestion** supplies a [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) just before the task; **prior familiarity** supplies a larger reservoir of expectations accumulated beforehand. Both can help an observer turn an uncertain visual pattern into something that feels linguistically meaningful. The study demonstrated associations consistent with this explanation, although it did not isolate the precise perceptual or cognitive process responsible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why seeing more words does not establish accuracy

This is the most important limitation of the result. The dependent measure was the **number of words deciphered**. A participant who proposed many uncertain words could therefore score higher on that measure than somebody who cautiously reported only one or two. Nothing about the positive 0.35 correlation establishes that the additional words supplied by more Roswell-involved participants corresponded more closely to the memo's original text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>

Indeed, the suggestion results give a reason to resist equating productivity with accuracy. If participants can obtain atomic-testing vocabulary after being told that the document concerns atomic testing, then producing coherent-looking words from the image is not by itself strong validation of those words. The same blurred marks evidently allowed interpretations compatible with different narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There was also an unfortunate limitation in the surviving experimental data. Houran and Randle reported that the research assistant discarded the participants' original score sheets after compiling the database and recording words common to the three conditions. Consequently, the authors could not later provide a complete word-by-word count showing exactly how many participants in each experimental condition had perceived every proposed word.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That prevents a stronger retrospective test of an especially interesting question: did people with greater Roswell familiarity merely propose **more** words, or did they independently converge more often on the **same** words? Those are very different evidential outcomes. Independent convergence on the same location and same reading would be more interesting than a proliferation of mutually incompatible guesses.

The study did find some cross-condition agreement. A few words or fragments appeared at corresponding locations regardless of whether participants had received the Roswell, atomic-bomb or blind framing. The authors therefore did not conclude that the entire document was hopeless or that every claimed word resulted from suggestion. Instead, they argued that some portions might potentially be recoverable if future work used stricter methodological controls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## Familiarity can help and bias at the same time

The experiment does not justify the sweeping claim that knowing about Roswell automatically makes someone worse at reading the Ramey memo. Domain knowledge can genuinely be useful. Someone familiar with 1947 military terminology, communications formats, names, locations and abbreviations can recognise plausible structures that an uninformed observer might overlook.

The problem arises when **plausibility is allowed to substitute for visual evidence**. The more degraded a letter sequence becomes, the more candidate readings can fit it. Prior knowledge then has greater opportunity to resolve uncertainty. A proposed word may feel compelling because its letters seem to fit the marks, its grammar fits neighbouring guesses and its meaning fits the Roswell story. Those three forms of apparent confirmation are not fully independent if the initial reading itself was prompted by the story.

The historical dispute illustrates the difficulty. Published Ramey-memo interpretations compared by Houran and Randle disagreed extensively, although investigators sometimes converged on particular words or structures. Their comparison table records both agreements and substantial variation between proposed readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span> Contemporary claims continue to treat readings such as “victims of the wreck” as potentially significant, but even Randle has publicly acknowledged that such wording remains an interpretation of what is visible rather than an uncontested transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thesun.co.uk/news/35262595/roswell-crash-memo-key-cracking-ufo-mystery-alien-bodies/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thesun.co.uk">[The Sun]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thesun.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ufologist Kevin Randle argues that the memo, photographed in the hands of Brigadier General Roger Ramey, includes the phrase &quot;victims of...</span></span></span>

The practical consequence is that **[confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) should not rise simply because a reading sounds historically appropriate**. A Roswell-compatible phrase has two possible explanations: it may reflect genuine information preserved in the photograph, or Roswell knowledge may have helped the observer organise ambiguous marks into a Roswell-compatible phrase. The 2002 experiment shows that the second pathway is real enough that it cannot simply be ignored.

## What would count as stronger evidence of a real reading?

The knowledge effect suggests that the strongest Ramey-memo decipherment would be one that survives attempts to remove contextual influence. The original researchers themselves favoured methodological controls rather than abandoning the document altogether.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

A persuasive test would therefore separate **recognition from expectation**. Independent readers should ideally encounter the same image without knowing that it is connected with Roswell, without seeing previous proposed transcripts and without being given a vocabulary of expected terms. Candidate readings could then be recorded before historical context was disclosed. Agreement should be evaluated by exact position as well as by word, rather than accepting approximately similar phrases.

Most importantly, success should be defined in advance as **repeatable agreement**, not simply the number of words somebody can produce. If blind observers independently recover the same unusual word from the same location, that is much harder to explain through Roswell-specific expectation. If knowledgeable observers produce many more words but those readings diverge from one another or follow whichever contextual story they have been given, increased apparent legibility is much less impressive.

That is the central lesson of the Houran–Randle experiment. Roswell familiarity was associated with **more decipherment**, but more decipherment was not demonstrated to mean **better decipherment**. On an image as ambiguous as the Ramey memo, feeling able to see additional words and establishing what the document actually said remain two separate achievements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case...</span></span></span>
