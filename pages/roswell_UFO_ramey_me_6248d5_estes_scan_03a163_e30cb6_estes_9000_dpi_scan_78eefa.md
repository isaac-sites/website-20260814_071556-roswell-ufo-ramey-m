## Introduction

Russ Estes’s 1998 examination of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) is memorable for the number attached to it: **9,000 dpi**. Yet the important result was not an extraordinarily detailed transcription. It was almost the opposite. After photographing, magnifying, scanning and digitally manipulating a large reproduction of the 1947 photograph, Estes reported that he still could **not reliably decipher the memo**. His few proposed readings were explicitly tentative, and some conflicted with the much more dramatic [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) circulating among Roswell researchers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That makes the Estes scan unusually useful evidence. It tested the proposition that enough enlargement and professional imaging would make the disputed characters resolve themselves into unambiguous letters. They largely did not. The episode therefore illustrates the central limitation of high-resolution work on the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) photograph: an extremely fine scan can preserve and expose whatever information survives in the source being scanned, but it cannot automatically restore letter strokes that the earlier photographic chain failed to record distinctly. Federal digitisation guidance similarly distinguishes sampling resolution from the actual quality and recoverable detail of an image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[digitizationguidelines.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span></span></span>

## The 16-by-20-inch print and imaging setup

The fullest published account of Estes’s work appears in James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 *Journal of Scientific Exploration* paper, which cites a 19 November 1998 letter from Estes to Randle as its source. According to that account, Estes was a professional documentarian and worked from a **16-by-20-inch print made by the University of Texas library**. His investigation was therefore substantially more elaborate than simply enlarging a small reproduction on a computer monitor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The equipment described was extensive. Estes reportedly captured the image with a professional video camera and high-quality macro lens, processed it with professional imaging software, and inspected the material using a jeweller’s loupe, magnifying glass and microscope. He then scanned it at a reported **9,000 dpi**, producing a file described in the 2002 paper as **1.7 gigabits** in size, which could be enlarged and manipulated still further.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those figures should be interpreted carefully. The 9,000-dpi number describes the sampling of Estes’s source material; it does **not** establish that the tiny memo within J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})’s 1947 photograph contained anything approaching 9,000 dpi of genuine textual information. Modern Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative guidance defines spatial resolution as the density of picture elements used to represent an object, while stressing that spatial resolution by itself does not guarantee the quality of the information represented. Canadian Heritage Information Network guidance likewise notes that increasing scanning resolution produces larger files, with recommendations for detailed photographic prints far below 9,000 ppi in ordinary preservation imaging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/790816115/FADGI-Technical-Guidelines-for-Digitizing-Cultural-Heritage-Materials-3rd-Edition-05092023" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FADGI Technical Guidelines 2023 &#124; PDF &#124; Image Scanner &#124; Image Resolution...</span></span></span>

That distinction is especially important here because Estes was examining a **photographic print containing an image of a sheet of paper**, rather than scanning the memo itself. Every stage between the original typed characters and his digital file imposed a potential limit: the characters' small size within the 1947 scene, the camera lens and focus, [film grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}), exposure, the memo’s angle and curvature, photographic printing and finally the scan of that reproduction. A later scanner can sample those surviving tones extremely finely, but it cannot assume that each apparent edge or dark patch corresponds to an intact typewritten stroke.

There is also an important source distinction that can easily disappear in retellings of the 9,000-dpi story. Estes’s published account concerns the **16-by-20 print**, whereas other investigators subsequently or separately worked with high-resolution scans made from the original negative. Houran and Randle specifically distinguished Estes’s work from Rob Belyea’s examination of scans produced after the original negatives were taken from the University of Texas at Arlington Special Collections to a computer laboratory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> The university’s own archival material confirms that the original July 1947 negatives are held in its Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span>

That difference matters. Calling Estes’s file a “9,000-dpi scan” is accurate as reported, but it should not be mistaken for a 9,000-dpi extraction directly from Johnson’s original camera negative.

## What Estes could and could not read

The clearest finding was negative: **Estes said he could not reliably decipher the text**. Houran and Randle report that this remained his judgement despite the large print, specialised equipment, extreme scanning resolution and multiple ways of examining the image. Only when pressed for a best guess did he offer possible readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those guesses are revealing precisely because they were so limited.

One relatively promising area was the familiar Fort Worth line. After also examining an **8-by-10 enlargement specifically of the memo area**, Estes thought he could perceive something rendered in the 2002 account as **“Fort Work, Tex”**, but only with limited confidence. The likely resemblance to “Fort Worth, Tex.” is noteworthy because other independent readers also identified Fort Worth in approximately that location. Houran and Randle’s later experiment found “Fort Worth TEX” among the small number of readings that appeared across differently primed groups of observers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Elsewhere, Estes’s results became much less word-like. At a position where competing investigators had proposed readings including “Disk” and “ELSE”, he saw something resembling **“ELA*”** — with the asterisk representing an unresolved character. He acknowledged that this apparent sequence made no sense; it was simply his attempt to describe what the ambiguous marks looked like rather than force them into a meaningful sentence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The supposed signature provides another useful example. Competing readings had treated the bottom of the memo as potentially containing an identifiable name. Estes could not see a convincing version of the proposed signatures. At most, the published account says, he thought there might be an **“M”** somewhere in the word and possibly **“LE”** towards its end. Those fragments could be made compatible with “Temple”, but they fell far short of demonstrating that “Temple” was actually present. Estes compared the process of turning such marks into confident words to seeing **“faces in the clouds”**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The disputed “victims” reading was even more consequential. Later, Randle summarised Estes’s character-level objections to that proposed word. Estes thought its first character looked more like a **P than a V**; another character appeared lower-case despite the proposed teletype text otherwise being treated as capitals; and what was supposed to be the final **S** looked to him more like an italic figure 5. These observations did not prove an alternative word. Their importance was that the visible shapes did not uniquely require “VICTIMS”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span></span></span>

That is a substantially different evidential claim from saying that Estes “read the memo”. What he recovered was better described as a mixture of **recognisable structure, occasional plausible character fragments and ambiguous blobs capable of supporting several interpretations**.

## Why 9,000 dpi did not produce 9,000-dpi evidence

The apparent paradox disappears once scanning resolution is separated from **source resolution**. Imagine a blurred printed letter occupying only a handful of distinguishable tonal transitions in a photograph. Scanning that photograph with far more pixels gives a more finely sampled representation of the blur. It does not necessarily reveal whether the original character was an E, F, P or some other letter if the distinguishing stroke had already been lost.

Library of Congress material provides a useful real-world illustration of the principle. In describing digitised panoramic photographs, the Library explains that a scan resolution attached to an intermediate film does not translate directly into the same resolution relative to the original photograph; the effective resolution depends on the relationship between the scanned intermediate and the original source. It also warns that reproduction quality depends on more than the nominal scan number.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/collections/panoramic-photographs/about-this-collection/technical-information/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on loc.gov.</span></span></span>

Modern preservation practice makes the same distinction in another way. Library of Congress duplication specifications vary scanning resolution according to the physical size and type of the original negative or object rather than treating the largest dpi number as inherently superior.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wwws.loc.gov/duplicationservices/products-pricing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wwws.loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wwws.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Products &amp; Pricing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressProducts &amp; Pricing - Duplication Services (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span> Federal guidance likewise treats sampling frequency as only one component of imaging performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[digitizationguidelines.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span></span></span>

For the Estes experiment, therefore, the huge digital file had genuine value: it reduced the chance that **his digital sampling** was the obvious bottleneck. It permitted close inspection and manipulation without immediately running out of pixels. But once the scan was sampling the meaningful detail in the photographic print adequately, additional pixels could increasingly describe grain, blur and existing tonal transitions rather than reveal missing typewriter strokes.

This is why enlargement and enhancement need to be distinguished from recovery. Sharpening can make an edge appear more pronounced; contrast adjustment can separate light and dark areas; interpolation can make enlarged material smoother; and filtering can suppress some distracting variations. None of those operations guarantees that a newly conspicuous shape represents an original letter rather than a mixture of photographic grain, printing characteristics and processing choices.

## Why the negative result matters

Estes’s examination did not prove that **nothing** on the Ramey memo is readable. In fact, subsequent research points towards a more nuanced conclusion. Houran and Randle’s controlled 2002 study assigned participants to three conditions: they were told that the document concerned Roswell, told that it concerned atomic-bomb testing, or given no proposed context. Many participants reported words, but their readings were influenced by the context they had been given. At the same time, a few words appeared in corresponding locations across conditions, suggesting that some genuine textual information survives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The interesting lesson from Estes is therefore not “the memo is completely illegible”. It is that **partial legibility is not the same thing as a dependable transcription**.

That distinction became increasingly important as proposed readings grew longer and more consequential. The 1998 Roswell Photo Interpretation Team, for example, proposed phrases involving “VICTIMS”, a “CRASH” story, emergency powers, Magdalena and weather balloons. Yet other analysts examining the same underlying photograph produced materially different words. Houran and Randle documented disagreements not merely over minor spelling but over words whose substitution could radically change the apparent meaning of the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Estes supplied a particularly useful counterweight because his uncertainty survived **after** unusually intensive technical examination. If the obstacle had simply been insufficient enlargement, his 9,000-dpi workflow should have made the controversial letters substantially easier to settle. Instead, he still encountered character shapes that could plausibly be classified in incompatible ways.

That conclusion was reinforced by another professional examination discussed alongside his. Rob Belyea of ProLab worked with high-resolution scans derived from the negative and said he could not confirm the proposed reading “Magdalena”; Houran and Randle quote him as believing that some investigators were effectively creating parts of their readings rather than extracting them securely from the image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> An earlier Air Force-sponsored effort had likewise reported in 1994 that digitisation left the photographs insufficient to visualise the details being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span></span></span>

The later experimental evidence makes Estes’s caution still more significant. Houran and Randle found that observers given a Roswell context spent longer examining the memo, while observers primed with an atomic-testing story reported words associated with that alternative scenario. Nevertheless, a small set of terms — including versions of “Fort Worth TEX”, “Story” and “Balloons” — appeared across conditions. Their conclusion was not that all readings were imaginary, but that the document combined **some legible information with enough ambiguity for suggestion and [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) to influence interpretation**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What the Estes scan actually recovered

Stripped of the impressive hardware specifications, the 1998 result is fairly modest. Estes recovered enough visual structure to make **a few tentative guesses**, particularly around the Fort Worth wording, but not enough stable character information to reconstruct a secure message. Other areas yielded fragments such as “ELA*”, possible isolated letters in the signature, or shapes inconsistent with confident readings proposed by other investigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is why the 9,000-dpi figure should not be presented as evidence that a complete hidden text was extracted. The scan demonstrated something more useful about the Ramey memo’s evidential limits: **very high sampling and very large enlargement did not make its disputed characters converge on one obvious transcription**.

The surviving photograph is not devoid of information. The University of Texas at Arlington still holds Johnson’s original negatives, and its archival description notes that researchers have continued trying to determine the memo’s contents; the institution itself describes the document as not having been definitively deciphered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span> Randle’s 2022 review likewise concluded that attempts to read it had not produced definitive results and documented continuing disagreement over the wording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationA Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; Journal of Scientific ExplorationAugust 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 20, 2022</span></span></span>

For the specific question raised by Russ Estes’s experiment, however, the answer is unusually clear. **The 9,000-dpi scan recovered a highly enlarged representation of the available photographic detail, not a reliable transcript of the Ramey memo.** Its most important product was therefore not a sensational new phrase but a practical demonstration of the boundary between seeing marks and proving what those marks say.
