## Introduction

The blind group is the most useful baseline in James Houran and Kevin Randle's 2002 [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) experiment because its 59 participants were given the difficult photograph without being told what the document was supposed to be about. Their results were strikingly sparse. Blind participants reported an average of only 1.6 deciphered words, compared with 4.6 in the Roswell-primed condition and 4.8 in the atomic-bomb condition. They also spent less time on the task: about 14 minutes on average, versus 20 minutes for the Roswell group and 16 minutes for the atomic group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_houran_randle_study_efb939_blind_group_baseline_876de7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Blind Baseline illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_houran_randle_study_efb939_blind_group_baseline_876de7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_houran_randle_study_efb939_blind_group_baseline_876de7-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That does not mean the [blind readers]({{ 'blind-readers/' | relative_url }}) saw nothing. Some identified the same fragments found in the themed groups, particularly "Fort Worth TX", "[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})" and "weather balloons". But they did not spontaneously produce the distinctive Roswell vocabulary that appeared after participants had been told a UFO-crash story. That contrast is why the blind condition matters: it provides the experiment's clearest indication of what readers could extract when the investigators did not first supply a narrative for ambiguous marks to fit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What the blind readers were actually told

Houran and Randle recruited 176 participants and randomly assigned them to three conditions. Fifty-nine were told that the photographed document concerned Roswell and might contain information about an extraterrestrial crash and cover-up; 58 were given an atomic-bomb-testing explanation; and 59 received no proposed subject at all. The researchers described this last group as "blind" with respect to expectations about the document's possible content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The blind condition was not, however, a test conducted with inferior imagery. Participants across the experiment were shown an 8-by-10-inch enlargement of the message area and computer displays derived from scans, were given a magnifying glass, and were asked to work through the ten-line block of text line by line. A research assistant who was blind to the researchers' goals and hypotheses could help them manipulate the computer images. No fixed time limit was imposed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

What distinguished the blind participants was therefore primarily the absence of a supplied explanation. They were effectively confronting an old, poorly resolved photographed document rather than "the Roswell memo" or an "atomic-bomb memo". That distinction is central. If a particular word really has enough visual information to be recoverable from the photograph, there is at least a reasonable [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) that some readers should be capable of reaching it without first being told that the document concerns a subject that makes the word meaningful.

The blind condition was not completely free of every conceivable prior influence. After the deciphering exercise, Houran and Randle measured participants' previous exposure to UFO and Roswell material, and they found that such individual characteristics were associated with the number of words deciphered across the experiment. "Blind", in other words, meant blind to the experimental suggestion, not necessarily ignorant of Roswell culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## How much disappeared when the story disappeared?

The clearest numerical result is the difference in the quantity of supposed text recovered. The Roswell group averaged 4.6 deciphered words and the atomic-bomb group 4.8, while the blind group averaged only 1.6. The overall effect of suggestion condition on the number of deciphered words was statistically significant, with the two themed groups producing significantly more words than the blind control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That comparison is especially revealing because giving participants a story did not simply make everyone read the same extra text. The additional readings tended to fit the particular story supplied.

In the Roswell condition, reported words included "remains", "crash" and "UFO". In the atomic-bomb condition, distinctive readings included "glasses", "flash", "atomic" and "laboratory". The blind condition instead generated relatively few interpretations, and Houran and Randle reported that these did not strongly reflect any particular scenario.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This is stronger evidence for a context effect than a simple finding that interested people try harder. Greater motivation may explain why the two themed groups extracted *more* apparent words, and the authors themselves raised that possibility. The Roswell participants spent about six minutes longer on the task than the blind participants. But effort alone does not readily explain why the extra vocabulary changed with the supplied narrative — UFO-related terms under one suggestion and atomic-related terms under another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The blind group consequently supplies an important counterfactual. Remove the explanatory story while leaving the difficult visual target essentially the same, and the elaborate interpretation largely collapses. What remains becomes especially interesting.

## Which readings survived without Roswell priming?

The experiment did not show that the photograph contained no recoverable text. On the contrary, the blind condition helps isolate a small core of readings that deserve more attention precisely because they were not confined to a themed group.

Across all three conditions, participants agreed to some degree on "Fort Worth TX", "story" and "weather balloons", with those readings appearing in the same locations in the document. Across the complete sample, the study recorded 52 identifications of "Fort Worth TX", 61 of "story" and 27 of "weather balloons".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The group-by-group figures make the baseline more informative. Table 3 reports "Fort Worth TX" from 24 Roswell participants, 17 atomic-bomb participants and 11 blind participants. "Story" appeared among 43 Roswell participants, 11 atomic participants and seven blind participants. "Weather balloons" was reported by 20 Roswell participants, five atomic participants and two blind participants. Various punctuation marks were also perceived throughout the conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those distributions require careful interpretation. Cross-condition recurrence is more persuasive than a reading confined to people who were explicitly encouraged to expect it, particularly when the word appears in the same place. Yet recurrence is not the same thing as a verified transcription. "Story" and "weather balloons", for example, were much more frequently reported in the Roswell group than in the blind group. The experiment therefore provides grounds for treating them as candidates for genuinely visible material, but not for assuming that their identification is independent of context.

One other contrast is useful. "Land" was identified 47 times across the experiment's two themed conditions but was not among the blind group's reported common readings. Houran and Randle suggested that participants in the primed groups might have been more motivated or discriminating, but the absence also illustrates the methodological problem: agreement between readers who share a context is weaker evidence than agreement that survives removal of that context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The blind results therefore create a practical hierarchy of [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}). A phrase reported only after a highly relevant suggestion deserves substantial caution. A reading reproduced under an unrelated suggestion is more interesting. A reading independently reproduced when no subject has been supplied is stronger still — although it remains provisional until independent, genuinely blinded analysis can establish reliable agreement.

## The baseline has an important data limitation

There is a significant limitation that is easy to miss when the experiment is summarised simply as three lists of words. The researchers could not later provide complete participant-by-participant frequencies for every deciphered word.

According to the paper, a referee requested those detailed counts, but the primary research assistant had disposed of the original score sheets after compiling the computer database and notes concerning words shared among conditions. Consequently, the authors retained detailed numerical information for the common readings, but not equivalent frequency data for every condition-specific interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That loss matters particularly for evaluating the blind baseline. Table 3 lists blind-condition readings including "Flew" alongside the cross-condition candidates, but the surviving published data do not let readers reconstruct exactly how often every exclusive interpretation occurred or assess the full distribution of low-frequency guesses. It would therefore be too strong to present the published blind vocabulary as a complete, finely quantified map of everything unprimed readers perceived.

The experiment is strongest at the group level: blind readers produced substantially fewer words, their readings lacked the pronounced scenario-specific character seen in the two primed conditions, and a limited set of possible words nevertheless recurred across conditions. It is weaker as a word-by-word validation experiment.

There is another caution in treating 1.6 versus roughly 4.7 words as a pure measurement of priming. The groups differed not only in suggested meaning but also in average time spent examining the material. Houran and Randle themselves proposed that the inherently interesting Roswell and atomic-bomb stories could have increased motivation. A later review by Randle reiterated this pattern: the Roswell group worked longest, the atomic group next longest and the blinded group least.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The result therefore supports an intertwined effect of expectation, interest and effort rather than providing a perfectly isolated measure of suggestion alone. Crucially, however, the thematic shift in the *kind* of words reported remains difficult to reduce merely to extra viewing time.

## Why the blind condition changes the transcription dispute

For disputed Ramey memo transcriptions, the blind group changes the relevant question. It is not enough to ask whether a proposed word can be made visually compatible with the marks after somebody points out where it supposedly appears. The stronger question is whether [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) who have not been told what the document concerns can identify the same word in the same location.

That distinction matters because the photograph is highly ambiguous. Earlier investigators had produced materially different transcriptions even while using enlargements, image processing and considerable effort. Houran and Randle concluded that parts of the document might genuinely be legible, but argued that the wider meaning attributed to those fragments remained vulnerable to suggestion and cognitive style.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The blind baseline therefore does **not** establish that Roswell-related readings such as "remains", "crash" or "UFO" are impossible. It establishes a more limited point: in this experiment those terms did not emerge as the conspicuous shared residue when the Roswell narrative was withheld. By contrast, fragments such as "Fort Worth TX" crossed the experimental boundary and therefore have a different evidential status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Nor does the baseline prove the official weather-balloon explanation merely because some participants reported "weather balloons". Only two blind participants were recorded as making that reading, compared with 20 in the Roswell condition. The interesting result is not that the experiment somehow decoded the document as a weather-balloon message. It is that this phrase had at least some cross-condition recognition, whereas several dramatic, scenario-specific terms clustered in the groups whose instructions encouraged precisely those interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What a stronger blind test would require

Houran and Randle ultimately treated blindness not merely as an experimental control but as a requirement for better Ramey memo research. They recommended analysis by at least three independent laboratories specialising in archival document reading and transcription, using standardised enhancement procedures and remaining blind to the document's expected content. Agreement would need to concern specific words in precise locations rather than merely a broadly similar interpretation of the message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That proposal follows directly from what the 59-person blind group demonstrated. With no narrative supplied, participants became much less prolific interpreters. Yet they did not become completely incapable of finding text. The combination is precisely what a useful baseline should produce: it suppresses some expectation-driven readings without automatically assuming that every perceived character is an illusion.

A later review by Randle acknowledged that the original study attracted criticism, including conceptual and statistical objections from advocates of particular Ramey memo interpretations, while also noting the absence of a direct or conceptual replication designed to resolve those criticisms experimentally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2713/1663" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span> That leaves the 2002 experiment suggestive rather than definitive. Its blind group should not be treated as the final transcription of the Ramey document.

Its narrower lesson is more durable. When 59 people were shown the same problematic material without being handed a Roswell or atomic-bomb narrative, they reported far fewer words and no comparably strong thematic story. A small number of readings nevertheless survived across experimental conditions. For assessing disputed Ramey memo transcriptions, those two findings belong together: **cross-blind agreement is evidence worth pursuing; a convincing reading that appears mainly after the reader knows what to look for requires much greater caution.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
