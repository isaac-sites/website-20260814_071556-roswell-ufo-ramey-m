## Introduction

The strongest candidates for genuinely surviving text in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) are not necessarily the most dramatic proposed words. They are the words that different readers identified **at the same locations despite being given different expectations about what the document contained**. In James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 experiment, three groups were primed with a Roswell/UFO [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}), an atomic-bomb story, or no story at all. Much of what participants reported changed with the suggestion. A small core did not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_expectation_effects_9e6957_cross_group_readabil_cf14fd-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Cross Group Test illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_expectation_effects_9e6957_cross_group_readabil_cf14fd-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_expectation_effects_9e6957_cross_group_readabil_cf14fd-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That distinction matters. Cross-group agreement provides a practical test for separating information plausibly constrained by the photographed marks from interpretations generated partly by context. The experiment found such agreement for “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”; “land” also attracted substantial agreement, although not across all three conditions. These results support a limited conclusion: portions of the photograph may contain recoverable text, while a much larger proposed transcription remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why matching locations matter


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Agreement is meaningful here only if readers identify the same word **in the same place**. If one participant reports “story” on one line and another sees “story” somewhere else, the match could be coincidental. When readers independently attach the same word to the same cluster of blurred marks, however, there is a stronger reason to suspect that those marks retain enough visual information to constrain perception.

</div>

Houran and Randle built their deciphering procedure around location. Participants examined enlarged and digitised versions of the memo and recorded what they perceived line by line across its ten-line text block. They had access to an enlarged print, several high-resolution images and a magnifying glass, while a research assistant who was blind to the study's hypotheses helped them manipulate the digital images. There was no time limit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This location requirement is particularly useful because the experiment deliberately changed what readers expected to find. The Roswell group was encouraged towards one semantic field, the atomic-bomb group towards another, and the blind group had no suggested subject. If the same sequence survives all three settings at the same position, the common factor is no longer the supplied story. The obvious common factor is the image itself.

That does not make the resulting word certain. Several people can still make the same mistake, particularly where a familiar word fits an incomplete pattern. But it gives such a reading a substantially different evidential status from a word that appears only when readers have been prompted with a matching scenario. Houran and Randle explicitly treated agreement on specific words in precise locations as the basis on which inter-rater reliability — agreement between [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) — should ultimately be evaluated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The readings that survived different expectations

The experiment's most useful result for this question is therefore not the total number of words participants thought they could read, but the small set that crossed experimental boundaries.

“Fort Worth TX” was reported at the same location by **52 participants overall**: 24 in the Roswell condition, 17 in the atomic-bomb condition and 11 in the blind condition. “Story” produced **61 reports overall**, distributed much less evenly: 43 in the Roswell group, 11 in the atomic group and seven in the blind group. “Weather balloons” was reported **27 times**: 20 in the Roswell condition, five in the atomic condition and two in the blind condition. The researchers also recorded punctuation across all three groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those figures reveal an important nuance that a simple list of “agreed words” can conceal. Cross-group survival is not the same as equal recognition rates. “Fort Worth TX” was comparatively well distributed among all three conditions. “Story” and especially “weather balloons” were much more likely to be reported by Roswell-primed readers, even though some readers outside that condition also identified them. The experiment therefore supports treating cross-condition agreement as a **continuum of confidence**, not a binary stamp declaring a word proved.

“Land” illustrates the boundary particularly well. The study recorded 47 reports of it, but 41 came from the Roswell condition and six from the atomic-bomb condition; it was not among the reported blind-group readings. Houran and Randle consequently described it as a word of “relative agreement” between the two suggestion conditions rather than placing it on the same footing as the three-condition matches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The shared readings are also significant because they overlap with portions identified in earlier attempts to decipher the photograph. Previous Ramey-memo investigators had independently proposed “AT FORT WORTH”, “WEATHER BALLOONS” and “LAND” in corresponding parts of their reconstructions. Their wider transcriptions differed considerably, and the researchers noted that those earlier investigators had shared information while working, which weakens claims of complete independence. Nevertheless, the experimental groups subsequently locating some of the same words provides a separate reason for thinking that limited regions of the photograph carry recognisable structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Suggestion-specific words fail the same test

The value of the cross-group criterion becomes clearer when it is applied to the words that **did not** survive it.

Participants primed with Roswell reported readings including “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”. The atomic-bomb group instead produced terms including “glasses”, “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. These scenario-congruent readings appeared when the relevant narrative had been supplied and did not emerge as common same-location readings across all three conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span>

That contrast is the experiment's most useful internal control. Both primed groups were looking at the same photographic material, yet changing the introductory story changed what appeared readable. The manipulation even affected quantity: the Roswell group reported an average of 4.6 deciphered words and the atomic-bomb group 4.8, compared with only 1.6 in the blind condition. The difference between the two primed groups and the blind group was statistically significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This does not establish that every word unique to one group must be false. A genuine but very weak word might be recognised only after an appropriate context directs attention towards it. The problem is evidential: once a candidate appears primarily under the condition that suggests it, the experiment cannot distinguish recognition from [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) strongly enough to treat that candidate as securely recovered text.

That distinction is especially relevant to sensational proposed readings of the memo. Earlier investigators had reached substantial agreement on phrases including “VICTIMS OF THE WRECK” and on “DISK” or “DISC” in their reconstructions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Yet those expressions did not emerge among the experiment's robust three-condition consensus readings. The 2002 experiment therefore supplies much stronger perceptual support for the modest shared fragments than for treating a complete extraordinary narrative as established from the photograph.

## Agreement narrows what the image can support

The experiment points towards a useful hierarchy for evaluating Ramey-memo transcriptions. At the strongest end are candidate words repeatedly assigned to the same location by readers who have different expectations, especially when [blind readers]({{ 'blind-readers/' | relative_url }}) also identify them. Below those are readings such as “land”, which recur across more than one context but fail to survive the blind condition. Weaker still are words largely confined to the group whose suggested story makes those words meaningful.

This hierarchy resolves an apparent contradiction in the 2002 findings. The researchers concluded both that **some of the document appeared genuinely legible** and that interpretation was substantially influenced by suggestion. Those claims are compatible. A degraded photograph can contain islands of adequate information surrounded by regions in which the visual evidence is too weak to determine a unique word. Houran and Randle explicitly interpreted the same-location agreement as evidence that parts of the memo were readable, while warning that the meaning connecting those fragments remained ambiguous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The surviving fragments themselves demonstrate the problem. “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” do not by themselves tell the reader what kind of document Ramey was holding or what proposition it made about Roswell. Even accepting those readings provisionally leaves unanswered whether “story” approves, rejects or merely mentions a particular account, and what relationship “weather balloons” has to the rest of the sentence. Recovering individual lexical islands is not equivalent to recovering the message.

There is also an important limitation in the underlying dataset. When a referee requested per-condition counts for every reported word, the researchers discovered that the original score sheets had been discarded after responses were entered into the database. Consequently, detailed surviving counts were available for the words recorded as common across conditions, but not for a complete participant-by-participant reconstruction of every proposed reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That makes the reported cross-group findings useful but prevents a more exhaustive modern reanalysis of the original response sheets.

## The later laboratory test raises the bar further

Houran and Randle argued that the logical next step was not another interested reader producing a transcription. They proposed triangulation: at least three independent, blind laboratories should work from the best available scans using standardised enhancement procedures, then compare whether they independently identified the same words at the same precise locations. Such a design would turn the intuition behind cross-group agreement into a formal reliability test.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A later effort moved in that direction. As described in a 2022 review, Houran arranged for high-quality memo scans to be evaluated independently by three qualified research laboratories using different image-processing techniques. Nikolas Galatsanos's group used blind deconvolution; André Marion applied Fourier-based enhancement and related processing; Hong Yan used filtering, wavelet-based methods and deblurring. The laboratories did not produce a corroborated transcription: two reported no clearly interpretable text, while Marion did not attempt a reading and found that image noise remained a serious obstacle because it occurred on roughly the same spatial scale as the text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

That later result is important when interpreting the 2002 cross-group agreement. It does not erase the participants' repeated recognition of “Fort Worth TX”, “story” or “weather balloons”. It does, however, caution against turning those recognitions into a claim that the whole memo is technically recoverable. The 2022 review concluded that attempts to decipher the photographed document had still not yielded definitive results and reported that the independent laboratory effort left the memo effectively illegible despite modest image improvements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

## What counts as genuinely readable?

For the Ramey memo, the most defensible standard is therefore deliberately conservative. A proposed word becomes more credible when independent readers, unaware of the desired interpretation, repeatedly place it over the [same image]({{ 'same-image/' | relative_url }}) coordinates. Confidence should rise further if independent technical analyses reproduce the result and if tests on comparable photographs with known text demonstrate that the imaging process preserves enough information for words of that size to be recovered reliably.

Houran and Randle themselves recommended essentially this approach: independent blind laboratories, comparison of precise word locations and formal measurement of agreement. They also proposed photographing known period military messages with an equivalent camera at different distances and angles, creating a control in which investigators could test whether their enhancement and deciphering methods actually recovered text known in advance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

On the existing experimental evidence, “Fort Worth TX” is therefore a notably stronger candidate for genuine surviving text than a scenario-specific word such as “UFO” or “atomic”. “Story” and “weather balloons” also pass the basic cross-condition test, though their heavily uneven distribution across conditions warrants more caution. “Land” occupies a weaker middle category because it did not survive into the blind group's reported readings. The many other proposed words remain substantially more dependent on interpretation.

That is the real value of cross-group agreement. It does not decipher the Ramey memo, nor does it settle what happened at Roswell. It provides something narrower but methodologically useful: a way to ask **which proposed words remain visible when the reader's expected story changes**. The small number that survive that test are the sensible starting points for any reconstruction; the words that disappear when expectations change should not be granted the same evidential weight.
