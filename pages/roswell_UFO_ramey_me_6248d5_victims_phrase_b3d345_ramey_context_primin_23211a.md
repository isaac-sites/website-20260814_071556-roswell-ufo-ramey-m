## Introduction

The most important controlled test of “context priming” in attempts to read the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) found that expectations changed what people thought they could see. In 2002, James Houran and Kevin Randle randomly assigned 176 participants to three conditions: one was told the blurred document concerned the Roswell UFO crash and a possible cover-up, another was told it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing, and a third received no subject-matter clue. The groups examined the same underlying photographic material, yet produced different sets of words that tended to fit the context they had been given.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_victims_phrase_b3d345_ramey_context_primin_23211a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Priming Test illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_victims_phrase_b3d345_ramey_context_primin_23211a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_victims_phrase_b3d345_ramey_context_primin_23211a-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That result bears directly on the disputed reading “victims of the wreck”. The experiment did not prove what the memo really says, nor did it show that every proposed word is imaginary. Instead, it demonstrated a more specific problem: where the photographic information is weak enough to permit several interpretations, prior expectations can help determine which interpretation a reader settles upon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## How the three reader groups were tested

Houran and Randle designed their experiment after years of competing [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }})-memo [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) had produced markedly different results. Their concern was that investigators might not simply be extracting letters from the photograph; they might also be using their knowledge of Roswell to turn indistinct marks into plausible Roswell-related language. The study therefore manipulated one thing that earlier investigators could not easily remove from themselves: knowledge of what the document was supposedly about.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The researchers recruited 176 adults, aged 18 to 68, and randomly assigned them to three groups of almost identical size. The instructions created deliberately different expectations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Roswell condition, 59 participants:** readers were told that the military memo might contain information about the crash, retrieval and subsequent cover-up of an extraterrestrial craft near Roswell in July 1947.
* **Atomic-bomb condition, 58 participants:** readers were told that the military memo might contain information about secret atomic-bomb testing.
* **Blind condition, 59 participants:** readers were simply asked to read a photograph of a document, without being told what its subject might be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

All three groups worked from substantially the same visual evidence. Participants had an 8-by-10-inch enlargement of the memo area and access to four high-resolution images supplied on a CD-ROM, including a [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}), different enlargements and an older print made before further handling of the original negative. They were also given a magnifying glass. A research assistant who was blind to the experiment's hypotheses helped operate the computer images when necessary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Participants recorded, line by line, whatever individual characters or complete words they believed they could identify in the ten-line block. There was no imposed time limit. Afterwards, they completed measures that included tolerance of ambiguity and previous knowledge of Roswell and the Ramey memo. This matters because the experiment was not merely asking whether people could invent Roswell-related phrases; it was testing whether changing the proposed *context of exactly the same ambiguous image* changed the resulting readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The suggested context changed what readers reported

The clearest result was not a particular transcription but a difference in how much text the groups believed they could decipher. The Roswell group averaged 4.6 perceived words and the atomic-bomb group 4.8, compared with only 1.6 in the blind condition. The difference by suggestion condition was statistically significant, with the two context-fed groups producing significantly more words than the blind group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The time spent examining the memo moved in the same general direction. Roswell-primed participants worked for about 20 minutes on average, compared with roughly 16 minutes in the atomic condition and 14 minutes in the blind condition. Houran and Randle therefore recognised an important complication: an interesting [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) may have increased not only expectations but also motivation and persistence. The Roswell and atomic scenarios gave readers something meaningful to search for, whereas the control group merely had an indistinct old document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

More revealingly, however, the *kind* of words reported also changed with the suggested story. In the Roswell condition, the study's table lists distinctive readings including “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”. In the atomic-bomb condition, distinctive readings included “glasses”, “morning”, “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. The blind condition produced a much sparser set, including “flew”, alongside a few words that overlapped with the other groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That pattern is the central evidence for priming. Nothing about the pixels changed when participants were told that the photograph involved atomic testing rather than Roswell. What changed was the interpretative framework they brought to those pixels. Indistinct forms that could be organised into “crash” or “UFO” under one story could support entirely different associations when readers had been led towards atomic testing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The study also found that the number of words participants reported was associated with factors other than the experimental suggestion, including age and previous UFO or Roswell knowledge. Houran and Randle consequently treated memo reading as an interaction between an extremely degraded stimulus and characteristics of the person trying to interpret it, rather than as a straightforward photographic-recovery problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Some words survived the change of context

The experiment did not produce the simple result that *nothing* in the Ramey memo can be read. In fact, one of its most interesting findings cuts in the opposite direction.

Across all three conditions, participants independently converged on several words in corresponding locations. “Fort Worth TX” was reported by 52 participants overall, “story” by 61 and “weather balloons” by 27. “Land” also received relatively substantial agreement, although it appeared in the two primed groups rather than across all three conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The distribution is instructive. For “Fort Worth TX”, the paper reports 24 identifications in the Roswell group, 17 in the atomic group and 11 in the blind group. “Story” was reported by 43 Roswell participants, 11 atomic participants and seven blind participants. “Weather balloons” was reported by 20, five and two respectively. These were not simply different groups producing completely unrelated fantasies; some visual information apparently constrained readers towards the same words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Houran and Randle therefore drew a two-part conclusion. They considered the cross-group agreement evidence that at least portions of the document might genuinely be legible. At the same time, they argued that the broader meaning constructed around those relatively stable fragments remained vulnerable to suggestion because inter-reader agreement deteriorated as interpretations became more ambitious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This distinction is crucial. Priming does not mean that a viewer can see absolutely anything in an image. A blurred but partially preserved word can still provide enough letter information to constrain interpretation. The problem arises in the uncertain territory between recognisable text and irrecoverable detail, where contextual expectations can supply what the photograph itself does not clearly specify.

## What happened to “victims of the wreck”?

For the parent dispute over “victims of the wreck”, the experiment is especially significant because that phrase had already become one of the most consequential proposed readings of the memo. Earlier investigators had repeatedly supplied versions containing “VICTIMS OF THE WRECK”, while another reconstruction substituted a radically different phrase: “ARE THE REMAINS OF THE MATERIAL YOU COMMANDED WE FLY”. The published comparison shows that agreement on the supposedly decisive line was therefore never universal even among dedicated memo researchers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The experimental participants did **not** independently reproduce “victims of the wreck” as one of the study's cross-condition [consensus]({{ 'consensus/' | relative_url }}) readings. Even in the Roswell-primed condition, the distinctive words reported in Table 3 include “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”, but not “victims”. By contrast, the words surviving across conditions were much less dramatic: “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That does not demonstrate that “victims” is absent from the original document. The experiment was not a sufficiently powerful image-analysis procedure to establish the true transcription character by character, and participants spent far less time with the material than some specialist investigators. What it does establish is a serious evidential warning: recognition of a Roswell-compatible word cannot be treated as independent confirmation if the reader already knows what Roswell-related word is expected at that location.

This is particularly important once a proposed transcription has become famous. A person shown the disputed letter shapes *after* being told that previous investigators see “victims” is no longer performing a blind recognition task. They are effectively testing whether ambiguous marks can be made to fit an already supplied candidate. The Houran–Randle experiment shows why those two procedures — independently discovering a word and recognising a suggested word — should not be treated as equivalent evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Priming can work through both expectation and effort

The experiment suggests two mechanisms that are easy to conflate. The first is **interpretative expectation**: telling someone that a document concerns atomic testing makes concepts such as “atomic”, “flash” and “laboratory” more cognitively available, while a Roswell story makes “crash”, “UFO” and related terms more available. When the visual stimulus is incomplete, these expectations can influence how uncertain letter shapes are organised into words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The second mechanism is **motivation**. Participants given the Roswell scenario spent the longest time looking at the material, while blind participants spent the least. More searching creates more opportunities to find candidate letter combinations, so context may change both *what* people expect to find and *how hard* they look for it. Houran and Randle explicitly raised this interpretation themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A later review by Randle described ancillary analysis of the data as supporting this broader pattern: more engaging contexts were associated with longer examination, more perceived words and more condition-specific readings than readings shared across conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

This means the experiment should not be reduced to the slogan that “people see whatever they want”. Its result is subtler. Participants did show some agreement where the image apparently contained enough information to constrain perception. But adding a narrative context increased the amount of material people thought they could extract and shifted some of their interpretations towards words compatible with that narrative. The weaker the visual constraint, the more room there was for expectation to participate in the reading.

## What the experiment cannot establish

The study has genuine limitations. David Rudiak, a leading advocate of a Roswell-related reconstruction, criticised it on conceptual and statistical grounds, arguing among other things that participants spent too little time examining the material and lacked enough contextual knowledge to make a proper reconstruction. Randle's later review acknowledged these criticisms and also noted that Rudiak identified statistical typographical errors in the published paper, although Randle maintained that the corrections did not alter the reported conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

The motivation difference is another limitation. Because the Roswell, atomic and blind instructions varied in how interesting and informative they were, the experiment did not isolate semantic priming perfectly from differences in effort. The average examination times themselves show that the groups approached the task differently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Nor was the experiment designed to determine the memo's definitive wording. It used ordinary participants rather than three specialist archival-imaging laboratories, and its central question concerned bias in interpretation. Houran and Randle themselves did not conclude that further analysis was pointless. Instead, they recommended standardised [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) of the best available source images, followed by independent, blind examination by at least three laboratories specialising in archival-document reading and transcription. Agreement would need to occur on the same words in the same locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Their proposed safeguard goes directly to the priming problem. Analysts should not begin with the assumption that the document is about Roswell, an extraterrestrial crash, atomic testing or any other specific story and then search for words that make that story coherent. The authors explicitly warned that working backwards from an assumed historical context could produce words that were not actually present in the image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why the priming test changes the “victims” debate

The experiment leaves a useful hierarchy of evidence. A word independently identified at the same location by readers who have not been told what to expect is substantially more persuasive than a word recognised after its proposed identity has been supplied. The Houran–Randle data produced several candidates for the first category — most notably “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” — but “victims” did not achieve that status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That makes the study particularly relevant whenever “victims of the wreck” is presented as though the phrase simply emerges from sufficient magnification. The experiment shows that the Ramey image occupies an awkward middle ground: it appears to preserve enough information for some genuine agreement, yet not enough to prevent expectations from influencing more speculative readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The appropriate conclusion is therefore narrower than either extreme. The priming experiment does not prove that “victims of the wreck” is wrong, and it does not prove that the entire memo is unreadable. It demonstrates that **context is an experimentally observed confound in attempts to decipher this particular photograph**. Because the disputed “victims” reading lacks the kind of context-independent agreement found for several less sensational words, it cannot gain strong evidential weight merely because informed Roswell researchers can see it once they know where and what to look for. That is precisely the kind of recognition that a properly blinded test is needed to distinguish from information genuinely preserved in the photograph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
