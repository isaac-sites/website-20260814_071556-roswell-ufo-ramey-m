## Introduction

The roughly 27 GB of [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) imagery released in late 2015 achieved something important, but much narrower than its size suggested. It gave researchers outside the original scanning team access to a substantially richer collection of source images: different exposures, contrasts, filters and imaging approaches, including very large files derived from the original photographic [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) held by the University of Texas at Arlington (UTA). That made independent checking and experimentation much easier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-ramey-memo-scans.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Scans</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo ScansNovember 30, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 30, 2015</span></span></span>

What it did **not** deliver was a dependable transcription. Researchers who tried different software and [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) methods still could not agree on much of the disputed text, and the later technical account identified the fundamental problem as scale: the tiny letter forms were recorded close to the level of the film's grain structure. More pixels, more processing variants and more gigabytes could expose that limitation in greater detail, but they could not recreate letter information that the 1947 photograph had never recorded distinctly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/SUNlite8_1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOShedding some light on UFOlogy and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 4, 2025</span></span></span>

## How 27 GB accumulated

The size of the collection is easiest to understand as the product of **many imaging variants**, rather than as one extraordinarily detailed 27 GB photograph. The April 2015 work returned to the archival negative and recorded it in several ways. The subsequently published account of Simon Schollum's technical report describes microscope, microfiche and digital-camera imagery being processed into RAW and enhanced files, with high-dynamic-range (HDR) techniques and image stacks used to manipulate tonal information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span></span></span>

The processing had specific purposes. Image stacking and tonal manipulation were intended to reduce interference from [photographic grain]({{ 'grain-ceiling/' | relative_url }}), separate the tone of the paper from the darker writing and isolate tonal values that might make character shapes easier to distinguish. In other words, the researchers were not simply enlarging the same scan repeatedly. They were generating alternative representations in the hope that information difficult to distinguish in one treatment might become clearer in another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/667605767/UFO" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; PDF &#124; Unidentified Flying Object &#124; Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; PDF &#124; Unidentified Flying Object &#124; ScienceAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

By October, Kevin Randle reported that individual files could approach 250 MB. In November he described additional versions produced with different contrasts and filters. At the end of that month, when arrangements were made for wider distribution, he put the complete collection at about 27 GB and explicitly attributed its size to the variety of techniques, lighting conditions and filters used.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. A 27 GB collection sounds, at first, as though the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo had suddenly been captured with an enormous amount of new spatial detail. It would be more accurate to say that researchers had created a large **experimental image set around the same finite photographic record**. The collection offered many opportunities to interrogate that record, but the underlying information ceiling was still set when J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}) exposed the negative in 1947.

## The release turned a closed analysis into a reproducible test

One genuine achievement of the release was methodological. Before general distribution, possession of the largest new files was itself a bottleneck. Randle wrote in October that copies had been supplied to several people, including sceptical researcher Lance Moody, but that copyright restrictions and the sheer size of the files complicated wider publication. He was actively looking for somewhere capable of hosting them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

That problem was substantially overcome in November. Isaac Koi helped arrange online distribution, with assistance from Moody and his personal hosting resources. Randle then announced that the collection — approximately 27 GB — was available for outside examination. The scans were made available for non-commercial use under stated attribution conditions associated with UTA and the scanning team.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-ramey-memo-scans.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Scans</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo ScansNovember 30, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 30, 2015</span></span></span>

This changed what an outsider could do. Instead of judging a transcription from a small reproduction on a website, researchers could work with much larger files, compare alternative versions and apply their own enhancement procedures. It also made disagreement more informative. If different analysts working from substantially improved common source material continued to produce incompatible readings, the problem could no longer be attributed so easily to somebody having used a particularly poor internet copy.

The release therefore strengthened the **auditability** of Ramey memo research even though it did not strengthen any particular disputed transcription to the same degree. UTA later described its role as partnering with Kevin Randle, David Rudiak, Simon Schollum and Jerry Morelock to document and disseminate the Roswell photographs and research intended to determine the memo's contents. The library continued to describe the document as not definitively deciphered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span>

## Bigger files did not mean more recoverable letters

The central misconception exposed by the 2015 work is the difference between **digital resolution and original image information**. A very large scan can record an old negative with extraordinary fidelity. That does not guarantee that the negative itself contains a clean representation of every letter.

Schollum's examination was significant because it found no simple archival defect that could explain the failure. The negative was described as relatively well preserved; exposure was adequate; there was no evident camera or subject movement; and the memo lay within the camera's focus zone. The published technical account therefore identified scale as the principal difficulty. The dimensions of the type were extremely small relative to the distribution of the silver-halide grains forming the photographic image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span></span></span>

That is a harsher limitation than ordinary blur. If a clear letter has merely been scanned at insufficient resolution, rescanning can recover detail that was present in the source but omitted from the earlier digital file. If, however, a character was originally represented by only a marginal pattern among photographic grains, a higher-resolution scan mainly gives a more accurate picture of those grains.

The problem had been recognised before 2015. Sceptical analyst Tim Printy described the Ramey memo as a low signal-to-noise problem in which individual characters operated at or just above the image noise level. Such conditions make enhancement risky because grain structures and processing artefacts can resemble meaningful strokes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span> The 2015 archival work did not eliminate that concern; by showing that the negative itself was reasonably well focused and preserved, it arguably made the limitation clearer.

This is why the number of files could increase dramatically without a corresponding increase in certainty. Multiple contrast settings can make different weak structures conspicuous. HDR processing can expose tonal differences. Noise reduction can suppress distracting grain. None of those operations guarantees that the newly conspicuous shape corresponds uniquely to an original typed character.

The most revealing test came after the improved material escaped the immediate circle that had produced it. Members of the Roswell Slides Research Group received scans through Lance Moody and experimented with different software and methods. According to Tim Printy's contemporary account, the group still could not agree on much of what the document said.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/SUNlite8_1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOShedding some light on UFOlogy and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 4, 2025</span></span></span>

In that case, image processing of a difficult museum placard rapidly produced wording sufficiently clear for several researchers to recognise the same text. ## What could reasonably be claimed afterwards

The 27 GB release therefore supported stronger claims about the **quality and accessibility of the evidence base** than about the words on the memo.

Researchers could reasonably say that the 2015 campaign had gone back to the archival photographic source rather than relying solely on old enlargements or compressed copies. They could say that numerous imaging conditions and processing treatments had been tried, that the negative was not simply an unusable or badly deteriorated artefact, and that large working files had been opened to a wider range of analysts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-ramey-memo-scans.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Scans</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo ScansNovember 30, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 30, 2015</span></span></span>

They could also say that some previous practical objections had been weakened. A critic could no longer easily argue that the mystery persisted merely because nobody had bothered to rescan the source with modern equipment, or because researchers had kept the best material entirely to themselves. The April imaging campaign and November release directly addressed both problems.

What they could **not** responsibly infer from the 27 GB figure was that every disputed word had become recoverable. Nor did the existence of many enhanced variants make a phrase reliable simply because it appeared convincing in one of them. When the character signal sits close to grain noise, choosing among dozens of treatments creates another hazard: researchers can naturally favour the version in which an expected word looks clearest. The availability of the untreated or differently treated alternatives is therefore valuable precisely because it permits such apparent improvements to be checked rather than accepted at face value. The older signal-to-noise critiques and the lack of convergence among analysts make that caution particularly important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

This leaves an important distinction between **partial readability** and **dependable transcription**. Various researchers continued to argue that individual words or short phrases could be recognised, and some readings had attracted agreement before the 2015 campaign. But agreement on isolated fragments is not equivalent to recovering enough unambiguous characters to establish the disputed sentences. The new scans did not produce that higher level of consensus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

## The real result was a better-defined limit

Seen in that light, the 27 GB collection was not a pointless failure. Its most durable result was diagnostic. It shifted the question from **"Can somebody obtain a better scan?"** towards **"Does the original negative contain enough distinguishable character information for a unique reading?"**

The 2015 work provided better material for answering the second question, while simultaneously giving reasons for caution about the answer. The negative was sufficiently well preserved and focused to justify serious examination, yet the writing remained extraordinarily small relative to the photographic grain. Independent experimentation with the new files did not produce the convergence that would be expected from a genuinely decisive recovery of the text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span></span></span>

That is the central achievement of the huge scan release. Twenty-seven gigabytes bought researchers **more faithful access, more variants, more opportunities for independent testing and a clearer understanding of the technical bottleneck**. It did not buy 27 GB of new historical information. By the following year, even the researchers who had expected modern imaging to settle the matter acknowledged that no radical breakthrough had occurred, and the later published literature still characterised the memo as lacking a definitive decipherment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/04/ramey-memo-update.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Ramey Memo Update</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Ramey Memo UpdateApril 26, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 26, 2016</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo debate, that negative result is itself useful evidence. The 2015 release substantially reduced the plausibility that one more ordinary enlargement, contrast adjustment or high-resolution scan would automatically reveal a clean message. What remained was the harder problem the project had exposed: distinguishing genuine letter structure from photographic grain strongly enough that [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}), rather than merely individual interpreters, would consistently see the same words.
