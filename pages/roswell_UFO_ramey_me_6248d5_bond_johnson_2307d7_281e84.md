## Introduction

J. Bond Johnson matters to the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) story because he was not merely a later commentator: he was the young *Fort Worth Star-Telegram* reporter-photographer who entered Brigadier General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }})’s office on 8 July 1947 and photographed Ramey with the Roswell debris and the sheet of paper now called the Ramey memo. That core fact is supported independently of Johnson’s later memory. The photograph survives in the *Star-Telegram* collection at the University of Texas at Arlington, whose catalogue and publications identify the Roswell images as part of its archival holdings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span>

The difficulty begins when Johnson’s recollections decades later are used to supply details that the photograph itself does not establish. His accounts changed on questions such as how many exposures he carried, which photographs he took, what Ramey said about the debris and, most importantly for the memo, whether Johnson himself might have brought the paper into the office. Some later versions strengthened the apparent mystery; other statements undercut it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

That distinction is crucial. Johnson remains valuable as a participant who can explain how he came to be there. But where his retrospective testimony conflicts with, expands beyond or shifts around the contemporary record, the 1947 photograph and archival documentation deserve greater evidential weight.

## His visit to Ramey’s office

One of the more stable elements in Johnson’s account is how the assignment began. In an interview recorded by Roswell researcher Kevin Randle in March 1989, Johnson recalled returning to the *Star-Telegram* newsroom late on 8 July when his city editor asked whether he had his camera and sent him to Ramey’s office. Johnson remembered that a wire-service alert had reported that material connected with the Roswell [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) was being brought to Fort Worth. He then drove to the airfield with his 4-by-5-inch Speed Graphic camera.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

That broad account is compatible with the surviving historical framework. A modern *Star-Telegram* retrospective likewise identifies city editor Cullum Greene as the person who sent the 21-year-old Johnson to the base after news arrived concerning the Roswell material. It describes Johnson as a reporter who also carried his own Speed Graphic because the newspaper’s regular photographers were unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.star-telegram.com/opinion/bud-kennedy/article289713499.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: star-telegram.com">[Fort Worth Star-Telegram]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">star-telegram.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort Worth Star-Telegram‘Roswell Incident’ UFO crash in 1947 stirred Fort Worth &#124; Fort Worth Star-TelegramJuly 5, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2024</span></span></span>

There is therefore little reason to doubt Johnson’s basic role. He was at Fort Worth Army Air Field, he entered Ramey’s office, and he photographed the scene. The surviving image of Ramey holding the document is itself the strongest confirmation. UTA’s archived image metadata describes Ramey at Fort Worth Army Air Field on 8 July 1947, holding a telegram while inspecting debris associated with the Roswell incident, and credits the photograph to the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram* collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABrig_General_Ramey_Roswell_debris.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Brig General Ramey Roswell debris.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Brig General Ramey Roswell debris.jpg - Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

Johnson’s early recollection also contains an important detail about what he understood while he was there. He told Randle that he was briefed that the material was not a flying disc but a weather balloon. Randle further points to the contemporary *Star-Telegram* story, which reported that Ramey identified the material as a weather balloon and that a weather officer confirmed the identification. Whatever one makes of the military explanation itself, this is significant because it differs from later presentations in which Johnson appeared to remember Ramey as still baffled by the material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

By 1997, for example, a *Coast to Coast AM* discussion featuring Johnson and researcher Jaime Shandera presented a more dramatic version. The programme introduced Johnson as someone who had handled the debris and reported that Ramey had told him, in effect, that he did not know what it was. Johnson also discussed touching the material, although he said he had not subjected it to any kind of resilience test and had spent only a short time in the office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fight.fudgie.org/search/show/ab/episode/19970702_Wed" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fight.fudgie.org">[Fight]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fight.fudgie.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fight Art Bell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art Bell - Coast to Coast AM with Art Bell - Jamie Shandera - Roswell Crash - TranscriptJuly 2, 1997...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1997</span></span></span>

Those later remarks cannot simply be discarded, but they have to be read against the earlier recorded account and the 1947 reporting. The safest use of Johnson’s testimony is therefore to accept the part independently anchored by documentation — his assignment, presence and photographic role — while treating increasingly detailed dialogue or impressions with more caution.

## Where Johnson’s later recollections changed

The most consequential change concerns the sheet in Ramey’s hand. The entire importance of the “Ramey memo” depends partly on what kind of document it was. If it originated within military communications, its blurred text might preserve an internal account of Roswell. If, however, it was merely a wire-service bulletin or press copy brought in by a journalist, its evidential significance would be very different.

Johnson at one stage entertained precisely that second possibility. In a later interview conducted by Dennis Balthaser, he was asked about an earlier statement that the paper Ramey held might have been a press release or copy that Johnson himself had handed to the general. Johnson acknowledged that he had made such an earlier speculation: he thought he might have brought Ramey a copy of the Associated Press “flash” that his editor had given him. He then said that he had subsequently concluded that this speculation was wrong.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

That reversal matters more than it may initially appear. Johnson did not merely forget a minor feature of the room. He changed his position on the possible provenance of the very document whose wording later became the centre of the Ramey memo controversy. The photograph cannot settle that question: it shows Ramey holding a sheet, but not who brought it into the room. Johnson’s inconsistent recollections therefore cannot securely fill the gap.

The same pattern appears in his memories of the photographs themselves. In the 1989 interview quoted by Randle, Johnson remembered carrying one film holder containing two sheets of 4-by-5 film. Yet more photographs from the Fort Worth sequence were eventually identified. Randle later wrote that Johnson’s recollection of the number of film holders changed as additional images came to attention — from enough film for two exposures, to four or six, and later back towards a smaller number when Johnson disputed whether he had taken the [Marcel]({{ 'marcel/' | relative_url }}) photographs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

Randle, who began from a position broadly sympathetic to the Roswell mystery rather than as an outside debunker of the case, ultimately argued that the documentation made Johnson the likely photographer of the disputed [Marcel frames]({{ 'marcel-frames/' | relative_url }}) as well. His criticism was that Johnson appeared to adjust the remembered number of exposures and his authorship of particular photographs in response to what researchers had subsequently found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-timing-of-roswell-photographs.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

That does not establish deliberate fabrication. Ordinary autobiographical memory is reconstructive rather than a literal recording, and it can be altered by later discussion and information. Experimental research on eyewitness memory shows that post-event information can become incorporated into later recollection, while studies of “memory conformity” demonstrate that discussion with others can change what people subsequently report remembering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/ajacourtreview/382/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalcommons.unl.edu">[unl.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalcommons.unl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digital Commons Memory Conformity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Between Eyewitnesses...</span></span></span>

Johnson spent years discussing Roswell with investigators who themselves disagreed about what the photographs showed. By the 1990s he was participating publicly in debates about whether the debris was unusual and what the Ramey memo might mean. That history does not prove that any particular later memory was false. It does, however, give a strong methodological reason not to treat a vivid recollection from half a century later as equivalent to a record made on 8 July 1947.

## The memo’s provenance cannot rest on memory

The possibility that Johnson supplied the paper to Ramey illustrates the central evidential problem particularly well.

At one point, Johnson thought he might have carried the wire bulletin into Ramey’s office. Later he rejected that idea. Researchers consequently proposed several possible origins for the document: Johnson’s copy of the wire report, a military communication, a draft statement or some other office paper. Randle, despite regarding the memo as potentially important, has explicitly acknowledged that Johnson’s contradictory testimony leaves its source unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/ramey-memo-and-victims-of-wreck.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Ramey Memo and Victims of the Wreck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Ramey Memo and Victims of the Wreck</span></span></span>

The surviving photograph does not resolve provenance, but it sets firmer limits on what can responsibly be claimed. It proves that:

* Ramey was physically holding a document during the photographed Roswell debris session.
* The document contained several lines of typed or teletyped text.
* Johnson’s camera captured it inadvertently at sufficient scale for later researchers to attempt enlargements.
* The relevant [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) became part of the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram* photographic archive rather than appearing decades later without a chain of custody.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span>

It does **not** prove who authored the document, who handed it to Ramey, whether it was classified, or whether its blurred words contain any particular controversial phrase.

That distinction is useful because the memo’s history is sometimes narrated backwards from attempts to decipher it. Once researchers began proposing readings such as references to a “disc”, “wreck” or “victims”, the question of why Ramey possessed the sheet became much more consequential. Johnson’s later memories were then capable of increasing or decreasing the memo’s apparent importance depending on which version was accepted.

A more disciplined sequence runs in the opposite direction: establish first what the photograph and its provenance independently show, then use Johnson only where his recollection is consistent and corroborated. On that standard, his abandoned suggestion that he brought the sheet deserves to remain in the record as a genuine uncertainty rather than being erased simply because he later decided it was mistaken.

## The photograph is the more secure witness

The most durable evidence Johnson left is therefore not his oral testimony but his exposure of the shutter.

The original scene was recorded on large-format film in July 1947. UTA describes the Ramey memo image as part of the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram* collection, and contemporary attribution connects the Roswell photographs with Johnson. A 2024 *Star-Telegram* account continues to identify him as the reporter-photographer who covered the Fort Worth episode and notes that the Roswell photographs became among the most heavily requested material in UTA’s newspaper archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.star-telegram.com/opinion/bud-kennedy/article289713499.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: star-telegram.com">[Fort Worth Star-Telegram]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">star-telegram.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort Worth Star-Telegram‘Roswell Incident’ UFO crash in 1947 stirred Fort Worth &#124; Fort Worth Star-TelegramJuly 5, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2024</span></span></span>

That contemporary photographic chain has a major advantage over retrospective testimony: it does not change when researchers discover another frame, propose a new memo reading or ask a differently worded question. The physical limitations of the image are real — particularly the tiny size and poor resolution of the memo text — but those limitations are at least fixed and inspectable.

Researchers have worked from enlargements and scans derived from the archived photograph. In an experimental study of attempts to read the Ramey memo, James Houran and Kevin Randle described participants being shown multiple high-resolution scans and enlargements ultimately derived from the *Star-Telegram* photograph held at UTA. Their work emphasised how difficult it is to obtain an objective transcription from the ambiguous letter shapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on psu.edu.</span></span></span>

That problem is fundamentally different from Johnson’s memory problem. With the photograph, the uncertainty concerns optical information: what detail the film captured and whether blurred marks can be identified. With Johnson, the uncertainty concerns reconstruction across decades: which details genuinely survived from 1947 and which were modified by later information, interpretation or repeated retelling.

Neither source should be asked to do more than it can. Johnson can help reconstruct the human circumstances surrounding the photograph. The photograph can establish what was visibly present. Neither can, by itself, establish the full wording or origin of the memo.

## How much weight should Johnson’s memories carry?

Johnson’s testimony should be given **graduated**, rather than all-or-nothing, weight.

His recollections are strongest where they match external evidence: he worked for the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram*, was sent to Fort Worth Army Air Field on 8 July, entered Ramey’s office and photographed the Roswell display. Those facts are not dependent solely on memory and can safely form part of the historical reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.star-telegram.com/opinion/bud-kennedy/article289713499.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: star-telegram.com">[Fort Worth Star-Telegram]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">star-telegram.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort Worth Star-Telegram‘Roswell Incident’ UFO crash in 1947 stirred Fort Worth &#124; Fort Worth Star-TelegramJuly 5, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2024</span></span></span>

His testimony becomes weaker where later accounts diverge from his earlier ones or from contemporary reporting. Claims about precisely what Ramey said, how much Johnson handled the debris, how many film holders he carried, which frames he took and whether he supplied Ramey with the document all require qualification. The surviving record shows changes on several of those points rather than a single stable narrative preserved unchanged from 1947.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

This does not make Johnson useless as a witness. It makes him a normal retrospective witness whose testimony must be tested against contemporaneous evidence. Research on eyewitness memory provides good reason to expect later discussion and newly encountered information to influence recollection without requiring dishonesty on the witness’s part.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3630205/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, that leads to a clear hierarchy of evidence. The fact that Ramey held the document is secure because the camera recorded it. Johnson’s presence and role are strongly corroborated. His memories about the circumstances are useful where they remain consistent. But his changing recollections cannot establish what the memo was or where it came from.

The irony is that Johnson’s greatest contribution to the Roswell record is precisely the evidence least dependent on what he later remembered. Decades of interviews produced valuable context, disputes and corrections; the fraction of a second captured by his Speed Graphic produced the enduring primary source.
