## Introduction

Yes — a carefully designed period-camera experiment could answer one of the most important questions about the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}): **how much text the original photograph was physically capable of recording**. It could not tell us what Brigadier General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }})’s paper actually said, but it could establish whether disputed readings demand than a comparable 1947 photographic system can preserve.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_transcription_consen_5c6c25_period_camera_test_8f1681-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Camera Test illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_transcription_consen_5c6c25_period_camera_test_8f1681-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_transcription_consen_5c6c25_period_camera_test_8f1681-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

This is not merely a hypothetical suggestion. In their 2002 study of Ramey-memo interpretation, James Houran and Kevin Randle explicitly proposed photographing known period military messages with an identical camera, at different distances and angles, then testing how much of the known wording independent analysts could recover. They argued that such photographs could establish whether decipherment was possible in principle and provide controls for analysis of the real memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Done rigorously, that experiment could give the transcription debate something it has long lacked: a measured boundary between **information genuinely present in a photograph and information supplied by interpretation**.

## Recreating the photograph with known text

The attraction of a camera test is that the answer key exists before the experiment begins. With the Ramey photograph, researchers see indistinct marks and propose letters. With a reconstruction, investigators would begin with a completely known document, photograph it under controlled conditions, conceal its wording from the readers and measure exactly what survives.

There is a reasonable historical basis for attempting such a reconstruction. The surviving photograph is attributed to Fort Worth Star-Telegram reporter-photographer J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}), and the original [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) is held by the University of Texas at Arlington Special Collections. The university identifies the material as part of its Roswell photographic collection and provides dedicated high-resolution Ramey-memo material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English: Picture of Gen. Roger Ramey&#x27;s Memo, taken by James Bond Johnson Roswell crash message held in the hand...</span></span></span> Johnson was associated with a 4×5-inch Speed Graphic, a standard professional press-camera format of the period. Graflex documentation confirms that the Anniversary Speed Graphic was manufactured from 1940 to 1947, while the 4×5 version was the most common Speed Graphic size.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://graflex.org/speed-graphic/FAQ.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: graflex.org">[Graflex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">graflex.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Graflex Speed Graphic FAQ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Graflex Speed Graphic FAQ...</span></span></span>

That large negative matters. A 4×5 camera was not inherently a crude imaging device simply because it dates from the 1940s. Speed Graphics could carry lenses such as Kodak Ektars and other professional optics, and the large sheet-film format offered a substantial recording area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://graflex.org/speed-graphic/FAQ.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: graflex.org">[Graflex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">graflex.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Graflex Speed Graphic FAQ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Graflex Speed Graphic FAQ...</span></span></span> The real difficulty is that the memo occupies only a small, oblique portion of Johnson's photograph. The experiment therefore has to reproduce the **scale of the writing on the negative**, not merely use an old camera.

There is also uncertainty about some original exposure variables. A later technical discussion among Ramey researchers identifies the negatives as 4×5 Ansco Safety Film but describes the lens only as probably around 120 mm and says the film speed was unknown. That source is a researcher discussion rather than archival camera documentation, so those particulars should be treated as hypotheses to test rather than settled specifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey MemoOctober 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2015</span></span></span>

This uncertainty does not make an experiment useless. It means the reconstruction should be a **range test rather than a single theatrical re-enactment**. Researchers could use an appropriate Speed Graphic with several historically plausible lenses, films or sensitometric equivalents and exposure settings. Conditions that cannot be recovered with [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) should be deliberately varied.

The target document should likewise resemble the photographic problem rather than merely resemble a 1940s memo aesthetically. It should contain typewritten characters of known size and spacing, common and uncommon words, punctuation, numbers and deliberately similar letter combinations. Alongside realistic messages, the target could include neutral nonsense strings. Those strings would be particularly useful because a reader cannot reconstruct them from grammar or knowledge of Roswell.

## Distance and angle are the decisive variables

Houran and Randle's proposal specifically called for photographing period military messages at **various distances and various angles**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That is important because a demonstration that a Speed Graphic can photograph readable type at close range would prove almost nothing about Johnson's picture.

The reconstruction instead needs to match the original image geometry. Researchers could estimate the memo's dimensions and measure how large its visible region is relative to Ramey and other features in the original frame. A test document could then be positioned until it occupies the same proportion of a 4×5 negative. Its tilt, curvature and [folds]({{ 'folds/' | relative_url }}) should also be reproduced as closely as the photograph permits.

Several variables should then be changed systematically:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Camera-to-document distance:** move through a bracket around the best estimate rather than assuming one distance is exact.
* **Document angle:** reproduce the apparent obliquity and test modest deviations on either side.
* **Focus:** include both optimal focus and plausible small focusing errors from fast press work.
* **Aperture and shutter speed:** bracket historically plausible combinations, recording every setting.
* **Lighting and contrast:** reproduce directional indoor illumination and test how shadow, glare and paper tone affect fine type.
* **Processing:** compare carefully processed negatives with plausible press-oriented processing conditions rather than assuming laboratory-perfect film.
* **Position in the frame:** because optical performance can vary across an image field, the test message should occupy the corresponding part of the negative.

</div>

A proper experiment would also place resolution targets in at least some calibration exposures. Modern camera testing uses spatial-frequency measures such as modulation transfer function, or MTF, to quantify how effectively an imaging system retains contrast as details become finer. ISO 12233 is an established standard for measuring still-camera resolution, and current US federal digitisation guidance explicitly recognises it among image-quality standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.image-engineering.de/en/resources/image-quality/resolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: image-engineering.de">[Image Engineering]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">image-engineering.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Image Engineeringunderstanding image resolution, MTF and SFRA range of spatial frequencies needsto measure the modulation ratio in the ta...</span></span></span> A period-camera experiment need not pretend that a 1947 photograph was made under modern test-laboratory conditions; the point of calibration is to connect measured resolving performance with actual character recovery.

This separates two questions that are often blurred together. **Optical resolution** asks whether the camera-film system recorded enough spatial detail. **Text recognition** asks whether that detail preserves distinctive features of particular letters. A barely resolved vertical stroke may demonstrate that information exists without providing enough information to distinguish an E from an F, an R from a P or one tightly spaced word from another.

## Known wording exposes false readability

The most powerful part of the experiment would come after the photographs were developed and digitised. Analysts would receive the test images without being told the source wording.

That matters because the Ramey memo already has experimental evidence of context effects. Houran and Randle gave 176 participants versions of the decipherment task under Roswell, atomic-bomb and blind conditions. Participants given either suggested context reported significantly more words than the blind group: means of 4.6 words for the Roswell condition and 4.8 for the atomic-bomb condition, compared with 1.6 for the blind condition. The suggested context also changed the kinds of words participants perceived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

At the same time, some readings did recur across groups in identical positions. Their study reported agreement including “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”, leading the authors to conclude that parts of the document appeared genuinely legible even though its broader meaning remained highly susceptible to suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A reconstruction could distinguish those two phenomena much more cleanly because every proposed letter could be scored against ground truth. Suppose, for example, that independent readers reliably recover large or high-contrast words but begin producing mutually inconsistent readings once characters fall below a particular image size. That transition would provide an empirical **recoverability threshold**.

Researchers could then ask whether controversial Ramey-memo words fall above or below that threshold.

Even more revealing would be false positives. If blind analysts repeatedly report plausible English words from regions where the known target actually contains different wording or nonsense strings, the experiment would quantify how readily degraded photographic marks generate convincing but erroneous readings. That result would be directly relevant to the memo's disputed phrases without requiring researchers to decide in advance which Roswell interpretation is correct.

## The original negative must remain the benchmark

A reconstruction cannot replace examination of the historical negative. Modern federal guidance on forensic digital-image management requires original images to be preserved, processing to occur on verified working copies and processing steps to be documented sufficiently for another trained examiner to reproduce them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/document/osac-2024-n-0011-standard-guide-forensic-digital-image-management-version-10" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span> The same principles make sense here: archive the untouched scans, identify every transformation and distinguish information recorded in the source from features created or emphasised during processing.

This distinction is particularly important because higher pixel counts do not automatically recover more historical information. A scanner can sample film grain, scratches and processing irregularities at extremely high resolution without recovering letter detail that the original camera never resolved. Federal digitisation guidance similarly treats resolution, noise, dynamic range and other characteristics as separate image-quality properties rather than assuming that pixel dimensions alone determine fidelity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on digitizationguidelines.gov.</span></span></span>

The Ramey material itself illustrates why this matters. Houran and Randle worked with several photographic sources, including an enlarged print and four high-resolution versions derived from the Fort Worth Star-Telegram material. They nevertheless described the document as extremely difficult to read and noted uncertainty over claims that newer scans were superior.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> The University of Texas at Arlington has subsequently made high-resolution material available, but better digitisation cannot manufacture optical information that was absent from the negative at exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.libraries.uta.edu/roswell/ramey-memo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.libraries.uta.edu">[UTA Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.libraries.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">THE UNIVERSITY OF TEXAS AT ARLINGTON SPECIAL... The period-camera test therefore answers a different question from another round of digital enhancement. Enhancement asks, **“Can we display the surviving signal more effectively?”** Reconstruction asks, **“What signal should a photographic system under these conditions have been capable of recording in the first place?”**

Those questions reinforce each other, but they are not interchangeable.

## Using the test as a benchmark for disputed words

The experiment would become most useful if its results were converted into a pre-agreed standard rather than used merely to produce another set of visually impressive enlargements.

For example, test photographs could be sorted by how closely their document scale, angle, focus and measured resolution match the historical frame. Independent readers or specialist laboratories could then transcribe the controls using exactly the same enhancement workflow intended for the Ramey memo. Houran and Randle recommended at least three independent, blind laboratories using standardised enhancement and measuring agreement on particular words in precise locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The control photographs would reveal what confidence those laboratories actually deserve. A useful benchmark might distinguish three levels:

**Recoverable:** independent readers consistently identify the correct character sequence in matched control photographs.

**Partly recoverable:** readers agree on some character shapes or word length but cannot reliably recover the complete known word.

**Below demonstrated recovery:** readers produce inconsistent or incorrect words when presented with known text photographed at comparable detail.

A claimed Ramey reading should then be judged against the performance achieved on the controls. If analysts can reliably identify seven- or eight-letter control words whose characters occupy the same film area and suffer comparable blur, tilt and contrast loss, a disputed Ramey word of similar dimensions becomes more credible as an imaging proposition. It still requires independent agreement on what that word is.

If, however, analysts cannot correctly recover known words under equal or better conditions, claims of reading equally fine structures in the historical photograph would require much stronger justification. Digital sharpening could make shapes easier to inspect, but it could not by itself demonstrate that the shapes encode the proposed letters.

There is an especially useful intermediate outcome. The experiment might show that **word length and broad character structure survive while individual letters do not**. In that case, a responsible transcription could record something such as an eight-character group with several uncertain positions rather than promoting one complete English word. That would be genuine progress because it would convert subjective confidence into an experimentally justified limit.

## What the experiment could — and could not — settle

A successful reconstruction could substantially strengthen the [consensus]({{ 'consensus/' | relative_url }}) standards for the Ramey memo, but “settle” needs careful definition.

It **could** demonstrate whether 1940s press-camera technology, under conditions reasonably matching Johnson's photograph, was capable of preserving type at the relevant scale. It could identify which combinations of distance, angle, focus and processing destroy reliable character information. It could measure false-reading rates, test enhancement procedures against known answers and provide an objective difficulty scale for disputed portions of the historical image.

It **could not** recreate every uncontrolled detail of 8 July 1947. The precise lens, film response, focus error, exposure, processing chemistry, camera movement and three-dimensional shape of Ramey's paper may never be known with sufficient precision. A surviving vintage Speed Graphic is also not automatically optically identical to Johnson's individual camera. Ageing lenses, shutters and film substitutes introduce further differences.

Most importantly, a reconstruction cannot prove that a particular controversial phrase appears in the Ramey memo. Showing that comparable letters *could* have survived is not evidence that those letters *did* survive. That second step still requires blind, reproducible agreement on the historical image itself.

Houran and Randle made essentially this distinction when they proposed the experiment: known-text photographs could demonstrate decipherability **in principle** and serve as controls for independent laboratories examining the actual memo. They also cautioned that there was ultimately no substitute for the original document or a good verifiable copy, neither of which is available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That makes the camera test valuable precisely because its conclusion need not favour either side of the Roswell argument. If comparable photographs preserve far more type than sceptics expect, the case for continued controlled transcription becomes stronger. If they preserve only coarse word shapes while disputed readings depend on tiny, unstable strokes, confidence in those readings should fall. If the results occupy the middle ground — some words recoverable, others fundamentally ambiguous — that too would be a useful result.

For the Ramey memo, the most important contribution of a 1940s camera reconstruction would therefore not be another proposed transcription. It would be a **calibrated ceiling on what any transcription is entitled to claim**.
