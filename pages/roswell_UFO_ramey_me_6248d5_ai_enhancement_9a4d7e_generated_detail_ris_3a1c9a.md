## Introduction

Generative [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) can make a damaged or low-resolution historical photograph appear clearer, but it can also introduce details that were never recorded by the camera. This distinction is central to debates over AI-assisted restoration of historical images, including attempts to examine ambiguous photographs such as the Roswell [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}). A generated letter stroke, facial feature, texture, or object may look entirely natural because the system is designed to predict plausible visual patterns — not to recover missing evidence with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/limits-on-super-resolution-and-how-to-break-them-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.ri.cmu.edu">[Robotics Institute Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.ri.cmu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robotics Institute Publications Limits on Super-Resolution and How to Break Them</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robotics Institute PublicationsLimits on Super-Resolution and How to Break Them - Robotics Institute Carnegie Mellon University...</span></span></span>

Once a reconstruction looks like a genuine photograph, viewers may forget that they are seeing a prediction rather than an original record. ## Why generated details look convincing

### AI enhancement predicts, rather than retrieves

Traditional restoration methods generally aim to improve information already present in an image: increasing contrast, reducing noise or correcting damage. Generative enhancement works differently. Machine-learning models learn statistical relationships from large image datasets and use those patterns to estimate what missing areas might look like. When a photograph is too blurred or incomplete, the system may create a visually convincing solution because it has learned what similar objects, faces or letters usually look like.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/limits-on-super-resolution-and-how-to-break-them-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.ri.cmu.edu">[Robotics Institute Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.ri.cmu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robotics Institute Publications Limits on Super-Resolution and How to Break Them</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robotics Institute PublicationsLimits on Super-Resolution and How to Break Them - Robotics Institute Carnegie Mellon University...</span></span></span>

This makes the technology useful for presentation and exploration, but problematic for historical verification. A model that improves a photograph of an old document may produce sharper-looking characters, yet those characters are not necessarily the same characters that existed on the original paper. The software is solving a prediction problem, not recovering a lost recording. Research on super-resolution has long recognised that highly enlarged images can require “hallucinated” detail because multiple high-resolution images could plausibly match the same low-resolution source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/limits-on-super-resolution-and-how-to-break-them-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.ri.cmu.edu">[Robotics Institute Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.ri.cmu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robotics Institute Publications Limits on Super-Resolution and How to Break Them</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robotics Institute PublicationsLimits on Super-Resolution and How to Break Them - Robotics Institute Carnegie Mellon University...</span></span></span>

The danger increases when the missing information is meaningful text. A viewer may interpret a newly clear-looking letter as evidence because humans naturally treat readable writing as a strong factual signal. However, a generated character can be the model’s most likely guess rather than a preserved fragment of the historical object. Research on text super-resolution shows that recovering readable text from severely degraded images remains difficult because important character information may simply no longer exist in the input.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_iccv_2017/html/Xu_Learning_to_Super-Resolve_ICCV_2017_paper.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openaccess.thecvf.com">[CVF Open Access]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openaccess.thecvf.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Xu Learning to Super Resolve ICCV 2017 paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CVF Open AccessICCV 2017 Open Access Repository...</span></span></span>

## Examples of invented image features

### The Ramey memo and the problem of unreadable letters

The Roswell [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo illustrates why generative enhancement creates a particular challenge for historical photographs. The photograph contains a genuine 1947 image of a document, but the surviving view is limited by distance, angle, image quality and the physical condition of the photographed page. Attempts to interpret parts of the memo have produced disagreement over whether certain marks represent specific words or phrases.

Generative tools could make those marks appear more legible by producing realistic-looking letter shapes. However, the more successful the visual reconstruction appears, the greater the risk that observers confuse plausibility with proof. A generated “D”, “S” or other letter form may look like a recovered feature while actually representing one possible interpretation among several. In a historical dispute, that difference is decisive: an attractive reconstruction is not the same as an archival discovery.

### Faces, textures and missing objects

The same issue appears beyond documents. AI restoration systems can improve portraits by creating facial details, skin textures and sharp edges that match learned patterns from other images. Earlier research on face hallucination demonstrated that neural networks could generate realistic high-resolution faces from extremely small inputs, precisely because they use learned visual priors rather than relying only on the pixels available in the original image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2017/html/Yu_Hallucinating_Very_Low-Resolution_CVPR_2017_paper.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openaccess.thecvf.com">[CVF Open Access]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openaccess.thecvf.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yu Hallucinating Very Low Resolution CVPR 2017 paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CVF Open AccessCVPR 2017 Open Access Repository...</span></span></span>

For historical photographs, this can create subtle distortions. A person’s expression may appear clearer, clothing may gain texture, or damaged sections may be filled with plausible background detail. These changes can improve viewing experience, but they may also alter the historical record if users mistake them for authentic information.

The distinction between restoration and invention is therefore not always obvious. Removing dust, repairing scratches or improving contrast usually attempts to preserve existing evidence. Adding missing detail through a generative process introduces a new layer: an interpretation of what might have been there. The difference may be visually invisible to a casual viewer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/history-colourisation-controversy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Tubers are upscaling the past to 4K</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historians want them to stopOctober 1, 2020 — The article discusses the growing trend of digital upscaling and colorizing old film footag...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2020</span></span></span>

## Risks for historical evidence

### A convincing image can create false confidence

Historical photographs are often treated as direct evidence because they appear mechanical and objective. A camera captures light from a scene, giving photographs a special relationship with the past. Generative enhancement complicates that relationship by creating images that look photographic while containing predicted information.

This matters most in cases where photographs are used to answer disputed questions. An AI-enhanced image of an unclear document, object or person may enter online discussions detached from its editing history. Later viewers may encounter only the enhanced version and assume that the newly visible details were always present.

The problem is amplified by human perception. People are generally better at recognising patterns than at judging whether those patterns are genuinely supported by limited evidence. When a reconstructed image aligns with an existing belief or [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), viewers may be especially likely to accept it as confirmation.

### Archives need a clear boundary between restoration and reconstruction

For museums, archives and researchers, the key governance question is not whether generative tools should be banned. They can help audiences engage with fragile historical material and can support exploratory research. The issue is whether the output is labelled accurately.

A useful distinction is between:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Evidence-preserving enhancement:** changes intended to reveal existing information, such as improving contrast or reducing scanning defects.
* **Interpretive reconstruction:** changes that generate plausible details not directly visible in the source image.
* **Presentation versions:** visually improved images designed for public engagement rather than forensic analysis.

</div>

Keeping these categories separate helps prevent a generated image from being treated as a substitute for the original record.

### Provenance becomes part of historical analysis

As generative editing becomes easier, the history of an image file becomes increasingly important. [Provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}) systems such as Content Credentials, developed through the Coalition for Content Provenance and [Authenticity]({{ 'authenticity/' | relative_url }}) (C2PA), aim to record information about how digital media was created and edited. These systems can help provide context about an image’s origin and modification history, although they do not by themselves determine whether the content shown is historically true.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8912793-c2pa-and-synthid-in-openai-generated-images" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: help.openai.com">[OpenAI Help Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">help.openai.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openai.com.</span></span></span>

For historical photographs, provenance should accompany — not replace — critical examination. A file that records [AI enhancement]({{ 'ai-enhancement/' | relative_url }}) is easier to interpret than one that silently presents generated detail as if it were recovered evidence. The central principle remains simple: an enhanced image can help people see possibilities, but only the original photograph can establish what the camera actually captured.
