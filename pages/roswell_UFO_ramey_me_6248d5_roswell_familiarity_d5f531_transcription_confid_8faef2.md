## Introduction

Competing [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) expose a central problem in deciphering it: a reading can feel remarkably clear once its words form a coherent sentence, even though another careful reader can assign quite different words to the same blurred marks. Published comparisons do contain pockets of agreement — notably around “Fort Worth, Tex.” and “weather balloons” — but elsewhere proposed readings diverge sharply, sometimes changing not merely a word but the apparent meaning of an entire passage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_transcription_confid_8faef2-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Reading Disputes illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_transcription_confid_8faef2-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_transcription_confid_8faef2-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That pattern matters because confidence is not the same thing as [reproducibility]({{ 'reproducibility/' | relative_url }}). The strongest test of a transcription is not whether one investigator can make its sentences fit Roswell history, but whether [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}), preferably working blind, recover the same letter forms from the photograph. James Houran and Kevin Randle's experimental work found enough agreement to suggest that parts of the document may genuinely be legible, while simultaneously concluding that interpretation is strongly susceptible to suggestion and that earlier analysts showed weak inter-reader reliability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## How proposed readings diverge in the same regions

The most revealing evidence is the side-by-side comparison assembled in Houran and Randle's 2002 paper. It brought together readings attributed to Neil Morris, David Rudiak, Don Burleson, Tom Carey and John Kirby, including revised interpretations from some contributors. The comparison is valuable precisely because the investigators were not simply choosing between two complete rival documents. They often agreed on islands of text while producing strikingly different readings immediately around them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Consider the second line. Several investigators interpreted a central sequence as some version of “victims of the wreck”, with Rudiak, Burleson and Carey extending the passage towards wording about material or victims being “forwarded”. Kirby, however, proposed a substantially different reading: “the remains of the material you commanded we fly”. The disagreement therefore concerns the very portion that becomes most consequential in arguments that the memo refers to casualties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The contrast becomes even stronger farther down. Around a region where “disk” or “disc” was proposed, Rudiak reconstructed language about something being shipped, while Burleson and Carey proposed wording involving Los Alamos or an atomic laboratory. On the following line, Rudiak saw references including aircraft types, Wright Field and Roswell; Burleson instead read a statement about urgent powers and a site near Carlsbad, while Carey proposed urgent powers and a site north-west of Roswell. These are not minor spelling disagreements. They turn essentially the same photographic information into different places, actions and narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Line seven provides an especially useful control because agreement and disagreement coexist. “Weather balloons” appears across several transcriptions, but the surrounding words vary markedly: one interpretation describes something being “sent out”, another has an “only show” construction, and another reads the passage in terms of a mission or crew. Later discussions by Randle consequently described “weather balloons” and “Fort Worth, Texas” as nearly universal readings while acknowledging that other apparently obvious words lacked real consensus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This uneven distribution of agreement is more informative than either extreme claim that the memo has been “decoded” or that absolutely nothing can be read. Some character groups apparently carry enough photographic information to constrain multiple observers towards similar answers. Others do not.

## Why coherent sentences can create false confidence

A major trap begins after an investigator tentatively identifies a few words. Suppose “Fort Worth”, “disc”, “wreck”, “weather balloons” or another historically plausible term is accepted. Those words cease to function merely as individual visual identifications: together they begin suggesting what the whole message ought to be saying. Grammar supplies likely connecting words, Roswell history supplies likely people and places, and the reconstructed sentence can then be used to interpret the next ambiguous cluster.

That process can be useful. Reading degraded writing inevitably involves more than recognising isolated letters. Word length, grammar and surrounding text legitimately constrain possible readings. Rudiak, for example, has explicitly defended using grammar, spelling, word length and historical context alongside perceived letter forms when reconstructing the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/05/tony-bragalia-and-end-of-roswell-slides.html?showComment=1431274415716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Tony Bragalia and the End of the Roswell SlidesMay 10, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2015</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that the same method creates a feedback loop. Once a candidate sentence makes sense, uncertain marks can be interpreted in whatever way completes that sentence. The completed sentence then feels like additional confirmation that its component words were read correctly. Semantic coherence — “this sounds like what a military message about Roswell would say” — can quietly become mistaken for independent visual evidence.

The history of the memo contains a concrete warning. A [Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})-team transcription included such striking elements as “victims”, “crash”, “emergency powers”, “Magdalena” and “weather balloons”. Yet Rob Belyea of ProLab, examining high-resolution scans and considering character counts and letter combinations, specifically rejected the proposed “Magdalena” identification. Houran and Randle quote him as concluding that investigators were extracting readings that, in his assessment, were partly being made up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Russ Estes produced another instructive disagreement. In a region where other investigators proposed “disk” or another meaningful word, he reported seeing something resembling “ELA*” and acknowledged that it made no sense to him. That apparently unhelpful answer is methodologically important. A transcription that preserves meaningless or unresolved marks can sometimes be more faithful to weak visual evidence than one that resolves every uncertain cluster into a fluent historical narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

The persuasive power of coherence therefore needs to be separated from the quality of the underlying character identification. A complete sentence can be psychologically compelling while depending on several individually fragile decisions.

## What disagreement says about ambiguous letter forms

The Ramey photograph imposes unusually severe limits on letter recognition. The document occupies only a small part of the original photograph; its text is affected by distance, angle, grain, folds and other photographic degradation. Later scans can enlarge or process the surviving information, but enlargement cannot automatically restore character detail that the source image never captured clearly enough. Houran and Randle described the document as extremely difficult to read even with sophisticated technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

Under those conditions, an indistinct group of pixels may be compatible with several letter combinations. The competing transcriptions show the practical consequence. In one area investigators can converge on a place name; a few characters away, the alternatives may become different nouns, verbs or entire phrases. The important question is therefore not simply “Can this shape resemble an M?” but “How many other characters could reasonably produce the same surviving shape?”

That distinction explains why agreement carries more evidential weight when it occurs independently. Brad Sparks' early work is a useful example in the history of the decipherment. A 2022 review by Randle reports that Sparks initially identified “BALLOONS” in 1980 and later perceived “weather balloons”, “Fort Worth, Tex.” and “disc”; the review characterises those later elements as unanimously or almost unanimously accepted among investigators, although debates over the precise extent of consensus have continued.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span></span></span>

By contrast, phrases such as the proposed references to Magdalena, Carlsbad, Roswell, Wright Field, an atomic laboratory or particular transport aircraft vary substantially between readers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> The sensible confidence level for those regions should therefore be lower unless the actual character evidence can independently discriminate among the alternatives.

This also means that agreement should be measured at the smallest practical level. Two investigators can be described as agreeing that a passage concerns Roswell while disagreeing over half its words. Conversely, they might genuinely identify the same short phrase while constructing incompatible narratives around it. Treating both situations simply as “agreement” hides precisely the information needed to judge reliability.

## The experiment reproduced the confidence problem

Houran and Randle tested the interpretive problem rather than merely arguing about particular transcriptions. Their participants attempted to decipher the photographed document under different contextual instructions. The researchers then compared what participants reported seeing with previous proposed readings. Their conclusion was deliberately mixed: there was unexpectedly strong agreement on certain words in particular locations, suggesting that at least some real textual information survives, but the broader interpretation remained vulnerable to suggestion and cognitive differences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That result is important for understanding confidence. The experiment does not justify dismissing every proposed word as projection. If unrelated readers repeatedly recover the same word from the same position, the hypothesis that the word is genuinely represented gains support. But the study also found that contextual information affected how much text participants believed they could decipher, while prior UFO–Roswell exposure correlated with the number of words reported.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

In other words, confidence can increase faster than reliable information does. A reader who knows the historical [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) has a richer vocabulary of plausible completions: Roswell, wreck, disc, aircraft, military units, locations and press statements. Those possibilities may sometimes help identify genuine text. They also make it easier to turn weak character shapes into meaningful language.

The authors consequently argued for a much stricter protocol for further work: standardised enhancement of the best raw photographic data and analysis by at least three independent, blind laboratories specialising in reading and transcribing degraded documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span> That recommendation directly addresses the weakness revealed by the competing transcriptions. The aim is not simply to obtain another expert reading, but to discover which readings survive when investigators cannot influence one another and are not primed with a preferred Roswell narrative.

## Confidence should follow reproducibility

The competing Ramey-memo transcriptions suggest a practical hierarchy of evidence. At the strongest end are character sequences recovered independently by multiple readers in the same location and supported by recognisable letter forms. In the middle are plausible readings for which several investigators agree but contextual expectation could still be doing substantial work. At the weakest end are elaborate phrases that appear in one reconstruction while equally serious readers produce entirely different words from the same marks.

This hierarchy changes how seemingly dramatic phrases should be evaluated. “Victims of the wreck”, for example, matters enormously if those characters can genuinely be demonstrated; its importance to the Roswell debate does not itself make the reading stronger. The appropriate question is whether the photographic shapes constrain readers towards that wording independently of knowing what the phrase would imply. The published comparison shows considerably more convergence there than in some regions, but it also documents an alternative “remains of the material” reading and substantial variation in the surrounding text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The same caution applies in the opposite direction. “Weather balloons” is comparatively well supported as a reading, but the phrase alone does not settle what the memo says about them. Competing versions place different language around those words, so moving from “these characters probably say WEATHER BALLOONS” to “therefore the memo describes a cover story” requires an additional interpretive step.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is the larger lesson of the transcription dispute. The photograph appears to contain genuine pockets of recoverable text surrounded by much weaker information. Those pockets can anchor interpretation, but they can also make neighbouring guesses feel more certain by turning fragments into a story. The sharp disagreements among experienced Ramey-memo readers are therefore not a peripheral controversy. They are evidence about the limits of the document itself.

A convincing future decipherment would need to reverse the usual logic: establish characters first, establish independently reproducible words next, and construct sentences only afterwards. Until substantially different readers can reproduce the disputed wording without being led towards it, the fluency of a proposed transcription is better treated as a hypothesis about the memo than as proof that its blurred letters have actually been read.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>
