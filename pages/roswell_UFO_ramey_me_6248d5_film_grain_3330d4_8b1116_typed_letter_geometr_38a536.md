## Introduction

The most useful way to distinguish genuine typewritten strokes from [photographic grain]({{ 'grain-ceiling/' | relative_url }}) in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) is to ask whether the marks form **coherent character geometry**. A real typed letter is not merely a collection of dark patches. Its stem, bowl, crossbar, diagonal or enclosed space must occur in mutually compatible positions, and neighbouring characters should normally share a plausible baseline and spacing rhythm. Grain and other image noise can imitate one or two of those features by chance; it has much greater difficulty reproducing an organised sequence of them.

That distinction matters because the memo was not photographed as a document. It appears incidentally in a 1947 Fort Worth Star-Telegram photograph of Brigadier General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}), and the US Air Force's own later attempt to extract the text concluded that the available photographs were of insufficient quality to visualise the sought detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span> A 2002 controlled decipherment experiment likewise found substantial [ambiguity]({{ 'ambiguity/' | relative_url }}) and susceptibility to suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The strongest Ramey memo reading, therefore, should not be the one that merely produces recognisable words after enlargement. It should be the reading in which the visible marks behave most consistently like physical impressions of repeated typewritten characters.

## A letter is a structure, not a handful of pixels

Character recognition depends heavily on relationships between marks. Consider the capital letters **E**, **P**, **R** and **O**. An E requires a vertical stem and horizontal strokes in characteristic positions. A P needs a stem plus an upper bowl. An R adds a lower diagonal to broadly similar upper geometry. An O depends on a substantially closed curved boundary surrounding an internal light region.

In a clean document these distinctions are obvious. In a severely degraded image, however, parts of the same strokes can disappear while unrelated dark marks appear nearby. Research on degraded-document processing consequently treats preservation of **stroke connectivity** as an explicit technical objective. One published method for broken and degraded documents uses post-processing specifically to de-speckle images while preserving stroke connectivity and improving text regions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231216316022" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Broken and degraded document images binarization</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Broken and degraded document images binarization - ScienceDirectMay 10, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2017</span></span></span> Another study of faint characters describes the difficulty of separating foreground from background noise while simultaneously maintaining proper connectivity of character strokes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1047320315001236" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Adaptive–interpolative binarization with stroke preservation for restoration of faint characters in degraded documents - Sci...</span></span></span>

That is directly relevant to the Ramey memo. Imagine that four dark patches appear where somebody proposes an R. The important question is not whether those four patches *can be imagined* as an R. It is whether their geometry provides independent evidence for an R: does a substantially vertical feature occupy the stem position? Does darker information form an upper enclosure or curve at the appropriate height? Is there evidence for the diagonal leg, and does it originate where an R's leg should originate?

If those relationships survive inspection, the identification has structural support. If the proposed R exists only after the observer mentally joins three unrelated specks, assumes a missing bowl and treats another blob as a diagonal, the photograph is supporting much less of the character than the finished transcription suggests.

This creates a useful evidential distinction between **damaged geometry** and **invented geometry**. A real letter may certainly have missing sections because the source is degraded. The problem begins when so much has to be reconstructed that several different letters could be produced from the same collection of marks.

## Continuity separates strokes from convenient specks

A genuine typewritten stroke need not appear as a perfectly solid black line. Typewriter ribbons, uneven impacts, focus, photographic exposure and later reproduction can all change its tonal appearance. But the underlying mark has direction and extent. A vertical stem should produce evidence concentrated along a roughly vertical path; a crossbar should extend laterally; a bowl should show appropriately distributed curvature.

Random photographic structure does not possess that obligation.

This makes **continuity** one of the strongest tests available at character scale. Suppose an alleged vertical stem consists of five dark regions. If their centres remain in roughly the same horizontal position through most of the letter's height, they collectively support a vertical stroke even if small gaps intervene. If instead they zigzag sideways and only resemble a stem when the observer draws an imaginary line through them, the evidence is weaker.

The same principle becomes more discriminating with bowls. An alleged O, P, B, D or R requires dark information to occur around a meaningful curved path. A light patch surrounded by unrelated grain is not automatically a counter — the enclosed or partly enclosed light area inside a letter. The surrounding darkness must itself behave like the boundary of the proposed glyph.

This is why de-speckling and connectivity are paired problems in document-image research. Removing every isolated dark component risks deleting real punctuation or fragments of damaged strokes; retaining every component risks treating background noise as text. Research on degraded historical documents describes faint text, background variations and multiple kinds of noise as major obstacles to reliable character recognition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6820306/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Effective and fast binarization method for combined degradation on ancient documents - PMCOctober 22, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 22, 2019</span></span></span> NIST work on document-image coding similarly notes that degraded documents commonly contain both connected and broken characters, with corruption near character edges capable of producing touching or broken forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=150716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tsapps.nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tsapps.nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Document Image Coding for Processing and Retrieval</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Document Image Coding for Processing and RetrievalJanuary 23, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 23, 2009</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, therefore, a good reading should minimise what might be called **discretionary joining**: connections that exist primarily because the proposed word tells the observer where a line ought to be.

## Enlargement can make a weak skeleton look complete

This problem becomes less intuitive once the memo is enlarged dramatically on a modern display. A character that occupied a tiny region of the original photograph may become centimetres tall on screen. Its grain clusters and tonal variations then look like substantial visual objects.

But display size and captured detail are different quantities.

The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative defines resolution in terms of an imaging system's ability to resolve finely spaced detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/term.php?term=resolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesResolution - Glossary - Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines Initiative...</span></span></span> Its technical guidance also cautions that spatial resolution — the number and spacing of pixels — does not by itself guarantee the quality of the information represented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/FADGI_Still_Image-Tech_Guidelines_2010-08-24.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FADGI Still Image Tech Guidelines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesAugust 2010March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span> NIST defines resampling as changing image size or resolution by adding or subtracting pixels through interpolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/40251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">glossary term</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Resampling &#124; NISTJanuary 15, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 15, 2025</span></span></span>

Consequently, turning a tiny ambiguous Ramey memo glyph into a 100-pixel-high image does not mean that the 1947 photograph recorded 100 independent observations through the height of that glyph. Interpolation can make boundaries smoother and easier to inspect, but it cannot retrospectively make the camera resolve details that never reached the photographic record.

That distinction is crucial when evaluating letter skeletons. Enlarged grain can produce apparent miniature strokes. Contrast adjustment can make several nearby dark regions appear more unified. Sharpening can emphasise edges. Thresholding can convert a vague grey patch into a hard black component. Each operation may improve visibility, but it can also change which fragments appear connected.

A claimed letter is therefore stronger if its essential geometry remains recognisable across several reasonable presentations of the source — for example, modestly different contrast or threshold settings — rather than appearing only under one highly selective treatment.

The broader OCR literature demonstrates how consequential image quality can be. A NIST experiment using machine-printed documents found character-recognition error rates ranging from about 1 per cent to as high as 74 per cent as print and image quality deteriorated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/publications/impact-image-quality-machine-print-optical-character-recognition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">impact image quality machine print optical character recognition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">impact image quality machine print optical character recognition</span></span></span> The numbers cannot simply be transferred to the Ramey photograph, which is a very different imaging problem, but the result illustrates the underlying point: recognisable-looking text and reliable character identification cease to be equivalent as image quality declines.

## Baseline and spacing give each letter a second test

The geometry of a typed character does not end at its outer edge. A typewriter imposes structure on the entire line.

Forensic examination of typewritten documents traditionally considers horizontal and vertical spacing, type style, character defects and alignment. FBI guidance summarised by the US Office of Justice Programs identifies three broad examination areas: size and spacing, type style, and unique characteristics such as character and alignment defects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/examination-typewritten-document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span> A separate forensic treatment similarly identifies character design, horizontal and vertical spacing, letter height, lateral or vertical misalignment and ribbon characteristics as informative properties of typescript.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/identification-typewritten-documents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

These principles provide unusually useful checks on proposed Ramey memo characters because they use information outside the individual glyph.

If a supposed word contains six characters, their probable lower edges should collectively suggest a baseline. Individual impressions may ride slightly high or low, but a reading that requires alternate letters to jump dramatically above and below the apparent line is geometrically expensive. It asks the typewriter to behave irregularly exactly where the proposed transcription needs irregularity.

Spacing offers another constraint. Mechanical typewriting produces a repeating horizontal advance determined by the machine. For ordinary monospaced typewriting, narrow letters such as I occupy essentially the same advance as wide letters such as M. Even machines capable of proportional spacing still operate according to constrained mechanical increments rather than placing each character arbitrarily. Historical typewriting can therefore be examined through recurring horizontal positions as well as glyph shape. The importance of spacing in forensic typewriter identification is well established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/examination-typewritten-document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, an analyst can therefore estimate probable character centres before deciding what the word says. If apparent vertical strokes or dark concentrations recur at approximately regular intervals, those positions provide candidate character cells. A proposed transcription should fit them.

This reverses a common but risky procedure. Instead of deciding that a cluster “looks like” a desired word and then assigning its dark marks to letters, the analyst first asks where the physical typing positions appear to be. Only then should candidate letters be tested against the information inside each position.

## Repeated letters provide an internal control

One of the most valuable geometric tests does not require knowing the memo's wording at all: compare proposed instances of the same character.

A typewriter repeatedly strikes the same physical type element for a given key. The resulting impressions will vary because of ribbon condition, paper contact, impact and photography, but their basic geometry should remain related. Indeed, forensic typewriter examination relies partly on type style and recurring individual defects for precisely this reason.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/examination-typewritten-document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

That creates an internal consistency test for the Ramey memo. If an analyst labels one ambiguous form as an E, another proposed E elsewhere should share at least its principal geometric organisation: the stem should occupy the corresponding side, crossbars should occur at comparable heights, and the overall proportions should not require a fundamentally different glyph.

This does not demand pixel-for-pixel identity. That would be unrealistic in such a degraded photograph. What matters is whether the same explanatory model works repeatedly.

The test becomes particularly powerful when a proposed transcription contains several occurrences of common letters. If one supposed R is identified from a strong vertical stem and recognisable upper bowl, but another is accepted despite lacking both because the surrounding word requires R, the transcription is applying inconsistent evidential standards.

Grain has no memory. It can produce an accidental E-like combination in one location and an entirely different E-like combination elsewhere. A real typewriter, by contrast, repeatedly imposes a related character geometry. Repetition therefore offers a way to distinguish isolated resemblance from systematic textual structure.

## The Ramey experiment shows why geometry must come before vocabulary

The need for structural tests is not hypothetical. James Houran and Kevin Randle's 2002 study directly investigated interpretive bias in attempts to read the Ramey memo. They recruited 176 participants and divided them among three conditions: 59 were told the document concerned the Roswell UFO case, 58 were told it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing, and 59 were given no suggestion about its contents. Participants examined an enlarged print and several high-resolution images derived from Fort Worth Star-Telegram photographic material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The experiment is important here not because it settles the memo's wording — it does not — but because it demonstrates why semantic expectations cannot substitute for letter geometry. The researchers explicitly investigated whether ambiguous visual material was being interpreted according to prior expectations, and the different suggestion conditions produced evidence of such confounding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This is exactly where isolated specks become dangerous. Once an observer expects a word such as “disc”, “victims”, “balloon” or “Roswell”, the vocabulary supplies a ready-made template. A few dark patches can be assigned to the required stems and curves, while absent features are treated as photographic loss.

The proper order of inference should run in the opposite direction:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. establish probable character positions from spacing and line geometry;
2. map the visible dark information within each position;
3. identify continuous or plausibly connected strokes;
4. compare those structures with candidate glyphs and repeated characters elsewhere;
5. only then consider which resulting sequences make linguistic sense.

</div>

This order cannot eliminate interpretation, but it makes the interpretation accountable to the photograph. A candidate word that is semantically attractive but geometrically poor should lose to a less exciting reading with stronger visible structure.

## Isolated specks are especially weak evidence

The weakest possible character skeleton is one constructed from individually plausible but mutually unconstrained fragments.

Three dark spots might be interpreted as the ends of an E's horizontal bars. Add another nearby patch and an observer may perceive its stem. Yet if those components do not connect, align or occupy appropriate proportions, the proposed E is largely a product of perceptual completion.

The problem multiplies across a word. Suppose five alleged letters each require two speculative connections. The finished word may appear impressively coherent because language itself links the five guesses together. But geometrically, the interpretation rests on ten separate acts of completion.

That is why connected structure should carry more weight than raw dark-pixel count. A long, approximately straight feature spanning most of a character's height is unlikely to be equivalent evidence to four unrelated spots merely because both contain the same total dark area. The first has orientation and continuity; the second does not.

This principle is reflected in technical document restoration. Algorithms designed for broken documents explicitly attempt to distinguish noise from foreground while preserving connected character strokes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231216316022" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Broken and degraded document images binarization</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Broken and degraded document images binarization - ScienceDirectMay 10, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2017</span></span></span> NIST's work on degraded document patterns likewise describes ambiguous prototypes created by connected and broken characters and notes that edge degradation can cause characters to touch or fragment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=150716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tsapps.nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tsapps.nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Document Image Coding for Processing and Retrieval</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Document Image Coding for Processing and RetrievalJanuary 23, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 23, 2009</span></span></span>

The lesson for human inspection of the Ramey memo is straightforward: **a speck should not acquire evidential value merely because a proposed transcription gives it a job**.

## A stronger reading must satisfy several constraints at once

No single geometric test can authenticate a Ramey memo character. Genuine strokes can break. Grain can accidentally align. Perspective can alter apparent dimensions. Uneven exposure can suppress one side of a glyph. A single perfect-looking letter could therefore still be accidental.

The stronger criterion is convergence.

A persuasive candidate should satisfy several substantially independent constraints at once. Its defining strokes should occur in the right relative positions. Those strokes should show as much continuity as the source quality permits. Its enclosed spaces should correspond to actual surrounding structure rather than arbitrary light patches. The character should sit plausibly on the local baseline. Its centre should agree with the spacing rhythm of neighbouring characters. Other proposed instances of the same letter should exhibit compatible geometry. Finally, the identification should not collapse under modest changes in image presentation.

The historical record warrants that caution. The Air Force's 1994 investigation obtained first-generation Fort Worth Star-Telegram prints and negatives from the University of Texas at Arlington and sent the relevant photograph for digitisation and photo interpretation. The organisation reported on 20 July 1994 that the image remained insufficient to visualise the memo detail sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span> Later private investigators disputed how much could nevertheless be extracted from improved scans, while the 2002 Houran-Randle experiment showed that observers could reach some local agreements but that decipherment was vulnerable to contextual suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those findings are not proof that the memo is unreadable in every part. Nor do they prove that every proposed transcription is wrong. They establish something narrower and more useful: the image lies in a regime where **seeing letter-like marks is easier than demonstrating reliable letters**.

## What should count as convincing character geometry?

For the Ramey memo, [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) should rise in stages rather than jump from “dark mark” to “identified word”.

A single isolated blob provides little evidence beyond the existence of local darkness. Two aligned fragments may suggest a stroke. Several fragments following a coherent vertical, horizontal or curved trajectory provide more. Multiple correctly related strokes can support a candidate character. Several such characters occupying regular positions on a common baseline provide stronger evidence for a word. Repeated occurrences of compatible glyphs elsewhere strengthen the case further.

The reverse pattern should reduce confidence. A proposed letter is weak when its important strokes appear only after aggressive processing; when dark components must be connected across arbitrary gaps; when expected components are absent while contradictory ones are ignored; when its position breaks the local baseline or character rhythm; or when another supposed instance of the same letter requires an entirely different visual definition.

This is a stricter standard than asking whether a transcription “looks right”, but the Ramey memo requires a strict standard precisely because the potential historical implications make meaningful words tempting. NIST's imaging work provides the underlying caution: more pixels or greater apparent precision do not automatically guarantee greater accuracy, and image degradation can dramatically affect machine-print recognition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/publications/precision-and-accuracy-scientific-imaging" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">precision and accuracy scientific imaging</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">precision and accuracy scientific imaging</span></span></span>

Character geometry therefore offers a disciplined middle ground between two overstatements: that nothing whatsoever can be learned from the photographed memo, and that any plausible-looking enlarged phrase constitutes recovered text. Some regions may preserve enough structure for cautious character-level conclusions; others may not.

The decisive question is not whether dark marks can be arranged into a familiar word. It is whether the proposed letters behave like **repeated physical products of a typewriter**. Real strokes should exhibit organised direction, connectivity and proportion. Real characters should occupy plausible positions relative to a baseline and their neighbours. Repeated letters should share recognisable structural features. Grain can imitate any one of these properties locally, but it has no systematic reason to reproduce all of them together.

That is why character geometry is one of the strongest available safeguards in reading the Ramey memo. It forces an interpretation to earn its letters one stroke at a time, rather than allowing an expected sentence to turn photographic specks into typography.

    var joiner = url.indexOf('?') >= 0 ? '&' : '?';
    var params = [];
    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
