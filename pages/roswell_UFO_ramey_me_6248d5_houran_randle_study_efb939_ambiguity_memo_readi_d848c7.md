## Introduction

In the Houran–Randle experiment on the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), **tolerance of ambiguity was associated with how many words participants said they could decipher from the degraded photograph**. That matters because the experiment was not merely testing whether a Roswell-themed prompt could steer a reading. It also suggested that different observers approached the same uncertain visual material differently. The published correlation table reported strong relationships between the number of words deciphered and the study's high- and low-tolerance classifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The important caution is that **reporting more words is not the same thing as reading the memo more accurately**. Houran and Randle found some cross-group agreement on particular words, but they also found readings that followed the suggested Roswell or atomic-bomb context. Their individual-difference result therefore raises a useful methodological question: when someone sees a great deal of text in an extremely unclear image, is that evidence of superior perception, or evidence of a greater willingness to turn uncertain marks into words? The experiment could establish an association with reported readability; it could not by itself establish which observers were actually correct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What tolerance of ambiguity meant in the experiment

Houran and Randle measured tolerance of ambiguity after participants had attempted to decipher the photograph. They used Lange and Houran's Rasch-model adaptation of MacDonald's AT-20 ambiguity-tolerance measure. The study describes it as a yes/no questionnaire in which higher scores indicated greater tolerance for ambiguity. Its items dealt with reactions to problems lacking obvious solutions and to vague or impressionistic material — characteristics plainly relevant to the task of staring at badly degraded writing and deciding whether recognisable words are present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The underlying AT-20 was developed by A. P. MacDonald in 1970 from an earlier 16-item instrument. MacDonald reported that expanding it to 20 items improved split-half reliability and cross-validated the measure on 789 undergraduates; he also found associations with measures of rigidity, dogmatism and performance on a complex task.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.2466/pr0.1970.26.3.791" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sage Journals Revised Scale for Ambiguity Tolerance: Reliability and Validity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsRevised Scale for Ambiguity Tolerance: Reliability and Validity - A. P. Mac Donald, 1970June 1, 1970...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 1970</span></span></span> Later reviews have nevertheless stressed that “tolerance of ambiguity” has never been a perfectly uniform psychological construct: different scales can capture reactions to novelty, complexity, uncertainty, contradiction and problems that resist solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/psychology/tolerance-of-ambiguity" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Tolerance of Ambiguity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tolerance of Ambiguity - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

That distinction is important for understanding the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) experiment. The measure was **not an eyesight test, a photographic-analysis test or a direct measure of transcription skill**. It was intended to characterise a person's response to ambiguity. A high score therefore should not be translated into “better at reading blurred documents”, just as a low score does not mean “poor eyesight”. The researchers were examining cognitive style as one possible influence on what observers reported seeing.

There was also a specific reason for including such a measure in a UFO experiment. Houran had previously reported lower ambiguity tolerance among self-described UFO percipients than among controls, in research framed around the possibility that conventional but ambiguous aerial phenomena could sometimes be misinterpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/pms.1997.85.3.973" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sage Journals Tolerance of Ambiguity and the Perception of UFOS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsTolerance of Ambiguity and the Perception of UFOS - James Houran, 1997December 1, 1997...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 1997</span></span></span> Earlier work by Houran and Rense Lange had likewise examined ambiguity tolerance in relation to paranormal experiences and beliefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/pms.1996.83.2.365" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sage Journals Tolerance of Ambiguity and Fear of the Paranormal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsTolerance of Ambiguity and Fear of the Paranormal - James Houran, Rense Lange, 1996...</span></span></span> The Ramey study effectively brought that individual-differences question into a controlled visual-decipherment task.

## Who reported seeing more words?

The experiment's correlation table is striking, but it needs careful reading. Houran and Randle divided ambiguity scores into classifications, listing “tolerance of ambiguity” for scores above 30 and “intolerance of ambiguity” for scores below 20. They reported Spearman correlations of **.66** between the high-tolerance classification and number of words deciphered, and **.78** between the low-tolerance classification and number of words deciphered, both marked significant at *p*<.01. The same table reported a smaller correlation of **.35** between participants' UFO–[Roswell knowledge]({{ 'knowledge-effect/' | relative_url }})/exposure measure and the number of words deciphered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

At first sight, the ambiguity result looks paradoxical: both ends of the scale appear strongly associated with producing more words. But the authors had deliberately separated the extremes rather than treating ambiguity tolerance simply as one continuous predictor. Their methods explain that they dichotomised scores because previous work had led them to think that both high and low levels might be relevant to extraordinary beliefs or magical thinking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> The safest interpretation is therefore the narrow one: **extreme ambiguity-response styles were associated with the amount of material participants reported deciphering**. The table does not justify a simple claim that “the more ambiguity-tolerant someone was, the better they read the memo”.

Nor should the correlation coefficients be treated as accuracy scores. The dependent measure was the **number of words deciphered**, not the number independently proved correct. That distinction becomes particularly important because the experiment simultaneously demonstrated a large contextual effect. Participants given a Roswell or atomic-bomb [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) reported an average of roughly 4.6 and 4.8 words respectively, whereas the blind group averaged only about 1.6.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

In other words, conditions that encouraged people to extract more apparent language did not necessarily give them more genuine visual information. The photograph remained the same. What changed were the observer, the interpretive context and apparently the willingness or motivation to convert indistinct patterns into candidate words.

## More perceived text did not mean more legible text

This is the central risk in applying the experiment to claims about the Ramey memo. Suppose one observer confidently produces ten words while another offers only two. It is tempting to regard the first observer as more successful. But that conclusion follows only if the additional eight words can be independently validated.

Houran and Randle's manipulation shows why that assumption is unsafe. Participants primed with Roswell-related information produced readings such as “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”, while participants given an atomic-bomb frame generated terms such as “atomic”, “flash”, “glasses” and “laboratory”. The researchers therefore had direct evidence that plausible-looking words could emerge in a direction encouraged by prior context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

At the same time, the study did **not** conclude that the photograph contained no readable information. Across the three conditions, participants converged at identical locations on several terms. “Fort Worth TX” was reported by 52 participants, “story” by 61 and “weather balloons” by 27; “land” also showed substantial agreement across the two primed conditions. Houran and Randle took this convergence as evidence that some portions of the document probably were genuinely legible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That creates a more useful distinction than simply calling the memo “readable” or “unreadable”:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Repeated independent agreement at the same location** is evidence that a particular visual feature may contain recoverable text.
* **A large quantity of words from one observer** shows that the observer can construct a transcription, but does not demonstrate that the transcription is correct.
* **Words that track an observer's supplied context** are especially vulnerable to expectation effects.
* **Confidence or apparent completeness** cannot substitute for agreement among genuinely independent readers.

</div>

The Ramey photograph can therefore contain some authentic textual signal while still leaving enough uncertainty for observers to construct substantial amounts of additional, but unreliable, language. Those two propositions are not contradictory.

## Why confidence must be separated from accuracy

The study's surviving data place an important limit on how far this issue can be taken. Houran and Randle did not establish a ground-truth transcription against which every participant's proposed word could be scored. More seriously, the original participant score sheets were discarded by a research assistant after the computer database and notes had been prepared. When a referee later asked for participant-by-participant counts for each proposed word, the researchers could provide detailed figures only for words recorded as common across the experimental conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That loss matters especially for the ambiguity-tolerance finding. With complete raw responses, one would want to ask whether people at different points on the ambiguity scale merely **produced more candidate words**, or whether they produced more words that were independently reproduced at precisely the same locations by blind observers. Those are very different outcomes. The published study establishes the former association much more clearly than the latter.

There is another complication: effort and motivation were not perfectly controlled. The Roswell group spent about 20 minutes on the task, compared with about 16 minutes for the atomic-bomb group and 14 minutes for the blind group. Houran and Randle themselves suggested that an intriguing story might motivate participants to work harder and consequently perceive more words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Thus the raw number of deciphered words potentially combines several things: perceptual sensitivity, persistence, [prior knowledge]({{ 'prior-knowledge/' | relative_url }}), expectations, cognitive style and willingness to make a guess.

The authors' own interpretation consequently emphasised both **suggestion effects and cognitive style**, rather than treating the number of words as a direct measurement of photographic information. They also pointed to weak agreement among earlier memo interpreters as a reason for caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The safer test is independent agreement

The strongest lesson from the ambiguity result is methodological rather than psychological. A persuasive Ramey-memo reading should not depend heavily on the personality, expectations or confidence of the person proposing it. It should survive changes of observer.

Houran and Randle therefore recommended a much stricter procedure for future work: use standardised enhancement of the best available source material and have at least three independent laboratories specialising in archival-document reading analyse it blindly. Agreement should concern **specific words in precise locations**, allowing inter-rater reliability to be assessed. They explicitly noted that earlier interpretations were unsuitable for a clean reliability calculation because investigators had not followed a common protocol and were already aware of the document's possible Roswell context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That recommendation directly addresses the ambiguity-tolerance problem. If a disputed word is genuinely encoded with sufficient visual information, independent readers should tend to recover it despite differences in cognitive style. If a proposed word mainly reflects an observer's expectations or readiness to resolve uncertainty, agreement should deteriorate when contextual clues are removed and analysts work independently.

This also explains why the small set of cross-condition words in the Houran–Randle experiment deserves more evidential weight than an impressive-looking complete transcription by any single reader. The former survived at least some variation in suggestion and observer; the latter may contain genuine signal, but its apparent clarity cannot establish its own reliability.

## What the experiment actually supports

The ambiguity finding is best understood as a warning against equating **subjective readability with objective legibility**. Houran and Randle found that individual characteristics were related to how much text people reported extracting, while experimental suggestions changed both the quantity and semantic character of those readings. Yet independent observers also converged on a limited number of words, indicating that the photograph was not necessarily devoid of recoverable information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That mixed result is more informative than either extreme position in the Ramey memo debate. The experiment does not show that every proposed word is an illusion, and it does not show that observers who see many words possess unusual decoding ability. Instead, it demonstrates why a degraded document needs a higher evidential standard: **the more ambiguous the stimulus, the less weight should be placed on an individual's sense that a reading “looks obvious”.**

For the Ramey memo, confidence becomes useful evidence only when it is replaced by something testable: independent blind readers, standardised images and procedures, agreement on the same letter or word positions, and [reproducibility]({{ 'reproducibility/' | relative_url }}) across observers. Until that happens, the Houran–Randle ambiguity result gives a clear reason to distinguish what a reader believes they can see from what the photograph can demonstrably support.
