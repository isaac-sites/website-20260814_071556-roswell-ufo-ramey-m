## Introduction

A disputed [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) word should not be treated as established merely because several readers say they can see it. A convincing claim needs an agreement rule fixed in advance: [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) must identify the **same word, in the same position, from substantially the same visible character sequence**, without first being told what answer researchers hope to find. The rule must also say beforehand how much agreement will count as strong enough.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_memo_agreement_thres_13d740-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Agreement Rules illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_memo_agreement_thres_13d740-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_memo_agreement_thres_13d740-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That standard follows directly from the best published experiment on the memo. James Houran and Kevin Randle found that suggestion changed what people thought they could read, yet some words were independently recovered at matching locations across different conditions. They therefore recommended blind, independent analysis and measurement of agreement on specific words at precise positions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The important next step is governance: define what counts as agreement **before** examining the results. Otherwise researchers can unconsciously lower the bar for a favoured reading or redefine near-matches as confirmation after seeing what readers produced.

## Why ‘Several Readers Agree’ Is Too Vague

The phrase “several readers agree” conceals three different questions: how many people attempted the passage, whether they were genuinely independent, and exactly what they agreed about. Five people reporting a word sounds impressive until it emerges that 100 people examined the image, that some knew the proposed transcription beforehand, or that their readings came from neighbouring rather than identical positions.

The Houran-Randle experiment illustrates why the denominator matters. Its 176 participants were randomly assigned to a Roswell condition, an atomic-bomb condition or a blind condition. The researchers specifically looked for agreement on the identity of words **at the same location**, regardless of suggestion condition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Their strongest cross-condition results were revealing. “Fort Worth TX” was reported by 52 participants, “[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})” by 61 and “weather balloons” by 27. “Land”, reported by 47, was different: it appeared at the same location in the two suggested-context groups but not as a common reading across all three conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The group breakdown matters even more. “Fort Worth TX” was reported by 24 Roswell-primed participants, 17 atomic-bomb participants and 11 blind participants. “Story” was reported by 43, 11 and 7 respectively; “weather balloons” by 20, 5 and 2. By contrast, readings such as “remains”, “crash” and “UFO” appeared among Roswell-condition responses rather than as cross-condition [consensus]({{ 'consensus/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those figures do **not** establish a universal numerical threshold for the memo. Houran and Randle did not provide one. They show something more useful: raw vote totals cannot be separated from experimental conditions. A word recovered independently under different expectations carries a different evidential weight from a word concentrated among readers who were primed with the interpretation it supports.

There is also an important limitation in the 2002 data. The original score sheets were discarded after data had been entered, leaving detailed word-level information principally for readings common across the conditions. That makes the experiment valuable evidence about suggestion and cross-condition convergence, but an unsuitable dataset from which to reverse-engineer a definitive percentage threshold for all future [Ramey memo claims]({{ 'memo-claims/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Agreement Must Match Word, Position and Characters

A useful agreement policy should make the unit being tested unusually strict. “Reader A saw something like *victims* and Reader B also mentioned *victims* somewhere” is not enough. The claim concerns photographic information at a particular place on the paper, so agreement must remain tied to that place.

Houran and Randle explicitly framed their proposed test in terms of independent laboratories showing agreement on **specific words in precise locations**. They recommended at least three independent, blind laboratories specialising in reading and transcribing archival documents, working from standardised enhancement of the best available source material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For a modern reader experiment, that principle can be translated into three linked requirements:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Word match:** readers independently produce the same lexical reading. Variants such as “victim”, “victims” and “victims?” should be retained separately in the raw data rather than silently merged.
* **Position match:** the proposed word must occupy the same line and character span. A matching word elsewhere on the memo is not corroboration of the disputed instance.
* **Character-sequence match:** the visible marks must support compatible letter-by-letter readings. Researchers should record uncertain characters rather than forcing every mark into a complete word.

</div>

That last requirement is particularly important for the Ramey memo because earlier investigators themselves used uncertainty notation. Houran and Randle describe Neil Morris's transcription method as distinguishing letters he considered secure from best guesses and undecipherable positions. Yet the investigators' overall transcriptions still differed substantially; the paper notes that researchers shared information while working, undermining their suitability as a genuinely independent reliability sample.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

Character positions also impose a useful physical constraint. The proposed methodology discussed by Houran and Randle called for determining letter spacing from clearer text, extrapolating positions into poorer areas, and estimating word lengths before interpreting difficult passages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> A candidate reading that requires six letters where the image geometry supports four or five should therefore lose credibility even if the completed word sounds plausible in a Roswell narrative.

This is where agreement becomes more informative than simple recognition. Two readers might both guess “victims” because they know that *victims of the wreck* is a famous proposed reading. Stronger evidence would be two blind readers independently marking the same seven character positions and assigning compatible letters to them before either learns that “victims” has ever been proposed.

## What Threshold Should a Word Have to Clear?

There is no scientifically established Ramey-specific cut-off such as “60 per cent agreement proves a word”. Inventing one would merely replace informal judgement with arbitrary arithmetic. The defensible policy is therefore a **tiered threshold established prospectively**, combining a minimum number of independent readers with a formal measure of inter-rater agreement.

Simple percentage agreement is easy to understand but does not account for agreement that may arise by chance. Formal inter-rater measures are designed to quantify the consistency of independent judgements; available measures differ according to the structure of the rating task.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.statology.org/inter-rater-reliability/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: statology.org">[Statology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">statology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What is Inter-rater Reliability? (Definition &amp; Example</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What is Inter-rater Reliability? (Definition &amp; Example)February 27, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 27, 2021</span></span></span> For a Ramey experiment involving multiple readers, missing or uncertain characters and nominal alternatives, the statistical method should therefore be selected when the scoring protocol is designed rather than after the data are collected.

As a general benchmark rather than a Ramey-specific law, methodological guidance commonly treats a Krippendorff's alpha of about 0.80 or above as suitable for reliable conclusions, while values from roughly 0.67 to below 0.80 support only tentative conclusions. Crucially, researchers emphasise that acceptable thresholds depend on the context and consequences of error rather than functioning as universal constants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2021-046/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.r-project.org">[r-project.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.r-project.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on r-project.org.</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, that argues for a conservative rule because the disputed words are being asked to carry unusually large historical implications. A practical policy could classify results this way:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Claimable reading:** high pre-specified inter-rater reliability, convergence across genuinely blind readers or laboratories, agreement on the same location and character span, and no strong competing reading reaching a comparable level.
2. **Tentative reading:** meaningful convergence exists, but agreement misses the high threshold, depends on uncertain characters, or a substantial minority produces a plausible competing transcription.
3. **Unresolved:** agreement is weak, heavily fragmented, sensitive to contextual priming, or produced only after readers have been exposed to proposed answers.

</div>

The distinction prevents a statistical result from doing more work than it can support. “Statistically significant agreement” is not synonymous with “the word has been proved”. Houran and Randle themselves cautioned against over-interpreting even statistically significant results from a future blind triangulation study.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A further safeguard is replication. Their proposed three-laboratory model is stronger than merely pooling individual votes because it tests whether separate analytical teams converge despite working independently. Their later correction of an accidental “Fort Work, Tex” transcription to “Fort Worth, Tex” provided a small but telling example of why independent checking matters: even researchers explicitly studying transcription error can introduce transcription errors themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/501208456/jse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; Falsifiability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; FalsifiabilityJanuary 1, 2002...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2002</span></span></span>

## Why the Threshold Must Be Fixed First

The central governance rule is simple: **do not decide what counts as convincing after seeing which rule makes a preferred word pass**.

Suppose 40 per cent of blind readers independently report the same word. After seeing that result, supporters could call 40 per cent “remarkable agreement”. If only 25 per cent agreed, they might instead argue that one quarter is extraordinary given the poor image. If readers offered variants, researchers could decide afterwards that near-matches should count. Every adjustment may sound individually reasonable, yet together they create a moving target.

The remedy is to register the scoring rules before revealing the disputed answers. The protocol should specify the image versions readers will receive, whether enhancement is permitted, how locations are numbered, how [uncertain letters]({{ 'uncertain-letters/' | relative_url }}) are recorded, whether spelling variants count as matches, which agreement statistic will be calculated, what threshold distinguishes a claim from a tentative reading, and what happens when two competing words both attract substantial support.

This matters especially because the Ramey memo experiment demonstrated that expectations can alter interpretation. Participants told they were looking at material concerning atomic-bomb testing produced context-fitting readings such as “flash”, “glasses” and “atomic”, while Roswell-primed participants generated Roswell-compatible material. Blind participants produced fewer interpretations overall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> The problem is therefore not merely dishonesty or partisanship. Normal perception can make ambiguous marks appear meaningful once a plausible context has been supplied.

The same principle applies to image processing. Houran and Randle recommended standardised enhancement of the best raw material and comparable software before comparing independent laboratory results. They rejected calculating reliability from the existing historical transcriptions because those investigators had neither followed a common analytical protocol nor remained blind to the document's possible content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Agreement produced after researchers exchange interpretations is not the same evidence as agreement generated independently.

## A Defensible Rule for Future Ramey Claims

For this particular memo, the most defensible policy is not “majority wins”. It is **pre-specified blind convergence**.

Before readers begin, researchers should freeze the image set, location grid, transcription notation, treatment of uncertain characters, statistical measure and minimum reliability threshold. At least three genuinely independent analytical teams should work without being given proposed Roswell readings. Each candidate word should then be scored only where readers identify compatible characters at the same predetermined position. The full distribution of alternatives and unreadable responses should be published, not merely the winning answer.

A historically important word should clear a high reliability threshold—something in the region conventionally treated as strong inter-rater reliability, such as 0.80, is a defensible starting benchmark rather than an established Ramey constant—and should survive replication across independent groups. Borderline results should be labelled tentative rather than promoted to settled transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2021-046/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.r-project.org">[The R Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.r-project.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on r-project.org.</span></span></span>

That policy sets a demanding bar, but the existing evidence shows why it is necessary. Some portions of the photograph evidently contain enough information for readers under different conditions to converge: “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” were recovered at corresponding locations across all three groups in the 2002 experiment. At the same time, suggestion generated many additional, context-compatible readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The meaningful dividing line is therefore not between people who believe a proposed Ramey memo word and people who do not. It is between a word that **survives a rule written before anyone knows the answers** and one whose evidential standard is assembled afterwards. For disputed wording with potentially major implications for the Roswell case, only the former deserves to graduate from an interesting possible reading to a reproducible transcription.
