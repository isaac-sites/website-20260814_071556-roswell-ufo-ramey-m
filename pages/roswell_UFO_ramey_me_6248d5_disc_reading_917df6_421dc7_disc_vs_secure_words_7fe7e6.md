## Introduction

The strongest reason to be cautious about reading **“DISC”** in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) is not that the photographed document is completely unreadable. It is that some other words perform markedly better when readers are tested independently. In James Houran and Kevin Randle’s controlled 2002 study, participants given three different descriptions of the memo repeatedly converged on **“Fort Worth TX”**, **“[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})”** and **“weather balloons”** at the same locations. “DISC” did not achieve that cross-condition agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_disc_reading_917df6_421dc7_disc_vs_secure_words_7fe7e6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Secure Words illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_disc_reading_917df6_421dc7_disc_vs_secure_words_7fe7e6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_disc_reading_917df6_421dc7_disc_vs_secure_words_7fe7e6-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That contrast creates a useful internal benchmark. The photograph evidently contains enough surviving visual information for certain words to emerge despite different expectations. A proposed reading as consequential as “DISC” should therefore be expected to show comparable resistance to suggestion. Instead, it belongs to the more disputed layer of transcription. The clearest words do not settle what the memo means, but they demonstrate what comparatively strong word-level evidence can look like — and “DISC” falls short of that standard.

## Which words survived independent readings?

Houran and Randle approached a central problem with attempts to decipher the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo: people already familiar with Roswell may know what they expect or hope to find. The researchers divided 176 participants into three groups. Fifty-nine were told that the document concerned Roswell and might contain information about a government cover-up; 58 were instead given an atomic-bomb context; and 59 were given no suggested subject at all. Participants examined an enlarged print plus four high-resolution versions of the memo and recorded what they thought they could read line by line.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The experiment confirmed that suggestion mattered. People primed with the Roswell story produced readings such as “UFO”, “crash” and “remains”; people primed with an atomic-bomb story produced alternatives including “atomic”, “flash” and “laboratory”. That is important because it demonstrates experimentally the very danger at issue in the “DISC” debate: recognisable-looking marks in a degraded image can acquire words that fit the story supplied to the reader.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Yet suggestion did not explain everything. Three readings appeared at identical locations across all three conditions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **“Fort Worth TX”** — 52 participants in total: 24 in the Roswell condition, 17 in the atomic-bomb condition and 11 in the blind condition.
* **“story”** — 61 participants: 43, 7 and 11 respectively.
* **“weather balloons”** — 27 participants when the researchers combined the corresponding reading across groups; the table records 20 Roswell-primed participants and five atomic-bomb-primed participants reading the full phrase, while two blind participants read “balloons”.
* **“land”** also attracted substantial agreement, with 47 readings, but it appeared in the two primed conditions rather than all three.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

Those results are especially useful because the researchers were not merely asking whether people could imagine plausible words. They were looking for the **same words in the same places despite different contextual cues**. Houran and Randle concluded that the agreement showed that at least portions of the photographed document really were legible, even without sophisticated computer [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That conclusion cuts both ways. It rebuts the simplistic claim that nothing whatsoever can be recovered from the memo. But precisely because some text can apparently survive independent testing, it also gives investigators something against which weaker readings can be compared.

## Why “DISC” does not match that benchmark

“DISC” or “DISK” occupies a different evidential category. Earlier specialist investigators had proposed those readings, and some regarded the letters as among the clearer portions of the document. But the controlled experiment did **not** reproduce “DISC” as one of the words shared across its Roswell, atomic-bomb and blind conditions. The study’s abstract instead emphasised that only “a few words” were consistently perceived at corresponding locations across all three groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The distinction matters more than a simple tally of opinions. “Fort Worth TX” survived a test in which some participants were actively encouraged to think about an entirely different historical subject and others were told nothing about the document. “Story” did likewise. Even the balloon reading persisted outside the Roswell-primed group. Their recognition therefore cannot easily be attributed solely to participants knowing the familiar Roswell narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

“DISC”, by contrast, is almost perfectly positioned to benefit from contextual [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}). Anyone told that a nearly illegible document concerns Roswell has an obvious reason to anticipate words such as *disc*, *crash* or *UFO*. The experiment actually produced “crash” and “UFO” specifically among the Roswell-primed readings, while the atomic-bomb group generated vocabulary appropriate to its own supplied story. This does not prove that “DISC” is absent. It shows why seeing a contextually attractive word is weaker evidence than recovering it independently of that context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There is also direct evidence that the disputed character group does not compel a single reading. Earlier photographic examiner Robert Estes used professional [imaging methods]({{ 'imaging-methods/' | relative_url }}) and scanned material at 9,000 dpi, producing a very large digital file for manipulation. He still said he could not reliably decipher the memo. When pressed for tentative impressions, he thought he could make out something like “Fort Work, Tex” with limited confidence. But at the location where others had proposed “Disk” or “ELSE”, Estes saw something closer to “ELA*”, while acknowledging that it made no semantic sense to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

That comparison is revealing. A reader can be wrong about an individual letter — “Work” rather than “Worth”, for example — while still recovering enough character structure for the underlying place name to become recognisable. At the proposed “DISC” location, however, disagreement extends to the basic identities of the characters. The dispute is therefore not merely over whether the last letter is **C** or **K**; competing readings can imply a different word altogether.

## Consensus words are not automatically Roswell proof

The secure-word benchmark also prevents an opposite mistake: treating agreement on “Fort Worth”, “story” or “weather balloons” as proof of a particular interpretation of the whole memo. Houran and Randle explicitly warned that agreement on individual words did not establish the surrounding meaning. Their participants could recover pieces of text while still being strongly influenced by suggestion when turning those pieces into a coherent narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This distinction between **word recognition** and **document interpretation** is central to the Ramey memo debate. “Fort Worth TX” is historically unsurprising in material associated with the Fort Worth setting. “Story” is semantically flexible. “Weather balloons” is certainly relevant to the public explanation being discussed around the Roswell episode, but the phrase by itself does not reveal whether the document is reporting that explanation, proposing it, criticising it or merely repeating information already circulating.

The same caution applies even more strongly to “DISC”. Suppose, for argument’s sake, that the four characters eventually were established as that word. It would still be necessary to determine its grammatical surroundings before drawing conclusions about what the writer meant by it. Claims involving quotation marks, shipment, wreckage, victims or a deliberate cover story require additional disputed characters and words. Confidence cannot simply propagate from one possible word into an entire reconstructed sentence.

This is why some pro-Roswell interpretations go much further than the controlled evidence warrants. David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }})’s reconstruction, for example, incorporates “DISC” into a much broader proposed message involving wreckage, transportation and a press release about weather balloons. That reconstruction is internally meaningful, but meaningfulness is not the same thing as independent recovery from the photograph. Houran and Randle specifically cautioned against extracting an overall context from unusual but unverified word readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

## The photograph itself imposes a hard limit

The uneven quality of the readings makes sense given the source material. The text does not survive as an original document available for inspection; investigators are attempting to reconstruct small type photographed indirectly while Ramey held the sheet. Houran and Randle described the memo as inherently ambiguous and noted that even experienced researchers failed to agree fully about its contents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The US Air Force's 1994 investigation illustrates the underlying imaging problem independently of the later word-reading experiment. Researchers obtained first-generation Fort Worth Star-Telegram prints and negatives from the University of Texas at Arlington and submitted the relevant photograph for digitisation and photo interpretation. According to the Air Force report, the organisation performing the analysis concluded on 20 July 1994 that the digitised photographs were of insufficient quality to visualise the sought-after textual details reliably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span>

Later civilian work demonstrated that investigators could nevertheless perceive some fragments, and Houran and Randle's experiment provides evidence that a few of those fragments contain genuine recoverable signal. The two findings are not necessarily contradictory. A degraded photograph can preserve enough information for relatively favourable combinations of letters to be recognisable while leaving other character groups below the threshold for dependable transcription.

That is exactly why the secure words are so informative. They show that the question is not simply **“Can anything be read?”** It is **“Which proposed readings remain stable when expectations change?”**

## What the contrast means for confidence in “DISC”

The fairest assessment is therefore neither that “DISC” has been disproved nor that it has been securely deciphered. Its weakness is comparative: it has not demonstrated the same robustness as the memo's strongest word-level readings.

Houran and Randle proposed a demanding way to improve on the existing situation. They recommended standardised enhancement of the best available raw material, followed by analysis by at least three **independent, blind laboratories** specialising in archival-document transcription. Agreement should concern particular words at precise locations, allowing inter-rater reliability — the degree to which independent readers reach the same result — to be assessed rather than assumed. They also argued that previous interpretations were unsuitable for such a reliability calculation because most investigators had neither followed a standardised protocol nor been blind to the document's supposed context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Applied specifically to “DISC”, that framework supplies a straightforward evidential test. A strong case would require independent readers who do not know they are examining the Ramey memo to recover substantially the same letters at the same position, ideally across standardised versions of the best source image. If those readers repeatedly produced **DISC/DISK** without being primed with Roswell vocabulary, the reading would move towards the category occupied by “Fort Worth TX” and “story”. If they continued producing divergent character strings, confidence should remain correspondingly low.

The secure words therefore make the argument over “DISC” harder, not easier. They demonstrate that genuine agreement can emerge from this badly degraded document. Because that agreement exists elsewhere, “DISC” cannot be defended merely by saying that every part of the memo is ambiguous or that different readers inevitably see different things. The photograph itself supplies a tougher standard: **some readings survive changes in expectation; “DISC”, so far, has not shown the same independent resilience**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
