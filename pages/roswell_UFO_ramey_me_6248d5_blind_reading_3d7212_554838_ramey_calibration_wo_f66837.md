## Introduction

The most useful words in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) may be the least sensational ones. In James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 reader experiment, participants working under three very different expectations repeatedly located the same mundane fragments: “Fort Worth TX”, “[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})” and a balloon-related phrase reported by the authors as “weather balloons”. Those convergences matter because the experiment simultaneously showed that suggestion could change what people thought they saw elsewhere in the blurred document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_ramey_calibration_wo_f66837-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Calibration Words illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_ramey_calibration_wo_f66837-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_blind_reading_3d7212_554838_ramey_calibration_wo_f66837-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That makes these fragments valuable as **calibration words**. They provide examples of what relatively robust reading looks like when the same photographic marks are examined by people primed for Roswell, people primed for atomic-bomb testing, and people told nothing about the subject. The comparison does not establish what the whole memo says. Instead, it supplies a practical benchmark: disputed readings should ideally approach the cross-group stability achieved by these comparatively ordinary fragments before being treated as secure evidence.

## Why “Fort Worth TX” showed unusually strong convergence

Houran and Randle divided 176 participants among three conditions: 59 received a Roswell/UFO context, 58 were given an atomic-bomb-testing context, and 59 received no suggested subject. Participants examined an enlarged print of the memo together with higher-resolution versions derived from the Fort Worth Star-Telegram photographic material. The research assistant present during the task was described as blind to the experimenters’ goals and hypotheses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdo cz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Abcdo cz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdo cz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span></span></span>

Against that deliberately varied background, “Fort Worth TX” stands out. The study’s Table 3 records the fragment at the same location for 24 people in the pro-UFO group, 17 in the atomic-bomb group and 11 in the blind group: **52 readers altogether**, or just under 30 per cent of all 176 participants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The distribution is more informative than the total alone. A phrase found only by Roswell-primed readers could plausibly reflect expectations supplied by the briefing. “Fort Worth TX” cannot be dismissed so easily on that basis because people who had instead been told to expect atomic-bomb information also reported it, as did readers who received no narrative prompt. The proportion declined as contextual assistance disappeared — about 41 per cent of the Roswell group, 29 per cent of the atomic group and 19 per cent of the blind group — but the reading did not disappear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

There is also an unusually strong external reason for taking this particular reading seriously. The photograph itself came from the Fort Worth Star-Telegram collection and shows Brigadier General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) during the 8 July 1947 Fort Worth [photo session]({{ 'photo-session/' | relative_url }}). The study identifies the setting as Fort Worth Army Air Field and credits the relevant photographic material to the University of Texas at Arlington collection. In other words, “Fort Worth” is not merely a plausible-looking sequence of letters; it independently fits the known physical setting in which Ramey was photographed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That does not make every surrounding word reliable. It does something narrower and more useful: it demonstrates that the photograph retains enough information in at least one location for readers with incompatible expectations to recover a historically appropriate place name.

## How “story” and “weather balloons” performed across groups

“Story” produced the largest total agreement reported for a specific word. Houran and Randle counted **61 participants** identifying it in the same location: 43 in the Roswell condition, 11 in the atomic-bomb condition and seven in the blind condition. That is roughly 35 per cent of the full sample.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

Its distribution nevertheless illustrates why raw consensus totals need interpretation. About 73 per cent of the Roswell-primed group reported “story”, compared with about 19 per cent of the atomic group and 12 per cent of blind readers. So “story” survived all three conditions, but its visibility was dramatically boosted when readers were already thinking about Roswell. The fragment therefore occupies an interesting middle ground: there appears to be enough visual information for some unprompted readers to recover it, while contextual expectation appears to make that reading much easier to perceive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The balloon wording is even more instructive. The paper’s discussion says that **27 participants** converged on “weather balloons” in the same location. Table 3 breaks the relevant responses down as 20 in the pro-UFO condition, five in the atomic-bomb condition and two in the blind condition. There is a small but important reporting nuance: the blind-condition entry in the table is printed simply as “Balloons”, whereas the authors’ subsequent prose groups the cross-condition result under “weather balloons”. Readers should therefore distinguish the strong conclusion — a balloon-related reading appeared in all three groups at that location — from the stronger claim that every one of those 27 people independently supplied the complete two-word phrase.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That distinction is especially valuable in a document where interpretation can easily outrun image quality. The evidence supports a cross-group balloon reading, but the published table itself cautions against silently turning all levels of agreement into identical [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}).

The result is nevertheless striking because the blind participants had not been told that the image concerned Roswell. Only two of them reported “Balloons”, so this was hardly an obvious reading under fully blind conditions. Yet its appearance outside the Roswell group, together with “Fort Worth TX” and “story”, differentiates these fragments from words generated only inside a particular suggested narrative. The authors consequently concluded that some portions of the document appeared genuinely legible even though interpretation of its wider meaning remained susceptible to suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Consensus fragments are not a complete transcription

There is an important trap here. Because “Fort Worth”, “story” and balloon wording are compatible with the known Roswell press episode, it is tempting to use their success to validate an entire proposed transcription surrounding them. That inference is not warranted by the experiment.

Houran and Randle’s own comparison of earlier Ramey memo readings shows precisely the opposite problem. Investigators had produced substantially different interpretations of the same lines while agreeing more strongly on a few fragments. Line three, for example, repeatedly attracted versions of “AT FORT WORTH, TEX.” By contrast, other portions generated much greater disagreement. On the lower lines, several previous readings contained “story” and “weather balloons”, but the words connecting those fragments varied considerably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

This is why the calibration concept is useful. Agreement on a clear island of text does not automatically validate the uncertain text around it. A reader can correctly identify “Fort Worth” while being wrong about the preceding noun, verb or sentence. Likewise, recognising “weather balloons” does not establish whether the surrounding sentence says that balloons were the genuine explanation, a proposed public-relations explanation, or something else. Those are competing interpretations of syntax and context, and they require considerably more readable material than recognition of the two nouns alone.

That limitation is particularly relevant because some later Roswell interpretations place the consensus words inside highly consequential sentences. One pro-UFO reconstruction, for example, treats the lower lines as discussing a press-release strategy involving weather balloons. Such reconstructions may incorporate genuinely recognisable fragments, but the Houran-Randle experiment did **not** independently establish the intervening language needed to prove that interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.roswellproof.com/militarydebunk.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: roswellproof.com">[Roswell Proof]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">roswellproof.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell Proofmilitarydebunk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell Proofmilitarydebunk</span></span></span>

The distinction is therefore between **word recognition and sentence recovery**. The experiment offers comparatively good evidence for several isolated textual anchors. It offers much weaker evidence that researchers can reliably reconstruct complete sentences from the degraded marks between those anchors.

## Using the surviving words to test disputed readings

The calibration words suggest a straightforward hierarchy for evaluating Ramey memo claims. At the strongest level are fragments recovered in the same place by readers exposed to different expectations. “Fort Worth TX” is the clearest example because it crossed all three experimental conditions and also matches the independently known setting. “Story” also crossed every condition, although its much higher frequency among Roswell-primed readers indicates a substantial contextual effect. The balloon wording crossed conditions too, but with fewer readers and the table’s “Balloons” versus “weather balloons” qualification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That gives disputed words something concrete to be compared against. A proposed reading should become less persuasive as it moves away from this pattern — particularly if it appears only after readers have been told what to expect, requires substantial image processing before anyone recognises it, or fails to recur among independent observers. Conversely, a difficult word would become much more interesting if sizeable groups of genuinely blind readers independently supplied the same letters at the same location without being shown candidate answers.

The surviving fragments also show why “nobody can read anything” is too strong a description of the memo. Earlier examination had found the material extremely difficult: Richard Haines reportedly saw vague markings without being able to resolve useful individual words, and the US Air Force reported that a national-level organisation could not extract the desired detail even after digitisation. Yet the later reader experiment found reproducible agreement on particular portions. The defensible position is therefore not that the photograph contains no recoverable textual signal, but that **recoverability varies sharply from one part of the memo to another**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The experiment has limitations of its own. Most importantly for anyone trying to reanalyse the numbers, the authors reported that the original participant score sheets had been discarded after data compilation. They therefore retained detailed counts for the words recorded as common across conditions but could not reconstruct an equally detailed participant-by-participant catalogue of every attempted reading. That loss [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) the kinds of retrospective statistical checks that can now be performed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

Even with that limitation, the calibration words provide a more disciplined standard than simply asking whether a proposed transcription looks plausible when printed beside an enlargement. They demonstrate what happens when photographic signal is strong enough to push readers with different expectations towards the same answer — and, equally importantly, how much less confidence is justified when that convergence disappears.

## The calibration lesson

“Fort Worth TX”, “story” and the balloon wording do not settle the Roswell controversy. Their importance is methodological. They show that the Ramey photograph is neither uniformly readable nor uniformly meaningless. Some fragments survive changing expectations; many more proposed readings do not have comparable experimental support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Among the three, “Fort Worth TX” provides the strongest calibration anchor because it appeared across all conditions, attracted substantial agreement and corresponds to the independently established location of the photograph. “Story” achieved even greater numerical agreement but was much more concentrated among Roswell-primed readers. The balloon fragment appeared less often, while still crossing all three groups and therefore supplying another useful anchor — with the caveat that the published table gives the blind readers’ response as “Balloons” rather than the full phrase.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That pattern supplies a demanding but sensible rule for the harder Ramey memo claims. Words carrying major evidential weight should be expected to survive something resembling the test already passed by these mundane fragments: **independent readers, different expectations, the same location and substantially the same letters**. Until disputed terms achieve comparable convergence, the established calibration words tell us more than an attractive full transcription does — not because they reveal the memo’s entire message, but because they demonstrate how genuine agreement looks when readers are not all being led towards the same answer.
