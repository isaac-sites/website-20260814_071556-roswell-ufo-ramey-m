## Introduction

Barry [Greenwood]({{ 'greenwood/' | relative_url }})’s interpretation of the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) document became more specific between 2004 and 2009, but it did not become a claimed definitive transcription. In 2004, his argument was principally about **document type**: the formatting, punctuation and apparent ending looked sufficiently like a civilian news teletype that a newswire origin should compete seriously with the idea of a secret military message. His published conclusion was deliberately comparative — the newswire possibility was “at least equal to or greater than” the secret-message possibility — rather than certain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

By 2009, Greenwood thought he had found a much stronger clue. A blurred six-letter word apparently ending in “GHT” seemed to match the contemporary press error “[Warren Haught]({{ 'warren-haught/' | relative_url }})”, the name newspapers used for Roswell public information officer Walter Haut. That gave his newswire hypothesis a concrete textual counterpart. Yet Greenwood still acknowledged that “Warren” itself was not clearly visible and called it the “most likely fit”. The change, therefore, was from a broad probabilistic format argument to a more detailed probabilistic reconstruction — not from uncertainty to proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/09/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: September 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 2009</span></span></span>

## What Greenwood actually claimed in 2004

Greenwood’s March 2004 article, “An Observation on the [Ramey Memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }})”, began from an unusually cautious assessment of the photograph itself. He described the visible writing as roughly eight lines of fuzziness, “virtually impossible to read” apart from perhaps a few words or word groups, and pointed to the large number of competing interpretations already produced by researchers. His initial insight was consequently not that he had deciphered a hidden message, but that the **layout** looked familiar from teletype material he had handled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

That distinction is important. Greenwood thought the apparent final isolated group, spacing and line structure made it “more than reasonable” to classify the sheet as some form of teletype. But identifying a teletype did not answer the decisive question: was it military traffic or material originating with a newspaper? He therefore compared examples of both rather than presenting a line-by-line decipherment as his starting evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

His case for a journalistic source rested especially on punctuation and message endings. Greenwood observed that his military examples tended to avoid ordinary punctuation and that military traffic normally used 24-hour or “Zulu” time, whereas newspaper teletypes used conventional punctuation and civilian time notation. He thought the Ramey document contained a comma and full stop around the comparatively recognisable “AT FORT WORTH, TEX.” and quotation marks around “DISC”. He also noted that the same kinds of wording appeared in contemporary Roswell newspaper stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

But Greenwood’s conclusion carefully stopped short of declaring that those features established the document’s [provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}). After explicitly stating that “considerable doubt” remained about deciphering most of the text, he concluded that a newswire teletype was **at least as plausible, and perhaps more plausible**, than a secret document discussing Roswell aliens. That wording sets a useful baseline for judging his later position. His 2004 thesis was an alternative hypothesis intended to weaken [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) in a supposedly established military transcription, not a claim that he had conclusively read the sheet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

There was another source of uncertainty inside his argument. Photographer J. Bond Johnson had at one stage speculated that he might have handed Ramey an Associated Press “flash”, but later explicitly said that speculation was mistaken. Greenwood nevertheless regarded Johnson’s earlier recollection as compatible with his format evidence. Whatever one makes of Greenwood’s treatment of Johnson’s changing account, the disputed recollection could not independently establish that the photographed sheet was the AP copy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

## The Warren Haught clue strengthened the later case

Greenwood’s September 2009 “Ramey Memo Redux — Line 5” moved his argument onto different ground. He still described the document as lying at the “extreme limit of resolution” and said that only a few words seemed visible. He also recalled his 2004 position in restrained terms: he had “offered the possibility” that the sheet was a newswire teletype rather than a military document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/09/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: September 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 2009</span></span></span>

The new development arose while he was examining an enlargement and experimenting with red-and-blue 3-D filters. Greenwood later described the filters as producing only a small apparent improvement: they were not enough to make the indistinct marks genuinely readable, but seemed to make patterns stand out. That is a significant qualification because his later reconstruction did not emerge from a newly recovered, sharply resolved text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/additional-ramey-memo-scans.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Additional Ramey Memo Scans</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Additional Ramey Memo ScansNovember 7, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 7, 2015</span></span></span>

On what Greenwood called line 5, he thought the second word occupied six character positions and gave a strong impression of ending in “GHT”. Other readers had supplied different completions: “SOUGHT” was one possibility, while David Rudiak associated the cluster with “WRIGHT”, as in Wright Field. Greenwood instead remembered a contemporary newspaper clipping in which Walter Haut’s name had been rendered as “Warren Haught”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/09/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: September 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 2009</span></span></span>

This was a much more consequential clue than the generic observation that the sheet looked like a teletype. The erroneous name really does occur in surviving July 1947 press coverage. An Associated Press account printed in the *Seattle Daily Times* identified “Lieut. Warren Haught” as the Roswell Army Air Field public-information officer; the *Sacramento Bee* printed substantially the same identification, and the wording also appeared elsewhere. In reality, the officer was Walter Haut.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

For Greenwood, that coincidence offered something his 2004 argument lacked: a specific piece of contemporary newswire language that might map onto the disputed letter shapes. The surrounding press wording was suggestive too. The historical reports paired “Warren Haught” with “public information officer” and Roswell, precisely the sort of sequence Greenwood thought he could discern around the disputed portion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

Greenwood had found external context that made one interpretation of the indistinct marks substantially more coherent. He had not obtained new photographic information demonstrating that every proposed letter was actually present.

## His own wording preserves the uncertainty

The clearest limit on later claims about Greenwood’s reading comes from Greenwood himself. Discussing the proposed “Warren Haught” sequence, he expressly conceded that “we don’t see the word ‘Warren’ clearly”. His reasoning was instead that the preceding space appeared to contain six letters and that, given the documented “Haught” wording in Roswell press coverage, “WARREN” was the **most likely fit**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022</span></span></span>

Those phrases describe inference, not direct transcription. They effectively separate three different levels of confidence:

* Greenwood thought some portions, such as “AT FORT WORTH, TEX.” and “‘DISC’”, had comparatively broad support among readers.
* He thought the disputed six-letter group gave the impression of ending in “GHT”.
* He used word length, neighbouring apparent words and a real 1947 newspaper phrase to infer that “WARREN HAUGHT” was a particularly attractive reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/09/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: September 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 2009</span></span></span>

That hierarchy matters because the last step depends partly on contextual matching. Once a researcher knows that newspapers called Haut “Warren Haught”, blurred marks compatible with “GHT” can suddenly look much more meaningful. The match is historically interesting, but knowing the matching phrase can also influence what a viewer perceives in an ambiguous image.

This is not merely a theoretical concern in the Ramey-document debate. James Houran and Kevin Randle experimentally tested whether contextual suggestion affected attempts to read the photograph. Participants were randomly given Roswell, atomic-bomb or neutral contexts. The study found evidence that suggestion and cognitive factors influenced interpretations, while some words nevertheless attracted agreement across groups. Follow-up analysis reported that contextual conditions generated more condition-specific perceived words than words shared between conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Greenwood’s “Haught” discovery therefore cuts both ways. The existence of the unusual historical spelling is genuine evidence favouring his interpretation over a completely invented phrase. Yet the sequence became apparent to him in connection with reading the contemporary clipping. His reconstruction consequently cannot be treated as though a blind examination had independently yielded an unmistakable “WARREN HAUGHT” and researchers only afterwards discovered the same phrase in 1947 newspapers.

## Probability is not a transcription

Later summaries can easily flatten Greenwood’s changing position into a stronger claim than he actually made: that he “read” the Ramey document as a newspaper report mentioning Warren Haught. A more accurate description is that he progressively assembled a **newswire interpretation** from several mutually reinforcing clues.

In 2004, those clues were primarily structural: teletype layout, apparent punctuation, message-ending conventions and overlap between relatively readable fragments and newspaper language. His published judgement remained explicitly comparative, with the newswire explanation presented as at least competitive with the secret-message hypothesis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

In 2009, the “Warren Haught” connection made that interpretation more specific. It supplied an unusual historical phrase rather than a generic word that might appear in many military or newspaper texts. The fact that several contemporary newspapers demonstrably carried the erroneous name makes Greenwood’s observation substantially more interesting than a free-form attempt to impose words on noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

But specificity should not be mistaken for certainty. Greenwood still characterised the source image as being at the limit of resolution, admitted that “Warren” was unclear and described his completion as the most likely fit. A later scholarly review by Kevin Randle likewise presents Greenwood’s proposal as an interpretation and notes that other researchers rejected his conclusion about the document being a civilian news teletype. Brad Sparks, for example, argued that military traffic could sometimes contain ordinary punctuation and proposed that a less formal military message could account for features Greenwood regarded as journalistic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022</span></span></span>

That disagreement does not make Greenwood’s proposal worthless; it defines what the proposal can legitimately establish. His work demonstrates that a historically grounded civilian-news reconstruction can be fitted to important portions of the same ambiguous document that others have read as evidence of military recovery operations. It therefore weakens any claim that one exotic transcription is visually compulsory.

What it does **not** establish is a verified text reading “Warren Haught, public relations officer at Roswell, said” or an independently demonstrated newswire provenance. The surviving photograph has not yielded that degree of textual certainty. Indeed, subsequent discussion of newer scans continued to acknowledge the difficulty of obtaining clearly interpretable text, and the broader literature still treats the competing readings as unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span></span></span>

## How certain was Greenwood?

Greenwood’s confidence changed chiefly in **specificity**, rather than crossing a clean boundary from tentative to certain. In 2004 he had a format-based hypothesis: the Ramey sheet might be an ordinary news teletype, and he judged that possibility at least competitive with a secret military message. In 2009 he believed he had found a particularly telling textual clue — the apparent “GHT” sequence and its fit with the genuine 1947 press error “Warren Haught”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

That later clue plainly increased the explanatory coherence of his interpretation. “Warren Haught” was not a phrase manufactured solely to fit the photograph; it was circulating in Roswell news coverage at the relevant time. But Greenwood’s published language continued to distinguish what appeared comparatively visible from what he supplied through contextual inference. “Warren” remained unclear; “most likely fit” remained a probability judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span></span></span>

The most defensible reading of Greenwood’s trajectory is therefore neither that he merely floated an unchanged speculation nor that he eventually deciphered the memo. He moved from **“this looks compatible with a newspaper teletype”** towards **“this particular line may fit a documented Roswell newswire phrase unusually well”**. His own qualifications are part of the evidence, not incidental rhetoric. They are precisely why Greenwood’s interpretation is valuable in the Ramey memo dispute: it provides a concrete competing reading while simultaneously illustrating how much uncertainty remains when blurred marks, contextual knowledge and historical reconstruction have to do much of the work.
