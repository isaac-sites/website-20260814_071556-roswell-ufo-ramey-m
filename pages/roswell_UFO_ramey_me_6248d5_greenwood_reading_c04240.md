## Introduction

Barry Greenwood’s reading of the Ramey memo matters less because it “solves” the blurred document than because it shows how radically different conclusions can be built from the same few marks. Where other Roswell researchers reconstructed part of one disputed line as language associated with aircraft movements or a military investigation, Greenwood came to see something much more mundane: a garbled newswire reference to Roswell public information officer Walter Haut, rendered in contemporary press reports as “[Warren Haught]({{ 'warren-haught/' | relative_url }})”. That alternative reading led him towards a broader hypothesis that Brigadier General Roger Ramey may have been holding a civilian news teletype rather than a secret military message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The importance of Greenwood’s interpretation is therefore methodological. It demonstrates that apparently suggestive letter clusters in the photograph do not compel a single reconstruction. ## Greenwood’s alternative wording

Greenwood had examined the Ramey photograph for years before proposing his most distinctive reading. In a 2004 article, “An Observation on the Ramey Memo”, he argued from the document’s formatting that it resembled a teletype. He compared the photographed sheet with examples of military and journalistic transmissions and focused on such features as the apparent line arrangement, punctuation and the isolated material near the bottom of the page. His broader suggestion was that a newspaper-wire origin deserved at least as much consideration as the theory that the sheet contained a classified military account of Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/UFO_Historical_Revue/uhr11_text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive U.F.O. Historical Revue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive U.F.O. Historical Revue</span></span></span>

Greenwood’s case rested partly on features he thought differentiated routine news teletypes from military traffic. He observed that surviving military examples often substituted abbreviations such as “PD” for a period and tended to place identifying information and transmission times in characteristic positions. Newspaper teletypes, by contrast, made ordinary use of commas, full stops and quotation marks. In the Ramey photograph, Greenwood believed punctuation could be seen around comparatively less controversial fragments such as “AT FORT WORTH, TEX.” and “‘DISC’”. He therefore treated the document’s apparent style, not merely individual words, as evidence for a journalistic origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/UFO_Historical_Revue/uhr11_text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive U.F.O. Historical Revue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive U.F.O. Historical Revue</span></span></span>

His more striking development came in 2009. Returning to an enlarged photograph, Greenwood concentrated on a line in which other interpreters had commonly perceived a word ending in “GHT”, often reconstructed as “SOUGHT”. Greenwood instead connected those letters with wording he had found in July 1947 newspaper coverage: “Lt. Warren Haught, public information officer at Roswell …”. He acknowledged that the preceding word was not clearly visible, but argued that the space appeared compatible with a six-letter word and that “WARREN” was the most plausible completion if the apparent “GHT” belonged to “HAUGHT”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That connection was not invented retrospectively out of Roswell folklore. Contemporary Associated Press reports really did misidentify Walter Haut as “Warren Haught”. Surviving [July 8]({{ 'july-8/' | relative_url }}) newspaper transcriptions from outlets including the *Seattle Daily Times* and *Sacramento Bee* describe “Lieut. Warren Haught” as the public-information officer at Roswell Army Air Field. The wording therefore supplied Greenwood with a real 1947 textual template that could plausibly influence how he parsed the blurred shapes in Ramey’s hand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

## The passage where readings diverge

The disagreement becomes especially revealing when Greenwood’s reconstruction is placed beside David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }})’s. A recent specialist discussion summarising the dispute gives Greenwood’s reading in this area as “WARREN HAUGHT, PUBLIC RELATIONS OFFICER AT ROSWELL, SAID”, while Rudiak assigns entirely different words to the same approximate stretch, including references to a B-29 or C-47, Wright Field and Roswell. Whatever one thinks of either reconstruction, they cannot both represent an uncomplicated visual reading of the original marks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/the-missing-u/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span></span></span>

Greenwood’s own reasoning makes the role of contextual inference unusually visible. According to Kevin Randle’s 2022 review of the memo controversy, Greenwood first recognised the apparent “GHT” cluster, then recalled a July 8 newspaper clipping containing the misspelt surname “Haught”. From there, the surrounding spacing and the known Roswell wording helped him infer “Warren”. In other words, the proposed phrase was not recovered character by character in isolation; its reconstruction depended substantially on finding an independently known phrase that appeared capable of fitting the visual pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That is not automatically an illegitimate method. Historians, cryptanalysts and textual scholars routinely use linguistic context to resolve damaged writing. The problem is that contextual completion becomes hazardous when the underlying signal is extremely weak and several different contexts are available. Rudiak likewise argues that vocabulary, spelling, grammar and historical context can narrow possible readings of indistinct letter groups. Greenwood simply applied a different contextual corpus — contemporary press language rather than an assumed military communication — and arrived somewhere very different.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.roswellproof.com/reconstruct.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: roswellproof.com">[Roswell Proof]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">roswellproof.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey Roswell Message Copyright © 2001 by David Rudiak, All Rights Reserved Download text version in Microsoft Word rich text format (...</span></span></span>

The “Haught” clue is particularly instructive because it has genuine external support. The erroneous name appears in multiple period newspaper reports, and a July 9 *Washington Post* account likewise referred to “Lieut. Warren Haught” while discussing the Roswell announcement. That makes Greenwood’s proposal considerably more than a random alternative word. Yet the existence of matching wording outside the photograph does not establish that the same wording is actually present inside it. It only strengthens one possible contextual reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/washingtonpost9ul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Washington Post, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

## Why the newswire hypothesis remains contested

Greenwood used his textual reading to support a larger claim about the document’s identity. If the Ramey sheet repeated phrases appearing on the news wires, if it contained ordinary punctuation, and if its terminal formatting resembled civilian teletype traffic, then perhaps it was simply a press dispatch summarising the rapidly developing Roswell story. That possibility also intersected with photographer J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})’s later recollection that he had at one stage speculated he might have handed Ramey an Associated Press “flash”, although Johnson subsequently said that speculation was mistaken. Greenwood argued that the newswire possibility nevertheless remained viable independently of Johnson’s changing memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/UFO_Historical_Revue/uhr11_text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive U.F.O. Historical Revue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive U.F.O. Historical Revue</span></span></span>

Researchers favouring a military origin challenged that reasoning. Brad Sparks, for example, objected that actual military messages from the period were not uniformly devoid of conventional punctuation, weakening Greenwood’s attempt to distinguish civilian and military teletypes on that basis. Sparks also proposed that an informal “general-to-general” or back-channel message could depart from more rigid communication conventions, including normal address and signature practices. Randle’s later survey therefore treats the dispute not as a settled choice between an identifiable newswire and an identifiable military telegram, but as another example of how uncertain assumptions about document type feed directly into attempted transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

Greenwood himself had been cautious in 2004. After comparing surviving military and newspaper formats, he concluded that a newswire explanation was “at least equal to or greater than” the secret-message interpretation, rather than claiming that he had proved the sheet’s origin. He also conceded that considerable doubt remained about deciphering most of the document. That restraint is important because later summaries sometimes present rival Ramey memo “transcriptions” as though researchers were reading stable words from a merely faint page. Greenwood’s argument was instead probabilistic: format and a few apparent fragments appeared to him to fit journalism better than classified military traffic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/UFO_Historical_Revue/uhr11_text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive U.F.O. ## Why disagreement is itself evidence

The strongest lesson from Greenwood’s interpretation is that incompatible readings are not just an inconvenience awaiting better eyesight. They are evidence about the quality of the underlying visual information. In 2002, James Houran and Kevin Randle tested how suggestion affected attempts to decipher the memo. Participants were randomly given different contextual frames — Roswell, atomic-bomb testing or no suggested subject — before attempting to read the same ambiguous material. The study was designed precisely because established researchers had failed to converge on a common transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

Later technical work reinforced that caution. A follow-up project had high-quality scans independently examined by three specialist laboratories using techniques including blind deconvolution, Fourier processing, filtering and wavelet-based methods. As summarised in Randle’s 2022 review, none produced clearly interpretable text; the analysts found that blur and [film-grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}) noise remained severe obstacles, with relevant noise occurring at scales comparable to the characters themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That limitation changes how Greenwood’s “Warren Haught” should be evaluated. It is useful evidence that a civilian-news interpretation can be made to fit both some visible shapes and authentic 1947 vocabulary. It is not a secure transcription. Equally, the existence of Greenwood’s coherent alternative weakens claims that visually dramatic rival phrases — including readings invoked as evidence for victims, wreckage or secret aircraft movements — must be present simply because one researcher can outline letters that appear to form them. When one blurred sequence can plausibly become “SOUGHT”, “HAUGHT”, or something involving aircraft and Wright Field depending on the interpreter, confidence should fall rather than rise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[journalofscientificexploration.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

Greenwood’s contribution therefore sits in an unusual position within the Ramey memo controversy. His interpretation does not prove that Ramey was holding a newspaper teletype, and parts of his formatting argument have credible objections. What it does provide is a concrete competing reconstruction anchored to documented contemporary press language. The misspelling “Warren Haught” really circulated on 8 July 1947; Greenwood really could map that phrase onto marks other researchers mapped differently. That incompatibility is precisely why the memo cannot safely be treated as a verbatim historical document until a method can distinguish genuine character information from contextual pattern completion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>
