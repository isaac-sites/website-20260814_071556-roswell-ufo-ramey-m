## Introduction

The central problem with the Ramey memo is not simply whether a modern scan has enough pixels. It is **which physical object was scanned, and how many copying stages came before it**. The typed sheet occupies only a small, oblique part of J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})'s 4-by-5-inch press photograph. At that scale, a character may be represented by little more than a fragile pattern of [photographic grain]({{ 'grain-ceiling/' | relative_url }}) and tonal variation. Any reproduction step can therefore matter.

A print made from the camera [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) can lose fine contrast. A copy negative photographs that print again. Microfilm or microfiche adds another optical transfer. A digital scan then samples whatever detail remains. Increasing the resolution of that final scan can represent the surviving copy more precisely, but it cannot reconstruct letter strokes that disappeared during an earlier generation. That distinction is essential when assessing claimed readings of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/duplicationservices/products-pricing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Products &amp; Pricing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressProducts &amp; Pricing - Duplication Services (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

## From Camera Negative to Photographic Print

The strongest starting point is the camera negative because it contains the photographic record produced at the original exposure without an intervening reproduction. Contemporary discussion of the surviving [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) photograph identifies Johnson's camera as a Speed Graphic using 4-by-5-inch film, while researchers who examined the University of Texas at Arlington material have described the relevant film as the original negative rather than a later copy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/05/the-ramey-memo-negative.html?showComment=1462514194505" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo NegativeMay 5, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 5, 2016</span></span></span>

That does not mean the negative contains perfectly formed miniature letters waiting to be uncovered. The memo was not the subject of a close-up document photograph: it was a comparatively small object within a photograph of people, debris and an office scene. Its paper is turned relative to the camera, folded and unevenly illuminated. Some character strokes may therefore have been below the resolving ability of the original camera-film system or become inseparable from grain at the moment of exposure. Later processing cannot create photographic information that was never recorded.

The next generation, a conventional photographic print, introduces a second imaging system. Light passes through the negative and is projected onto photographic paper. Enlarger focus, lens performance, paper characteristics, exposure, development and contrast all influence what reaches the finished print. A print can consequently remain an excellent reproduction of the overall photograph while being measurably worse for a specialised task such as distinguishing tiny type.

This distinction is particularly important for the Ramey memo because ordinary visual quality and evidential usefulness are not the same thing. A print may show Ramey's face, clothing and the debris very clearly while failing to preserve a faint gap between two tiny dark marks that could determine whether an apparent character resembles one letter or another.

Archival digitisation practice illustrates why negatives and prints are treated differently. The Library of Congress distinguishes scans made from original negatives from those made from copy negatives and sets scanning specifications according to the physical source. Its current duplication guidance specifies 1,200 dpi for a 4-by-5-inch negative, while also warning that the quality of a digital copy ultimately depends on the characteristics and condition of the material being copied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/duplicationservices/products-pricing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Products &amp; Pricing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressProducts &amp; Pricing - Duplication Services (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

The Ramey case provides an unusually useful historical test of the [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}). The US Air Force's 1994 Roswell investigation reported obtaining original, first-generation prints from the University of Texas at Arlington and subsequently first-generation negatives. Investigators noticed the apparent writing on the paper in Ramey's hand and sent photographic material for digitisation and interpretation. The unnamed national-level organisation reported on 20 July 1994 that the photographs were still of insufficient quality to resolve the requested details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span>

That finding does not prove that no character can ever be tentatively identified. It does establish something narrower but important: access to an early photographic generation, followed by digitisation, did not automatically turn the memo into reliably readable text.

## What Copy Negatives and Microfilm Can Change

A copy negative is fundamentally different from the camera negative even if both ultimately appear as pieces of photographic film. The original negative records the scene itself. A copy negative records **another photographic reproduction** of that scene, commonly a print. The second camera-and-film stage therefore inherits all information already lost in the print and adds its own limits of focus, film grain, contrast and exposure.

For large features, those differences may be negligible. For the Ramey memo, they can be decisive. Suppose a tiny typed stroke survives on the original negative as a weak difference in density from the surrounding paper. During printing, that difference can be compressed until it is barely visible. When the print is copied again, the weak distinction may merge completely with neighbouring tones. A beautifully focused scan of the resulting copy negative then faithfully records the absence of that distinction.

The Library of Congress provides a real-world example of why researchers need to identify intermediate generations rather than simply asking for the "highest-resolution file". In its digitisation of panoramic photographs, it scanned intermediate 35 mm copy film at about 900 dpi. Yet because that film was itself a reduced reproduction of much larger originals, the effective resolution relative to the source panoramas was only about 50–100 dpi. The scanner's nominal dpi therefore described the sampling of the **intermediate film**, not the amount of original-picture detail surviving through the reproduction chain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/pan/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Panoramic Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressPanoramic Photographs - Digitizing the Collection - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

The same principle applies to microfilm and microfiche. These formats can preserve documents extremely well when properly produced, but they are still photographic reproductions with finite resolving power. Library of Congress preservation research cites microfilm quality benchmarks in which progressively lower film resolution moves small type from high quality, with fine details resolved, through a middle range where fine detail can disappear, towards marginal reproduction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/preservation/resources/rt/guide/guid_exp.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Scanning and Conpression Experiments</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressScanning and Conpression Experiments - Guidelines for Electronic Preservation of Visual Materials - Preservation R...</span></span></span>

For ordinary text, that may mean a serif becomes indistinct while the word remains perfectly readable. The Ramey memo presents a much harsher problem: researchers are not examining a page that filled the microfilm frame. They are interested in already minute type contained inside a photograph, meaning the relevant letter strokes have undergone substantial reduction before the microfilm scanner encounters them.

This creates several possible loss mechanisms in successive generations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Spatial loss:** neighbouring fine features become blended because the copying system cannot resolve them separately.
* **Tonal loss:** faint differences between ink, paper, shadow and photographic grain are compressed or disappear.
* **Grain interaction:** the grain structure of the new photographic generation is superimposed on detail inherited from the previous one.
* **Contrast changes:** copying can make weak marks vanish or make unrelated grain boundaries appear more character-like.
* **Cropping or reduction:** the memo may occupy fewer millimetres, and ultimately fewer useful samples, in an intermediate reproduction.
* **Digital compression and sharpening:** service JPEGs can introduce another distinction between what is genuinely present in an archival master and what merely looks sharper on screen.

</div>

The last point is demonstrable in mainstream archival practice. For its digitisation of the Harris & Ewing photographic negatives, the Library of Congress created uncompressed archival TIFF files without enhancement, including higher-resolution 16-bit greyscale versions, while much smaller online JPEG derivatives were mildly sharpened and compressed at roughly 10:1. These files depict the same underlying photographs but are not evidentially interchangeable for examination of minute structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/hec/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Harris &amp; Ewing Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressHarris &amp; Ewing Collection - Digitizing the Negatives - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

## Why a Later High-Resolution Scan Cannot Restore Earlier Losses

A high-resolution scan can solve one problem very effectively: **undersampling of the physical object presently in the scanner**. If a negative contains genuine fine detail but an earlier scan represented it with too few pixels, rescanning the same negative at an appropriate resolution can capture that surviving structure more faithfully.

This is why direct rescanning of the Ramey camera negative is potentially much more valuable than enlarging an old web image. Researchers involved in a later examination reported scanning the UTA negative and subsequently making the resulting files available for analysis. Kevin Randle's account identifies the Fort Worth Star-Telegram Collection at UTA as the repository and credits the group involved in scanning the negative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-ramey-memo-scans.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Scans</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo ScansNovember 30, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 30, 2015</span></span></span>

But scanner resolution has to be kept conceptually separate from **source resolution**. If a 300-dpi scan failed to sample a surviving letter stroke adequately, a 1,200-dpi scan might reveal it. If the letter stroke disappeared when an original negative was printed and the print subsequently copied, scanning that derivative at 1,200, 2,400 or 10,000 dpi merely produces increasingly precise measurements of the derivative.

The Library of Congress's digitisation work makes this distinction particularly clear. Some of its photographic collections have been scanned directly from original negatives at very high pixel dimensions: for example, Harris & Ewing negatives were captured at up to 10,000 pixels on the long side. By contrast, the Library also documents collections in which photographic prints were digitised from copy negatives. The [provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}) of the digital image is recorded because "digital file from original photo" and "b&w film copy neg." describe materially different reproduction paths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/hec/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Harris &amp; Ewing Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressHarris &amp; Ewing Collection - Digitizing the Negatives - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

Nor can image enhancement circumvent the limitation. Contrast adjustment can spread a narrow range of surviving tones across a wider visible range. Sharpening can emphasise boundaries. Combining exposures can sometimes help separate weak photographic information from an inconvenient tonal background. None of those operations independently proves that a newly prominent edge corresponds to a typewriter stroke rather than grain, copying artefact or noise.

That distinction becomes especially important once researchers begin comparing candidate words. Human vision is extremely effective at recognising familiar patterns in ambiguous material. Once a proposed transcription is supplied, marginal blobs can appear to organise themselves into the expected letters. A processing method that makes one proposed reading look clearer is therefore weaker evidence than a result that repeatedly emerges from the best-generation source under independently specified processing.

## The Air Force Result and Later Scans Are Not Necessarily Contradictory

The 1994 Air Force investigation and later private attempts are sometimes presented as though one must invalidate the other. The Air Force reported that its digitised material was insufficient to visualise the text sought, whereas later researchers have argued that high-resolution scanning permits at least portions of the memo to be interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span>

Copy generation offers one reason to avoid making that comparison too simple. "Digitised" by itself says little about the resulting evidential quality. What matters includes the exact physical source scanned, its generation, scanner sampling, tonal depth, focus, exposure and subsequent processing. The Air Force report says investigators obtained first-generation prints and negatives, but its short public description of the photo-analysis work does not provide enough technical detail to reconstruct every stage of the digitisation pipeline or establish that it was equivalent to later scanning procedures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span>

Conversely, the existence of larger or more sophisticated later files does not establish the correctness of a proposed transcription. A higher-resolution scan can demonstrate that an older digital file omitted information that physically survives on the source negative. It cannot demonstrate that every apparent stroke exposed by enhancement represents an unambiguous letter.

This is the crucial separation between **better reproduction** and **successful recovery of text**. The first can be measured through source provenance, pixel dimensions, tonal depth and controlled imaging. The second requires the surviving photographic signal to discriminate reliably between competing characters and words. The Ramey memo may improve substantially under the first test while remaining uncertain under the second.

## The Copy Chain Sets the Evidential Ceiling

For the Ramey memo, the useful hierarchy is therefore not "new scan beats old scan". It is **source generation first, scanning quality second, interpretation third**.

A direct, carefully controlled scan of the original camera negative is preferable because it eliminates unnecessary copying generations. A high-quality first-generation print can retain substantial information but has already passed through photographic printing. A copy negative inherits the print's losses and introduces another photographic transfer. Microfilm or microfiche may add reduction, contrast and resolving limits. A scan of any of these objects then records only what has survived to that point. Archival practice explicitly distinguishes these source types for precisely this reason.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/duplicationservices/products-pricing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Products &amp; Pricing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressProducts &amp; Pricing - Duplication Services (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

That hierarchy also changes how online enlargements should be judged. A large TIFF derived directly from an original negative can contain more genuine scene information than a smaller service JPEG. But a gigantic TIFF scanned from a poor intermediate copy may contain less original memo information than a much smaller file made directly from the camera negative. Pixel count alone does not reveal photographic ancestry.

The safest interpretation of the Ramey memo evidence is consequently narrower than either "modern technology can recover the message" or "the photograph contains nothing readable". Some letter-like information plainly survives, and better access to early-generation material can prevent unnecessary losses. Yet the Air Force's 1994 analysis found the material insufficient for the details it sought, and the continuing disagreement over later proposed readings shows that digitisation has not converted the whole document into stable, independently agreed text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span>

For any claimed transcription, the first question should therefore come before debates about individual words: **what generation of the Ramey photograph produced the pixels being examined?** If detail was discarded before those pixels were created, no amount of enlargement can put it back.
