## Introduction

The words “Fort Worth” can help establish that the photographed [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) paper concerns the same fast-moving Roswell story unfolding around Brigadier General Roger Ramey’s headquarters on 8 July 1947. They cannot, by themselves, establish who wrote the paper. That distinction matters because two plausible channels could naturally have put “Fort Worth” into the document: internal Army Air Forces communications dealing with material and decisions at [Eighth Air Force]({{ 'command-control/' | relative_url }}) headquarters, or civilian news-wire copy reporting those same developments.

Contemporary Associated Press reporting was already identifying Ramey with Fort Worth and circulating Fort Worth-datelined updates as the military revised its account of the Roswell object. Meanwhile, Ramey’s headquarters was itself in Fort Worth and was receiving the material from Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span> The location is therefore a strong contextual clue but a weak authorship test. Treating “Fort Worth” as proof that the sheet must be a military telegram — or, conversely, a newsroom wire printout — asks the place name to distinguish between two sources that both had good reason to use it.

## Why military traffic could say Fort Worth

The case for a military origin initially looks straightforward. Ramey was commander of the Eighth Air Force, headquartered at Fort Worth Army Air Field, and the material at the centre of the Roswell [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) had been sent from Roswell to his command. Contemporary AP reporting described Ramey as the Eighth Air Force commander at Fort Worth and said that he had received the object from Roswell Army Air Base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span> A photograph taken in Ramey’s office therefore catches the paper at precisely the point where “Fort Worth” would make operational sense in military correspondence.

There are several ways an internal document could have used the location without requiring the most elaborate proposed [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) of the memo. A message could identify where material had been forwarded, where an examination was taking place, where an officer or headquarters was located, or where further instructions were to be carried out. Kevin Randle, a Roswell researcher who has written extensively about the memo, has noted that even if the paper were a base teletype message, that would not automatically make it extraordinary or classified. [Military communications]({{ 'military-communication/' | relative_url }}) centres also handled routine and unclassified traffic, including material that could have summarised press reporting or provided guidance for dealing with enquiries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/ramey-memo-and-victims-of-wreck.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Ramey Memo and Victims of the Wreck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Ramey Memo and Victims of the WreckOctober 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2015</span></span></span>

That is an important restraint on the argument. “Fort Worth” is compatible with an internal military message, but it does not tell us what *kind* of military message it would have been. A routine briefing, press-related communication, operational update and highly sensitive command message could all contain the same place name.

Some interpretations go much further. One widely circulated reconstruction treats the sheet as an Army message from Ramey to [higher headquarters]({{ 'higher-hq/' | relative_url }}) and interprets disputed surrounding words as instructions about material being forwarded through Fort Worth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span></span></span> Those claims depend on readings of many far less secure characters, however. They cannot be derived from “Fort Worth” itself.

The methodological problem is especially important because the photograph does not preserve a clean document. The US Air Force's 1994 investigation reported that specialists who digitised the photograph still found its quality insufficient to recover the details sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span> Houran and Randle's later experimental study likewise found that suggestion substantially affected what observers believed they could read, even though a few fragments showed unusually strong agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> A plausible military context should therefore not be allowed to supply missing words around a relatively credible place name.

## Why civilian news copy could say the same thing

The civilian alternative is unusually plausible because Fort Worth was not merely where the photograph happened to be taken. It had already become a central location in the national news story.

The Roswell announcement travelled rapidly through the Associated Press on 8 July. Contemporary AP copy reported that the object had been sent to “higher headquarters” and identified Ramey in Fort Worth as the senior officer dealing with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span> Later that day, after the object had been identified publicly as balloon equipment, AP circulated an updated report datelined Fort Worth. A surviving account of that wire story begins by saying that Roswell's celebrated flying disc had been identified by a Fort Worth Army airfield weather officer as a weather balloon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/roswell-aliens-fermi-paradox" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">However, an examination reveals a confluence of secret government projects and Cold War era activities rather than extraterrestrial invol...</span></span></span>

In other words, “Fort Worth” is exactly the sort of wording that could occur in news copy independently of any military message. A wire-service story might contain it as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a dateline;
* the location of Ramey or Eighth Air Force headquarters;
* the destination of material sent from Roswell;
* the location where the object was examined;
* or the source of the military's revised explanation.

</div>

That overlap makes the place name intrinsically poor at separating military from journalistic authorship. The two information systems were describing the same events, people and movements. A military message might say that something was sent to Fort Worth; a news report could tell readers that something had been sent to Fort Worth. The geographical noun survives while the provenance changes completely.

The civilian possibility becomes still more relevant because the photographer, J. Bond Johnson of the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram*, gave conflicting accounts of the sheet's provenance. In an early account discussed by Randle, Johnson said that he had brought a news-wire “flash” with him and handed it to Ramey. He later withdrew or altered that version and said instead that the document came from Ramey's desk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/10/ufos-ramey-memo-j-bond-johnson-and-me.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: UFOs, the Ramey Memo, J. Bond Johnson and MeOctober 9, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 9, 2009</span></span></span> A recent specialist review of the dispute likewise describes the two incompatible versions: a newsroom wire printout carried to the base, versus a document already present in Ramey's office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo THE PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoTHE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

Neither recollection can simply be converted into proof. Johnson was reconstructing the episode many decades later, and the changes in his account are themselves a reason to avoid assigning provenance from testimony alone. Randle, despite favouring Johnson's earlier version, concluded that the photographer's contradictory recollections leave researchers with considerable uncertainty about exactly how the paper entered the photograph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/10/ufos-ramey-memo-j-bond-johnson-and-me.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: UFOs, the Ramey Memo, J. The surviving archival trail does securely identify Johnson's photographs, rather than the paper's author. The University of Texas at Arlington holds the relevant material within its *Fort Worth Star-Telegram* photographic collection and describes the object simply as the Ramey memo photographed in his hand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span></span></span> That tells us where the photographic evidence comes from; it does not establish where the photographed document came from.

## Fort Worth cannot settle the authorship dispute

The most useful way to evaluate the location clue is to separate three propositions that are often allowed to blur together.

**First, the reading may genuinely be “Fort Worth”.** Houran and Randle's controlled experiment provides unusually interesting support for that possibility. Participants were divided among a UFO-related condition, an unrelated atomic-testing condition and a condition receiving no suggested subject. Despite those different expectations, “Fort Worth TX” was among the fragments repeatedly identified in the same area of the image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span> That makes the location more interesting than a phrase recovered only after a reader has been told what to expect.

**Second, a correct location can help identify the document's subject matter.** Fort Worth connects naturally with the events of 8 July: Ramey commanded there, the Roswell material was taken there, journalists converged on the headquarters story, and news services issued reports concerning developments there. Contemporary reporting independently confirms those connections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span> The fragment therefore strengthens the case that at least this portion of the photographed text belongs to the immediate Roswell/Fort Worth news environment rather than being an unrelated sheet accidentally caught in the photograph.

**Third, however, subject matter is not authorship.** This is where the inference must stop. A document about the Roswell episode could have been generated inside the Army Air Forces, received through military communications, prepared for Ramey's dealings with journalists, or printed from civilian wire traffic. Every one of those routes could produce a document containing “Fort Worth”.

The distinction also exposes a circular argument sometimes made about the memo. If a researcher first assumes that the sheet is an internal military telegram, “Fort Worth” can appear to confirm a reconstruction involving the transfer of material to headquarters. If another researcher first assumes that Johnson carried an AP bulletin into the room, the same words can appear to confirm a civilian wire story. In both cases, however, the location is being interpreted through the provenance hypothesis rather than independently testing it.

The problem cannot be solved merely by adding disputed words around the secure fragment. Proposed readings claiming signatures, addressees, military abbreviations or distinctive journalistic phrasing would be genuinely useful *if independently established*, because those features could discriminate between document types. But that is exactly where the photographic limitations become decisive. The original Air Force examination could not extract sufficient detail, and subsequent researchers have produced substantially different transcriptions from the same small, grain-obscured characters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

Even Randle's discussion of possible origins illustrates the range left open by the evidence: he considered a draft press statement, a message received through the base communications centre, a summary of media reporting or another document associated with Ramey's handling of the press.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/ramey-memo-and-victims-of-wreck.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Ramey Memo and Victims of the Wreck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Ramey Memo and Victims of the WreckOctober 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2015</span></span></span> These possibilities differ sharply in provenance while sharing essentially the same historical setting.

## What would actually identify the paper's origin?

A genuine provenance test would require evidence that distinguishes military traffic from civilian copy rather than merely locating the story in Fort Worth. The most valuable features would be structural ones: a reliably readable sender or recipient, message-routing information, an authenticated signature block, military communications abbreviations used in an identifiable format, a news-service identifier, a dateline matching known wire copy, or a substantial run of text that could be matched independently against a surviving AP, INS or newspaper transmission.

That standard is deliberately higher than recognising individual topical words. It asks whether the document contains a pattern that one candidate source would be expected to produce and the other would not.

The distinction is particularly useful when assessing claims that the memo has already been identified as a telegram from Ramey. Some advocates argue that proposed readings such as a Ramey signature, second-person wording and military terminology rule out civilian news copy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo</span></span></span> Those are potentially discriminating features, unlike “Fort Worth”, but their evidential force is only as good as the underlying transcription. The photograph's limited resolution means that disputed readings cannot safely be treated as established text and then used to prove the document type from which the reconstruction was originally inferred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The same caution applies in the opposite direction. Johnson's recollection that he brought a wire-service flash into Ramey's office makes a civilian origin possible, but it does not demonstrate that the visible sheet is that particular piece of paper. His later account placed the photographed document in Ramey's office instead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo THE PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoTHE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span> Without an independently identifiable feature on the document, neither memory resolves the physical provenance.

## The location clue is stronger than the provenance clue

“Fort Worth” is valuable because it appears to be one of the comparatively resilient fragments in an exceptionally difficult photograph. It connects the sheet with a place unquestionably central to the Roswell story on 8 July 1947, and its recovery by readers exposed to different contextual suggestions makes it harder to dismiss as nothing more than a UFO-related expectation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Its value nevertheless has a clear ceiling. Fort Worth was simultaneously an Army Air Forces headquarters, the destination of the Roswell material, the location of Ramey's public response and a dateline in rapidly circulating civilian coverage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/seattledailytimes8jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: The Roswell 1947 incident in the Press, The Seattle Daily Times, July 8, 1947July 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span> The military and the press were therefore using the same geographical vocabulary at the same moment.

That is why the safest conclusion is narrower than either side of the authorship debate would prefer. The [Fort Worth wording]({{ 'fort-worth/' | relative_url }}) can strengthen confidence that part of the photographed paper belongs to the immediate Roswell/Fort Worth information environment. It cannot tell us whether the person who composed that text wore a uniform, worked at a military communications centre, prepared material for Ramey's press response, or wrote for a civilian news service. To establish the paper's origin, researchers need a feature unique enough to separate those channels. “Fort Worth”, precisely because it fits all of them so well, is not that feature.
