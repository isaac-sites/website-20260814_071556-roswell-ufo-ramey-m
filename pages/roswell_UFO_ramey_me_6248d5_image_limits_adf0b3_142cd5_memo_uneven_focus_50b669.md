## Introduction

The [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) was not photographed as a flat document. In J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})’s 8 July 1947 photograph, Brigadier General Roger Ramey is holding the sheet in his hand, with the paper visibly bent rather than arranged parallel to the camera. That geometry matters at the scale on which researchers are trying to identify individual typewritten characters: different parts of the same sheet can lie at slightly different distances from the lens, and therefore need not have exactly the same degree of focus.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_image_limits_adf0b3_142cd5_memo_uneven_focus_50b669-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Uneven Focus illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_image_limits_adf0b3_142cd5_memo_uneven_focus_50b669-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_image_limits_adf0b3_142cd5_memo_uneven_focus_50b669-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

The consequence is easy to miss. A photograph can make the whole memo look broadly “in focus” while preserving some tiny letter strokes better than others. Depth of field describes a range judged acceptably sharp; it does not mean every point within that range carries identical fine-detail resolution. Sharpness falls progressively as subject points depart from the distance of best focus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[Lenspire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

For the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo, this means uneven legibility across the sheet is physically plausible. It also means that success in reading one relatively favourable line cannot automatically establish that neighbouring, more defocused text is equally recoverable.

## How a bent sheet spans several subject distances

A flat document photographed straight-on presents a comparatively simple focusing problem: most of its printed surface is approximately the same distance from the camera. Ramey’s memo did not have that advantage. The surviving photograph shows a document held incidentally during the press photography associated with the Roswell [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}), rather than a document positioned for reproduction. The University of Texas at Arlington Libraries preserves the relevant photographic material and has produced high-resolution scans specifically because the memo remains an object of attempted decipherment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.libraries.uta.edu/roswell/ramey-memo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.libraries.uta.edu">[sites.libraries.uta.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.libraries.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nt with negatives and in particular the &quot;memo.&quot;...</span></span></span>

Once a sheet curves towards and away from the camera, points that would occupy one plane if the sheet were flat instead occupy a shallow three-dimensional surface. The difference may be modest in ordinary photographic terms, but the transcription problem is unusually demanding. Researchers are not merely asking whether the paper looks recognisable; they are asking whether tiny marks distinguish, for example, one typewritten letter from another.

Optically, exact focus corresponds to a particular subject distance. Points nearer or farther away form progressively larger blur spots at the image plane. ZEISS describes these as circles of confusion: material precisely at the selected distance receives the best focus, while material displaced towards or away from the camera is recorded with increasing defocus. Crucially, the change is continuous rather than a sudden boundary between “focused” and “unfocused”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[Lenspire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

Applied to the memo, the practical geometry is therefore:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a section of paper nearest the camera can sit on one side of the optimum focus distance;
* another section can pass closer to the best-focus distance;
* a portion bending away can lie on the other side;
* folds and local curvature can change that relationship again within a relatively short run of text.

</div>

This does **not** prove which particular Ramey memo line was at best focus. The historical photograph does not provide enough information to reconstruct the precise three-dimensional shape of the paper, lens focus setting and aperture with confidence. It establishes a more limited but important point: treating the entire memo as though every line had been photographed at one identical subject distance is not warranted by the visible geometry.

## Depth of field is not letter-level resolution

The phrase “within the depth of field” can give a misleading impression of uniform sharpness. Photographic depth of field is conventionally a zone of *acceptable* sharpness, defined using an allowable blur criterion. It is not a slab of space within which optical performance remains constant. Cambridge in Colour notes that everything immediately in front of or behind the focused distance begins losing sharpness, even when that deterioration is too small to be obvious at normal viewing size.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridgeincolour.com/tutorials/depth-of-field.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeincolour.com">[Cambridge in Colour]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeincolour.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridge in Colour Understanding Depth of Field in Photography</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge in Colour Understanding Depth of Field in Photography</span></span></span>

That distinction becomes particularly important when a photograph is enlarged far beyond its original viewing purpose. ZEISS points out that a region appearing sharp in an ordinary photograph can reveal blur when examined at high magnification, because the acceptable circle-of-confusion criterion depends partly on viewing conditions and enlargement. Cropped or greatly enlarged details therefore impose a more demanding sharpness requirement than the whole photograph viewed normally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[Lenspire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

The Ramey memo is an extreme example of that change in task. Nobody needs much resolution to recognise that Ramey is holding a sheet of paper. Reading its typewriting demands vastly finer discrimination. A letter may depend on whether a short stroke is present, whether two dark features join, or whether a small gap separates them. Slight defocus that is irrelevant to recognising the sheet can be decisive when distinguishing such character features.

Technical imaging literature expresses depth of field in essentially the same way. Edmund Optics defines it in relation to maintaining a specified level of image quality — including spatial frequency at a specified contrast — as object distance changes. In other words, the useful depth of field depends partly on *what detail must actually be resolved*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.edmundoptics.com/knowledge-center/application-notes/testing-and-detection/gauging-depth-of-field-in-your-imaging-system/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edmundoptics.com">[Edmund Optics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edmundoptics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Edmund Optics Gauging Depth of Field in Your Imaging System &#124; Edmund Optics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Edmund Optics Gauging Depth of Field in Your Imaging System &#124; Edmund Optics</span></span></span>

This is why the statement “the memo is in focus” is too coarse to settle the transcription question. For ordinary pictorial viewing, much of it may indeed qualify as acceptably sharp. The relevant question is narrower: **at each location on the bent sheet, was focus sufficiently precise to preserve the spatial detail needed to distinguish the original typewriter strokes?**

Those are different standards.

## Why neighbouring lines can have unequal transcription value

The strongest evidence that memo legibility is not uniform comes from the transcription work itself. James Houran and Kevin Randle's 2002 study gave participants an enlarged print and access to four high-resolution versions of the memo area, including material derived from the Fort Worth Star-Telegram collection at UTA. Despite that relatively favourable source material, interpretations remained highly variable. Yet a small number of words in the same locations were perceived consistently across different experimental groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That result does not demonstrate that paper curvature caused those particular words to be clearer. Other factors — character shapes, contrast, [film grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}), exposure, reproduction history and reader expectations — also affect apparent legibility. It does, however, show why a single whole-document label such as “readable” or “unreadable” is inadequate. Houran and Randle found evidence consistent with **local islands of greater legibility inside an otherwise highly ambiguous document**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Bent-sheet geometry supplies one plausible physical contributor to such local variation. Suppose one line happens to cross a part of the sheet relatively near the camera's best-focus distance. Its already tiny character strokes receive less defocus blur. A line farther along the curve may be only slightly nearer or farther from the lens, yet its strokes acquire somewhat larger blur spots. At normal size the difference may be invisible. After extreme enlargement, the first line can retain a little more usable edge information.

This effect should not be exaggerated. The memo's curvature is not a magic explanation for every apparent difference between lines, nor does it turn the sharper regions into clean text. Even perfect focus cannot restore detail limited by character size on the negative, film grain, motion, lens performance or damage. Focus is one link in a chain of limitations.

The key asymmetry is that **local good focus cannot compensate for other missing information, whereas local defocus can destroy information that might otherwise have survived**. A relatively well-focused character may still be undecipherable. A sufficiently defocused character has an additional obstacle before transcription even begins.

## Why enlargement makes the unevenness more important

Modern high-resolution scanning changes the visibility of the photographic record, not the optical conditions that created it. UTA's Ramey Memo project has used high-resolution and high-dynamic-range imaging to capture as much tonal information as possible from the surviving negatives. That is valuable because it reduces losses introduced by later copying and gives investigators better access to what the film actually recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.libraries.uta.edu/roswell/ramey-memo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.libraries.uta.edu">[sites.libraries.uta.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.libraries.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nt with negatives and in particular the &quot;memo.&quot;...</span></span></span>

But scanning cannot refocus the 1947 scene. If a stroke was spread by optical defocus when Johnson exposed the negative, a finer scan can describe that blurred photographic structure more precisely; it cannot recreate the original sharp edge as though the paper had occupied the correct focal distance.

This is particularly relevant because the Ramey memo is routinely examined as a heavily enlarged crop. ZEISS's treatment of depth of field explicitly warns that conventional acceptable-sharpness assumptions become less adequate under demanding enlargement: details that appear satisfactorily sharp in a whole image can look blurred when magnified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[Lenspire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

That produces an important paradox in memo research. Better scans can make differences in local image quality *more obvious* without necessarily making the underlying text proportionately more readable. One area may reveal recognisable character structure, while another resolves mainly into diffuse tonal marks and grain. The scanner has treated both areas equally; the 1947 optical image did not necessarily do so.

## What uneven focus means for attempted transcription

A defensible transcription method should therefore treat the memo as a collection of locally different image regions, not as a uniformly legible page. Confidence earned in one area should not simply be transferred to another.

Houran and Randle's experiment is instructive here because participants did not converge on one complete message even when supplied with several high-resolution representations and magnification. Their findings instead combined substantial disagreement with limited agreement at particular locations. They concluded that the document remained inherently ambiguous and recommended more systematic testing, including photographing known period-style messages at different distances and angles as controls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For the bent-sheet problem, that recommendation is especially relevant. A useful controlled reconstruction would not merely reproduce the approximate camera and typewriter. It would photograph known text on paper held with comparable folds, curvature and orientation. Investigators could then ask whether transcription accuracy changes systematically from one part of the curved sheet to another, and whether image-processing techniques preserve genuine characters or encourage false ones.

Until such controls establish otherwise, three distinctions matter when assessing claimed Ramey readings. A proposed word in a locally favourable region may deserve more consideration than one assembled from poorly defined marks elsewhere. Agreement on a relatively clear word does not validate an entire line reconstructed from weaker material. And enhancement that makes two regions look similarly bold does not establish that they contained equal original resolution.

## The strongest conclusion is local, not global

Bent-sheet depth differences do not prove or disprove any particular interpretation of the Ramey memo's contents. Their importance is methodological. The visible paper geometry gives a sound photographic reason to expect the document's tiny typewritten details to have been recorded with somewhat unequal focus, while optical theory explains why even points inside a conventional depth-of-field range do not possess identical fine-detail sharpness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[zeiss.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

That helps make sense of one of the memo's persistent features: researchers can find some areas more promising than others without the photograph ever becoming a uniformly readable document. Houran and Randle's experiments likewise found a few repeatedly perceived words amid much broader ambiguity, although their work cannot isolate focus as the cause of those local differences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The safest reading principle is consequently a local one. Each proposed character or word has to stand on the photographic information available **at that particular position on the sheet**. The apparent clarity of a better-focused section cannot supply missing strokes to a neighbouring section, and enlarging both to the same digital scale does not erase the difference. For a curved memo photographed incidentally in Ramey's hand, “some lines look sharper” and “the memo is decipherable” are very different claims.
