## Introduction

The short answer is: **possibly, but not securely**. Several experienced [Ramey-memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) interpreters have reported seeing quotation marks around the disputed word read as “DISC” or “DISK”, and David Rudiak makes the punctuation part of his reconstruction. Barry [Greenwood]({{ 'greenwood/' | relative_url }}), despite disagreeing with much of the extraordinary interpretation of the memo, has also said that the relevant word appears to him to be in quotes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That agreement makes the marks worth examining. It does **not**, however, turn them into independent confirmation that the letters between them spell DISC. The memo survives only as a small, obliquely photographed piece of paper, and even high-quality scans leave much of its text ambiguous. The same blur, grain, letter fragments and photographic artefacts that complicate identification of the word also complicate identification of punctuation beside it. The safest conclusion is therefore narrower: **quote-like marks may flank the disputed character group, but neither their identity nor their evidential meaning is established strongly enough to authenticate “DISC”.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why quotation marks strengthen the proposed reading

[Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }})'s reconstruction does not merely identify four isolated letters. It renders the relevant passage as something resembling **“IN THE ‘DISC’”**, making the punctuation part of a coherent textual interpretation. Contemporary summaries of his work likewise reproduce the word in quotation marks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/santafenewmexican22nov2002.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: the newspapers, David Rudiak&#x27;s work on Ramey memo, 2002November 23, 2002...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 23, 2002</span></span></span>

There is a historically plausible reason why *disc* might have received such treatment in July 1947. “Flying disc” was then a conspicuous and relatively novel expression in the rapidly developing flying-saucer [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}). Period newspaper coverage demonstrably placed *disc* or *flying disc* in quotation marks in some contexts. Rudiak consequently argues that “disc” could have appeared in what would now commonly be called scare quotes — punctuation signalling an unusual, recently coined or qualified use of a word. His defence of the transcription explicitly appeals to this contemporary usage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

That gives the proposed punctuation a certain internal plausibility. If the central marks really are D-I-S-C, small marks immediately before and after them could reasonably be quotation marks. Conversely, if quotation marks really can be identified independently, they make an unusual term such as *disc* somewhat more attractive than an ordinary unquoted word.

But notice the circularity risk. The interpreter who first thinks the central shapes resemble DISC has a reason to interpret nearby specks as quotation marks. Once those specks have been classified as quotation marks, their presence can then be cited as additional evidence that the central shapes really say DISC. Unless one part of that chain can be established independently, the two judgements are not genuinely separate pieces of evidence.

## Are the marks actually visible?

There is more support for the punctuation claim than might be assumed from Rudiak's reconstruction alone. Greenwood's examination is particularly relevant because he has been critical of attempts to extract an extraordinary Roswell narrative from the memo. In his discussion of the document, he nevertheless reported that several interpreters agreed on a word resembling “disc” or “disk” in quotes, adding that it appeared that way to him as well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

Houran and Randle's 2002 paper also records quotation marks as part of some proposed [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}). More importantly, the authors did not treat Rudiak's interpretation as established ground truth. Their central concern was precisely how suggestion and expectation affect attempts to decipher the degraded document. They describe Rudiak as reasoning that “disk” on the fifth line apparently appeared in quotes and that other apparently quoted terms suggested a consistent style. They regarded that contextual reasoning as reasonable in principle but warned that the supposedly unusual usages themselves remained unverified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That distinction is crucial. Saying **“several interpreters perceive quote-like marks here”** is defensible. Saying **“the photograph objectively establishes quotation marks here”** goes further than the available evidence warrants.

The underlying image simply does not provide clean typographical detail. Houran and Randle worked with an enlargement and several high-resolution versions derived from the Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* photograph collection, including material regarded at the time as among the best raw data available. Yet they still characterised the memo as extremely difficult to decipher and found that interpretation was influenced by suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002...</span></span></span>

There is an even more fundamental warning in the earlier official examination. The US Air Force reported that a national-level organisation had digitised the photograph in an effort to extract useful text but judged its quality insufficient to visualise the details sought. ## How punctuation can emerge from a degraded photograph

Quotation marks are unusually vulnerable in this kind of problem because they contain very little visual information. A capital letter has several strokes whose relationships can sometimes be compared with a typeface. A quotation mark may be represented by little more than one or two tiny dark shapes.

In the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) photograph those shapes have passed through several stages before reaching a modern viewer: type on the original sheet, photographic projection onto film, grain and focus limitations, the angle and curvature of the paper, reproduction or scanning, enlargement, and sometimes digital processing. At the end of that chain, a genuine punctuation mark may disappear — but an unrelated dark feature can also acquire the appearance of punctuation.

That is why enhancement cannot simply manufacture certainty. Enlargement can make a feature easier to inspect, and contrast manipulation can make faint tonal differences conspicuous, but neither creates original spatial detail that the photograph failed to record. Different processing choices can make borderline marks look stronger, weaker, joined or separated. The continued disagreement among people working from versions of the same photograph is therefore more informative than the apparent clarity of any single enhanced rendition. Houran and Randle specifically describe the memo as an ambiguous stimulus and argue that expectations influenced interpretation even though some portions of the document showed genuine agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The punctuation presents an additional problem: **the interpreter already knows where quotation marks ought to be** once a candidate word has been proposed. If DISC seems likely, two small marks in roughly the expected positions suddenly become meaningful. Without that lexical hypothesis, the identical marks might be dismissed as noise, fragments of neighbouring characters or photographic texture.

This does not mean the quotation marks are imaginary. It means their evidential status must be assessed under the same ambiguity rules as the letters themselves.

## The punctuation creates another problem for the memo

There is an intriguing complication that cuts in a different direction. Greenwood argued that quotation marks, commas and full stops were comparatively unusual in military teletype traffic of the period, while being much more normal in journalistic wire copy. In his examination of comparison documents, he therefore treated the apparent punctuation in the Ramey sheet as potentially relevant to identifying what sort of message Ramey was holding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

This matters because accepting the quotation marks does not automatically strengthen every part of the “secret military memo mentioning a disc” hypothesis. If the punctuation really is there, it may support the reading of a quoted unusual term; at the same time, according to Greenwood's argument, abundant punctuation could make the document look **less like terse military teletype traffic and more like journalistic material**. Greenwood noted that photographer J. Bond Johnson had at one point described the paper as a flash message associated with the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram*, although the document's identity has itself remained disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

That does not prove a journalistic origin either. Military communications could contain punctuation, particularly where quoted material was involved, and the precise nature and provenance of the sheet have not been established from the photograph. The useful point is narrower: the quote marks cannot legitimately be treated as evidence pointing in only one direction.

Indeed, Rudiak and Greenwood illustrate the interpretive fork particularly well. Both can accept something resembling a quoted DISC/DISK while drawing very different conclusions from the wider document. Rudiak regards the phrase as part of a highly consequential Roswell message; Greenwood regards punctuation and parallels with contemporary news copy as reasons to consider a much more mundane explanation for the sheet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Casebook Roswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey Memo</span></span></span>

## Why the quotes cannot independently prove DISC

The strongest way to test the punctuation claim would be to separate it from the word claim. An examiner who did not know the proposed transcription would need to identify, from the rawest available image, two marks as quotation marks at the same positions. Independent examiners would ideally reproduce that identification without being told that DISC was expected between them. The letters could then be analysed separately.

That kind of separation matters because the Houran–Randle experiment demonstrated the broader danger of contextual priming. Participants were divided into groups given different stories about what the document supposedly concerned, including Roswell, atomic-bomb testing and no suggested context. The study found that portions of the memo appeared genuinely recoverable, but that interpretation and inferred meaning were also influenced by suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Significantly, their experiment did **not** establish Rudiak's DISC reconstruction as one of the reliably recovered Roswell-specific readings. The researchers' broader recommendation was effectively replication under conditions designed to minimise priming and contextual contamination. That standard becomes particularly important when analysing features as tiny as quotation marks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There is also no clean logical shortcut from punctuation to vocabulary. Even if two quotation marks were accepted beyond reasonable dispute, they would establish only that **some expression was quoted**. They would not tell us what the intervening letters were. DISC, DISK or another character sequence would still have to succeed on its own visual evidence.

Likewise, establishing DISC would not by itself establish the punctuation. The propositions are related but distinct:

## What can reasonably be said

The quotation-mark claim is stronger than a purely idiosyncratic detail invented by one transcription. Multiple researchers have perceived a quoted word at approximately the disputed location, including Greenwood, whose broader interpretation differs substantially from Rudiak's. That makes the apparent punctuation a legitimate feature for further examination rather than something that should simply be ignored.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.scribd.com/document/314034746/2004-I-11-An-Observation-on-the-Ramey-Memo-ed-Barry-Greenwood-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fr.scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fr.scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of the Ramey Memo &#124; PDF &#124; Travel &#124; Young Adult...</span></span></span>

But the photographic limitation is decisive when assigning confidence. The quotation marks occupy the same degraded source image as DISC itself; they are not corroborating evidence from another document, another photograph or an independently readable line. The controlled work on the memo shows both that some real textual information survives and that expectation can steer interpretation of its ambiguous portions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

So the most careful transcription would preserve the uncertainty rather than silently promoting the punctuation to fact: **something resembling quotation marks may surround a character group that several researchers read as DISC or DISK**. Writing the phrase confidently as **“the ‘DISC’”** is useful when accurately reporting Rudiak's proposed reconstruction, but it should not be mistaken for a literal, securely deciphered quotation from the photographed memo.

The apparent quotes therefore add interest to the disputed [DISC reading]({{ 'disc-reading/' | relative_url }}), but not independent confirmation. They remain another interpretation of the same few blurred marks — and that is precisely why they cannot carry more certainty than the image itself permits.
