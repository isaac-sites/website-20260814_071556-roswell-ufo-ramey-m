## Introduction

A higher-resolution scan of the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) photograph can be a better record of the surviving [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) without being a better record of the words on the paper. That distinction is central to the Roswell memo problem. Once a scanner samples the negative finely enough, extra pixels increasingly describe what physically exists in the photographic medium itself: grain structure, density variations, scratches, dust and the softened boundaries produced by the original camera-and-film system. They cannot reconstruct page detail that the 1947 exposure never resolved.

This is not merely a theoretical warning. The Library of Congress explicitly distinguishes capturing the subject content of a photographic negative from capturing its physical structure, including [film grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}). Federal digitisation guidance likewise treats sampling resolution, sharpness and noise as separate image-quality properties. Applied to the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), the lesson is straightforward: a finer scan may make the negative more faithfully inspectable while making the memo look busier rather than more legible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/collections/fsa-owi-black-and-white-negatives/about-this-collection/technical-information/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressTechnical Information &#124; About this Collection &#124; Farm Security Administration/Office of War Information Black-and-W...</span></span></span>

## Sampling the negative is not resolving the page

The Ramey memo survives photographically, not as the original sheet. The relevant evidence is a small document within a press photograph taken on 8 July 1947; the original Fort Worth Star-Telegram negative is held by the University of Texas at Arlington. The problem is therefore a chain of reproduction: typewritten marks existed on paper; those marks were projected through the camera lens onto film; the developed negative preserved a finite photographic representation; and modern scanners subsequently sampled that representation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Original Fort Worth Star-Telegram negative now at University of Texas at Arlington Special Collections division.... Roger Ramey&#x27;s Memo &#124;...</span></span></span>

Each stage has a different meaning for “resolution”. Increasing scanner resolution affects only the last stage. It gives the digital file more samples across the physical negative. It does not retroactively increase the resolving power of the lens, improve the focus of the 1947 exposure or enlarge the amount of film area that originally represented each typewritten character.

This distinction is built into professional digitisation practice. Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative (FADGI) guidance for 4-by-5-inch film has historically specified thousands of pixels across the negative — roughly 1,600 pixels per inch in one published set of recommendations for 4-by-5 material — because an archival master should represent the photographic source well. The Library of Congress similarly lists 1,200 dpi as its standard high-resolution reproduction specification for 4-by-5 negatives. Those figures describe the digitisation of the film object; they are not promises that every tiny object photographed on that film will become readable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/ccdo-subcat-AT2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesGuidelines: Content Categories and Subcategories Objectives: Reformatting Historical Printed Matter, Documents and...</span></span></span>

The distinction can be expressed as three separate [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}):


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Digital sampling limit:** how finely the scanner measures the negative.
* **Photographic limit:** how much spatial information the original lens, focus, exposure and emulsion recorded.
* **Subject-detail limit:** how many of those recorded distinctions actually fall across something as small as a memo character.

</div>

Raising the first limit helps only until the second becomes dominant. Once the negative itself has been adequately sampled, adding pixels cannot recreate spatial information that never reached the emulsion as distinguishable detail.

FADGI's use of Modulation Transfer Function, or MTF, helps explain why nominal pixel count is not the same thing as usable resolving power. MTF describes how well an imaging system preserves contrast at different spatial frequencies; in practical terms, fine alternating detail becomes progressively harder to reproduce distinctly. A scanner can therefore produce a very large pixel array while the underlying photographic information at the scale of a tiny letter remains weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/term.php?term=modulationtransferfunction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Modulation Transfer Function</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization GuidelinesModulation Transfer Function - Glossary - Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines Initiative...</span></span></span>

## Why a better scan can reveal more grain

At ordinary reproduction size, film grain tends to merge perceptually into photographic tone. Under extreme enlargement, that same texture becomes visible as local light-and-dark structure. A sufficiently good scan is supposed to reproduce structures present on the negative rather than silently inventing a smoother surface.

The Library of Congress provides an unusually clear comparison. When planning the rescanning of its Farm Security Administration and Office of War Information black-and-white negatives, it stated that the objective was to capture the subject content, including fine detail and tonal range, but *not* to capture the negative's physical or structural properties such as film grain, discoloration and deterioration. That distinction is important precisely because those are different digitisation goals. A scan optimised to reproduce ever-finer properties of the negative can begin recording structures that belong to the carrier of the image rather than to the photographed subject.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/collections/fsa-owi-black-and-white-negatives/about-this-collection/technical-information/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressTechnical Information &#124; About this Collection &#124; Farm Security Administration/Office of War Information Black-and-W...</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, this creates an unusual difficulty. A landscape photograph can tolerate visible grain because nobody is trying to decide whether a particular microscopic density variation is the edge of a typed “R” or “B”. Here, however, the memo occupies only a small part of the frame, so enlargement brings photographic texture towards the same apparent scale as candidate character features.

That means a new high-resolution scan can genuinely contain more information than an older scan while adding little or no **memo information**. The extra information may consist of a more accurate rendering of:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* fine variations in the emulsion;
* the edges and clustering of visible grain structure;
* tiny scratches or accumulated surface marks;
* subtle density fluctuations in otherwise featureless portions of the photographed sheet;
* better sampled versions of already blurred character boundaries.

</div>

This is why “” needs a qualifier. **about the negative** is not necessarily **about the original typed page**.

The historical record of attempts to read the memo illustrates the problem. Houran and Randle reported that earlier microscope examination found vague word-like structure but little ability to identify individual letters reliably. They also quoted a 1994 government effort in which the photograph was digitised and subjected to photo interpretation, but was judged insufficient to visualise the details being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

By the time of their 2002 study, substantially better material was available. Participants were given an enlarged print together with four high-resolution images described by the authors as state-of-the-art scans: a negative representation of the memo region and several enlargements made from Fort Worth Star-Telegram material. Yet the existence of these improved reproductions did not turn the memo into straightforward text. Different proposed readings still diverged considerably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That historical progression matters. If scanner pixel count were the principal bottleneck, successive improvements in scanning should eventually have produced a sharp transition towards stable letter recognition. Instead, the problem persisted even as the reproduction technology improved. That does not prove that no additional wording can ever be recovered, but it is consistent with the limiting information already being weak in the photographic source.

## Grain can become more conspicuous as sampling improves

There is a further complication: film grain does not interact with digital sampling in a perfectly intuitive way. At inadequate sampling rates, fine structures can be misrepresented through aliasing. At finer sampling rates, a scanner may reproduce the irregular photographic texture more faithfully. The visual result can therefore change substantially even when the underlying negative has not changed.

In practical film scanning, apparent graininess is consequently not a simple yardstick for image detail. Specialist imaging discussion distinguishes actual film grain from scanner noise and grain aliasing; the latter can make grain structure appear coarser or otherwise different because the scanner is inadequately sampling high-frequency information. A higher sampling rate can reduce such sampling artefacts while simultaneously making genuine fine film structure more visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.normankoren.com/Tutorials/MTF8.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: normankoren.com">[Norman Koren Photography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">normankoren.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Norman Koren Photography Grain and sharpness in scans and enlarger prints</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Norman Koren PhotographyGrain and sharpness in scans and enlarger printsNovember 23, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 23, 2003</span></span></span>

This gives the Ramey problem two opposite failure modes. An insufficient scan can distort fine photographic texture, but an excellent scan can preserve that texture so successfully that extreme enlargement offers the eye a dense field of genuine yet textually irrelevant structure.

The important question is therefore not whether a particular dark fleck is “real”. It may be entirely real as a feature of the negative. The question is whether it is evidence of a character stroke produced by ink on Ramey's sheet.

That distinction is easily lost once an image has been magnified. On a computer display, a tiny patch of emulsion can occupy dozens of screen pixels. The enlargement makes its shape easier to inspect, but it does not establish its origin. Grain-related density structure can be enlarged just as effectively as the latent photographic image of a typed stroke.

Sharpening makes the distinction still more important. FADGI defines sharpening as amplification of spatial-frequency response through image processing to make an image appear sharper, and its technical guidelines explicitly measure sharpening separately from other quality characteristics. A crisp-looking enlarged edge is therefore not automatically evidence that the original negative contained a correspondingly well-resolved edge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/HIGHLIGHTED_Technical%20Guidelines%20for%20Digitizing%20Cultural%20Heritage%20Materials_3rd%20Edition_05092023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Technical Guidelines for Digitizing...</span></span></span>

## The Ramey scans show why file size is a poor test

The history of Ramey-memo reproduction offers a useful control against the assumption that the newest or largest scan must contain the best textual evidence. Houran and Randle described Stanton Friedman's CD-ROM scans as, to their knowledge, the best raw data then available, but noted [competing claims]({{ 'competing-claims/' | relative_url }}) that still newer scans were superior. Crucially, they said they knew of no empirical studies validating that claimed superiority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is exactly the distinction required here. “Higher resolution” can mean several different things: more scanner samples per inch, a larger output file, better optical resolving power, better focus on the negative, improved tonal precision, or genuinely increased recovery of subject information. These are not interchangeable.

Professional archival practice demonstrates the same principle at a larger scale. The Library of Congress has digitised some negative collections at very high pixel dimensions — its Harris & Ewing glass-plate project, for example, captured negatives at 10,000 pixels along the long dimension during scanning. Such specifications make sense for preservation because an archival master should leave ample information for future uses. They do not imply that every object inside every photographed scene is resolved to the same microscopic standard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/hec/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Harris &amp; Ewing Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressHarris &amp; Ewing Collection - Digitizing the Negatives - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

For the Ramey photograph, a hypothetical scan twice as dense in each direction would unquestionably supply more digital samples. But four times as many pixels would not mean four times as much evidence about the memo. If neighbouring new pixels are primarily describing smooth blur and increasingly fine emulsion structure, the file has gained measurements without gaining an equivalent number of independent clues about letter identity.

This is the central reason that pixel dimensions alone cannot settle disputes over readings such as the frequently proposed “victims of the wreck”. The 2002 study recorded sharply differing [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) of the same regions despite access to high-resolution source material. That disagreement is evidence that the underlying marks remained ambiguous enough to support competing interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## How to tell scan fidelity from subject detail

A useful test of a Ramey-memo scan should therefore ask what survives across independent representations rather than which enhancement produces the most persuasive-looking letters. Genuine subject detail should have stronger invariance than accidental texture.

**First, compare independent scans of the same negative.** A proposed character edge should remain in the same physical location and retain broadly compatible geometry when the negative is captured with different equipment or sampling conditions. Differences caused by scanner sampling, sharpening or noise are less likely to behave consistently.

**Second, distinguish stable photographic structure from stable typography.** A feature can recur perfectly between scans because it really exists in the negative's emulsion. Recurrence alone therefore establishes that the feature is photographic, not that it represents ink. The stronger question is whether neighbouring features combine into the expected geometry and spacing of a typewritten character or word.

**Third, inspect unsharpened archival-quality data before enhanced derivatives.** Sharpening and contrast manipulation are valuable viewing aids, but they can increase the apparent decisiveness of weak transitions. FADGI's treatment of sharpening as a measurable processing characteristic reinforces why an interpretation should not depend upon one aggressively processed rendering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/FADGITechnicalGuidelinesforDigitizingCulturalHeritageMaterials_ThirdEdition_05092023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Technical Guidelines for Digitizing C...</span></span></span>

**Fourth, test the method on known text photographed under comparable conditions.** Houran and Randle relayed a particularly useful proposal: obtain an identical camera, photograph period military messages from different distances and angles, and determine whether the known wording can actually be recovered. Such a control would show both what the photographic system is capable of recording and how often a deciphering procedure produces plausible but incorrect characters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That last test is more informative than simply commissioning another enormous scan. If a method cannot reliably recover known typewriting photographed at the same character scale, its success on an unknown and culturally loaded document is difficult to evaluate.

## What a genuinely better scan would establish

None of this makes high-resolution rescanning pointless. Quite the opposite: the best possible scan is desirable because poor digitisation should not become an additional bottleneck. Archival standards exist precisely to ensure that digital reproductions faithfully preserve what the physical source can yield. FADGI's current technical guidelines address photographic negatives alongside other cultural-heritage material and assess image quality through more than nominal pixel count, including spatial-frequency response, noise, tonal behaviour and sharpening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization Guidelines Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span></span></span>

A superior Ramey scan could therefore do several useful things. It could show that an older digital version was undersampled. It could distinguish scanner artefacts from persistent negative features. It could preserve tonal transitions more accurately, document scratches or contamination and provide a stronger common dataset for independent analysis.

What it cannot do is evade the information limit of the 1947 photograph.

That is the crucial dividing line between [photographic grain]({{ 'grain-ceiling/' | relative_url }}) and genuine character strokes. Once a modern capture system faithfully samples the surviving negative, the remaining uncertainty belongs increasingly to the negative itself. At that point, additional resolution may give researchers a clearer view of why the memo is difficult: not an endlessly hidden reservoir of smaller and smaller letters, but a finite photographic record in which weak typewritten signals, blur and physical film texture occupy overlapping scales.

For the Ramey memo, then, the strongest claim that can be made for a finer scan is also the most modest: it can give a more faithful digital representation of the evidence that survives. Whether that surviving evidence contains enough independent spatial information to distinguish disputed letters is a separate question — and no increase in pixel count can answer it merely by making the grain larger.
