## Introduction

Returning to the original Fort Worth Star-Telegram [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) mattered because, by 2015, the central uncertainty surrounding the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) was no longer simply whether better software could enlarge the writing. Researchers needed to know whether earlier prints, scans and reproductions had themselves become the bottleneck — whether recoverable photographic information still existed in the archival film but had been lost or softened during copying.

That was a reasonable possibility. First-generation photographic negatives normally preserve more of the camera-recorded image than later prints or copies, which introduce their own contrast, cropping and reproduction losses. The Library of Congress, for example, describes first-generation camera film as providing the sharpest detail compared with subsequent prints.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/csas/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Carnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCarnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South - Digitizing the Collection - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog...</span></span></span> The University of Texas at Arlington (UTA) preserves the Fort Worth Star-Telegram material from which the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) photograph originated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span>

The 2015 examination therefore addressed a more fundamental question than another proposed transcription: **did the original negative actually contain additional usable detail?** Direct examination showed that returning to the film was worthwhile — but it also revealed why better source material alone was unlikely to produce the hoped-for breakthrough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationA Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; Journal of Scientific ExplorationAugust 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 20, 2022</span></span></span>

## Why earlier enlargements and scans were no longer enough

Attempts to extract the Ramey memo's wording had already passed through several generations of reproduction and [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}). This created an important distinction between two possible causes of failure. The memo might genuinely have been recorded with too little detail in 1947, or useful information might have existed on the original negative but been degraded when the photograph was printed, copied, scanned or digitally processed.

Earlier work could not decisively separate those possibilities. The US Air Force itself had obtained first-generation UTA material during its Roswell investigation and sent the photograph for digitisation and interpretation. According to the Air Force report, the organisation performing that work reported on 20 July 1994 that the photographs remained of insufficient quality to visualise the details being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparenc...</span></span></span>

Civilian investigators subsequently pushed reproductions much harder. The 2002 study by James Houran and Kevin Randle records that documentary filmmaker Russ Estes examined a UTA-made enlargement with professional video equipment, macro optics, magnification and microscopy, and scanned material at 9,000 dpi. He nevertheless said that he could not reliably decipher the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

By that stage, simply increasing the pixel count of another derivative image had diminishing evidential value. A scanner can sample a print at extremely fine intervals without recovering image information that the print never retained. Enlarging an uncertain mark can make the mark bigger and easier to inspect, but it cannot establish whether its apparent strokes came from letters in the photographed document, [photographic grain]({{ 'grain-ceiling/' | relative_url }}), copying artefacts or later processing.

This problem was particularly important because different investigators were already reporting different readings of the same indistinct areas. Houran and Randle's 2002 experiment found that interpretations were vulnerable to contextual suggestion and recommended working from the best raw data, using standardised enhancement and independent, blind analysis rather than treating visually persuasive readings as self-validating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There was therefore a logical step before another round of transcription: remove as many generations of copying as possible.

## What researchers hoped the archival negative would preserve

The archival negative offered something no enlargement or downloaded scan could supply: the surviving physical record made from the image captured by the camera. That did not guarantee readable writing, but it provided the best opportunity to determine whether previous reproductions had concealed subtle density differences in the emulsion.

The distinction is standard photographic practice rather than something peculiar to Roswell research. In explaining its digitisation of another historic photographic collection, the Library of Congress notes that it selected first-generation negatives because the film exposed in the camera preserves the full frame and the sharpest detail relative to later prints.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/csas/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Carnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCarnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South - Digitizing the Collection - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog...</span></span></span> In the Ramey case the attraction was even stronger because the target was not a face or a large object but minute letter forms occupying a very small region of a 4-by-5-inch negative.

Earlier technical work also provided a specific reason to return to the source. Independent image-processing efforts reviewed in the later Journal of Scientific Exploration account had obtained only slight improvements. Different specialists using techniques including blind deconvolution, Fourier processing, filtering and wavelet-based methods failed to produce clearly interpretable text; one important difficulty was that noise occurred at spatial scales comparable with the putative writing. André Marion, one of the specialists involved, recommended obtaining new scans under specified conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

That recommendation gets to the heart of why the 2015 archival visit mattered. Digital enhancement operates on recorded information. If researchers could acquire a cleaner, more faithful representation directly from the film, algorithms would at least be working with a better starting signal. Conversely, if direct examination showed that the limiting ambiguity was already embedded in the photographic emulsion, increasingly elaborate processing of old copies would have much less justification.

The archival negative thus became a test of the **information ceiling** of the photograph itself.

## What direct examination of the film changed

The important result of the 2015 work was not a newly agreed sentence from the memo. It was a better diagnosis of the source image.

The later published account, incorporating Simon Schollum's 2015 technical report, says examination found the negative's emulsion sufficiently well fixed and readable, with no significant damage or degradation specifically affecting the memo area. Age, newspaper use and handling had left imperfections, but the investigation did not find evidence that deterioration had simply destroyed an originally clear text image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2713/1663" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationJournal of Scientific ExplorationFebruary 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 2026</span></span></span>

That finding narrowed the problem considerably. If the negative had been badly deteriorated, the history of failed readings could plausibly have been blamed on archival damage. If the negative were badly out of focus or severely underexposed, camera technique could have supplied another straightforward explanation. Instead, the technical assessment shifted attention towards the physical scale at which the writing had been recorded.

The numbers make that problem unusually concrete. In the published account of the work, the digitised full 4-by-5-inch frame measured roughly 3,663 by 2,743 pixels, while the visible message extended only about 148.5 pixels across in that representation. The report identified the relationship between the tiny letter forms and the distribution of the film's silver-halide grains as the principal constraint on reading them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2713/1663" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationJournal of Scientific ExplorationFebruary 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 2026</span></span></span>

Researchers did not merely put the negative through an ordinary desktop scanner. The archival work included microscopic examination, bracketed exposures and recordings made through UTA equipment; the negative was also photographed with macro- and micro-lens arrangements. These approaches were intended to distinguish genuine image density from the structure and imperfections of the photographic medium itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2713/1663" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationJournal of Scientific ExplorationFebruary 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 2026</span></span></span>

That was a significant change in the evidential question. Instead of asking, “How much larger can we make this word?”, investigators could ask, “Are the structures we are enlarging actually resolved letter strokes?”

## The negative exposed the real resolution problem

A photographic negative is not an infinitely detailed analogue surface. Its image is formed within a light-sensitive emulsion containing microscopic grains. When a large subject is photographed, many image-forming elements contribute to the representation of an edge or letter. When writing occupies only a minute fraction of the frame, individual character features can approach the scale of the film structure itself.

That appears to have been the critical difficulty with the Ramey memo. The later technical account describes scale — specifically the relationship between font dimensions and the film's silver-halide distribution — as the main determinant of whether individual letter forms can be identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span></span></span>

This explains an otherwise counter-intuitive feature of the 2015 effort: **a much better view of the negative did not necessarily mean a much better view of the words**. Higher-resolution capture could expose the grain structure more faithfully. But once the scanner or microscope was resolving the film itself, additional magnification increasingly revealed the medium rather than new documentary detail.

Earlier independent analyses had already encountered this problem from the digital side. The laboratories summarised in the 2022 review found that advanced processing could improve aspects of the image without yielding clearly interpretable text, in part because grain or speckle noise occupied frequencies comparable with those of the writing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span> Direct work on the negative made it harder to attribute that difficulty merely to a poor internet image or inferior photographic print.

That distinction is crucial when evaluating proposed readings. A dark patch that resembles part of an “R” after enlargement is not automatically evidence that the original document contained an “R”. At this scale, the apparent stroke may partly reflect how a tiny projected character interacted with individual grains, neighbouring characters, paper tone and the optical limits of the original exposure. Digital sharpening can emphasise those structures, but emphasis is not the same as recovery.

## The failed breakthrough was still informative

Measured solely by whether the memo became readable, returning to the original negative was disappointing. Contemporary accounts of the 2015 project describe dozens of new recordings made under differing conditions, and large digital files were produced, but there was no immediate consensus transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/09/ramey-memo-update.html?showComment=1443576324320" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Ramey Memo Update</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Ramey Memo UpdateSeptember 26, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2015</span></span></span>

Measured as an archival experiment, however, the result was more useful. It removed an important unknown.

Before direct re-examination, a reasonable objection to every failed reading was that investigators might simply have been working from inadequate descendants of a superior original. Afterwards, that explanation became substantially weaker. The archival film was indeed the preferable source, and examining it produced better information about its condition and grain structure, but it did not reveal a hidden layer of crisp typography waiting beneath generations of bad copying.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2713/1663" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationJournal of Scientific ExplorationFebruary 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 2026</span></span></span>

The result also helps explain why very large digital files should not be mistaken for equally large gains in evidential resolution. The 2015 team could sample the negative more densely and preserve more faithfully what remained on it. Once sampling became fine enough to expose the grain that constituted the image, however, additional pixels could not recreate letter features that the 1947 exposure had never distinctly separated.

That is the central value of returning to the original negative. The experiment moved the Ramey memo debate away from a vague assumption that “a better scan” must eventually solve the problem and towards a harder physical limit: **how much textual information did the camera and film actually record in 1947?**

## What the archival negative can — and cannot — establish

The 2015 work strengthens several conclusions without establishing the contents of the memo itself.

First, the preserved UTA negative is a more appropriate evidential starting point than later enlargements and reproductions. UTA confirms that the Ramey material forms part of its Fort Worth Star-Telegram collection, while the [2015 scans]({{ '2015-scans/' | relative_url }}) were explicitly credited to Kevin Randle, David Rudiak, Simon Schollum and Jerry Morelock and to the UTA Special Collections holding the negative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span>

Second, direct inspection weakened the hypothesis that storage deterioration was chiefly responsible for the memo's unreadability. The reported condition of the relevant emulsion instead pointed towards the tiny recorded scale of the writing as the more fundamental problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2713/1663" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationJournal of Scientific ExplorationFebruary 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 2026</span></span></span>

Third, it did **not** validate any disputed transcription. That distinction is especially important because experimental work had already demonstrated how readily expectations can influence attempts to interpret ambiguous marks. Houran and Randle consequently argued that convincing readings should be reproducible across independent observers or laboratories rather than dependent on contextual prompting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The subsequent history reinforces that caution. UTA was still publicly describing the words as obscured in 2017 and publicising a $10,000 offer for anyone who could decipher them definitively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span> Randle's 2022 review likewise concluded that attempts to decipher the memo had not produced definitive results, despite incorporating the higher-quality archival work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationA Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident &#124; Journal of Scientific ExplorationAugust 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 20, 2022</span></span></span>

The most defensible lesson from 2015 is therefore narrower but more consequential than a claimed reading. Researchers finally tested the assumption that generations of reproduction were concealing substantially better information in the original film. Going back to that film was exactly the right archival experiment. Its failure to release a clearly readable message showed that the obstacle was not merely access to a better copy: much of the remaining uncertainty appears to originate at, or close to, the resolution of the original photographic record itself.
