## Introduction

The 2015 archival rescanning of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) reached a revealing limit: the equipment became capable of resolving the photographic negative more finely than the negative had recorded the memo’s letter shapes. At more than 65,000 pixels per inch, the new imagery reportedly sampled at below one micron and exposed individual [film grains]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}) roughly 3–10 microns across. Yet the characters on the paper had been projected onto the 1947 negative at such a tiny scale that their distinguishing strokes were themselves entangled with that grain structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That distinction explains why simply scanning again at still higher resolution was unlikely to produce the hoped-for breakthrough. Once individual grains are resolved, additional pixels can describe those grains more precisely, but they cannot reconstruct letter strokes that the original exposure never recorded distinctly. The [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo problem had therefore moved from inadequate digitisation towards a more fundamental question: how much textual information survived in the physical photographic image at all.

## How little of the frame the memo occupied

The critical problem began when J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}) took the photograph in General Roger Ramey’s office on 8 July 1947. The photograph was not a close-up made to preserve a document. The sheet was merely an object within a much larger press photograph, held in Ramey’s hand while the camera recorded Ramey, Colonel Thomas DuBose and the displayed debris. The original Johnson negatives are preserved in the Fort Worth Star-Telegram Collection at the University of Texas at Arlington. UTA describes the document as having resisted definitive decipherment despite repeated scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span>

The scale figures reported from the 2015 examination show why. In the edited Simon Schollum technical report later published by Kevin Randle, the full 4×5-negative digital image was described as approximately 3,663 by 2,743 pixels, while the relevant message extended only about 148.5 pixels across in that representation. Schollum therefore identified scale — rather than gross deterioration, camera shake or missed focus — as the principal obstacle. The negative was judged adequately exposed, with the memo inside the camera's focus zone and without significant degradation specifically destroying its text area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That finding changes the meaning of the familiar enlarged Ramey-memo images. On a computer screen, the document can be made enormous. But screen size says nothing about the amount of original information behind the enlargement. A letter that occupied only a microscopic region of the negative does not acquire additional recorded strokes because its digital representation is expanded to hundreds of screen pixels.

Earlier investigations had already encountered this problem indirectly. Richard Haines reportedly examined the image microscopically in 1991 and could discern vague word-like forms and occasional letters but not enough individual characters to recover the message. In 1994, an unidentified national-level organisation consulted for the US Air Force digitised the photographs but reported that their quality was insufficient to visualise the sought details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The 2015 project was important because it tested whether those earlier failures were principally failures of reproduction technology. By returning to the archival negative with microscopes, bracketed exposures, RAW recording, macro and micro photography and high-resolution film, the researchers substantially reduced that uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

## Why film grain became comparable to character detail

Photographic film does not record a scene as an infinitely continuous surface. Its light-sensitive emulsion contains microscopic silver-halide crystals; the characteristics and distribution of these particles affect the film's ability to resolve fine detail. Kodak similarly defines photographic resolving power as the ability of an emulsion or optical system to reproduce fine detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/photographic-film" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Photographic Film</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Photographic Film - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

That becomes crucial when photographing text from a distance. A large printed letter may be represented across enough photographic structure for its vertical strokes, curves and openings to remain distinct. Reduce the same letter far enough and those features eventually approach the scale at which the emulsion itself is recording the image. At that point, the distinction between a genuine character feature and local grain variation becomes progressively less secure.

Schollum's 2015 assessment put the Ramey memo squarely in this regime. The report states that the decisive quantity was the size of the font on the negative relative to the size and distribution of the silver-halide grains. The resulting high-resolution files reportedly exceeded 65,000 pixels per inch, corresponding to sampling finer than one micron, while revealing grains estimated at roughly 3–10 microns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The important figure is not 65,000 pixels per inch by itself. It is what became visible at that sampling density: **the physical grains of the original photograph**. The scanner was no longer merely producing a coarse digital approximation of a smooth underlying letter. It was resolving the microscopic structure from which that photographic letter image was actually constituted.

This is also why film grain cannot simply be treated like dirt that can be removed to reveal perfectly preserved typography underneath. In conventional photographic film, the granular emulsion is part of the recording medium itself. Fine-grained films can reproduce greater detail than coarse-grained ones; Kodak, for example, explicitly links fine grain and resolving power in its descriptions of modern high-resolution black-and-white film.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kodak.com/en/still-film/product/professional/ektapan/ektapan-100-film/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kodak.com">[Kodak]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kodak.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EKTAPAN 100 Film &#124; Kodak</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EKTAPAN 100 Film &#124; Kodak...</span></span></span> The relevant question for the 1947 negative is therefore not merely whether grain obscures the letters, but whether enough grains recorded each distinguishing feature of each letter to preserve that feature unambiguously in the first place.

This distinction had been recognised before the 2015 work. Three independent expert analyses reported in a 2005 project applied methods including blind deconvolution, Fourier processing, homomorphic filtering and wavelet-based techniques. The later summary of those studies says that the techniques produced only slight improvements and no clearly interpretable text; one laboratory specifically concluded that the noise frequencies were on approximately the same scale as the textual information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The [2015 rescanning]({{ '2015-scans/' | relative_url }}) effectively attacked the remaining possibility that better source capture would separate those two scales. Instead, it made the grain structure itself clearer.

## Why sharper sampling could not create absent strokes

Digital resolution and photographic information are not the same thing. A scanner samples whatever density pattern exists on the negative. Increasing its sampling resolution is useful while the scanner is the weakest link: finer sampling can expose photographic detail that a coarse scan previously merged together.

But that improvement has diminishing returns. Once the digital system samples the negative finely enough to represent its meaningful physical variations, adding still more samples mainly produces a more detailed digital description of the same analogue record.

The 2015 workflow went well beyond simple enlargement. Researchers used a Nikon SMZ1500 microscope, a digital microfiche system, bracketed exposures, RAW files and Canon macro- and micro-photography. High-dynamic-range processing combined image states in attempts to reduce grain interference, separate the paper's tonal background from the typography and increase the tonal separation of apparent character forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

Those procedures could improve **visibility** without necessarily improving **recoverability**. For example, tonal processing can make a faint density difference easier for the eye to notice. Multiple exposures can capture a broader tonal range. Better optics can prevent the rescanning apparatus from adding another layer of blur. None of those operations, however, can guarantee recovery of a crossbar, serif, opening or diagonal that was never independently represented in the 1947 emulsion.

Schollum's reported conclusion was correspondingly stark: direct examination had provided a clearer view of the grain structure, but no further increase in visual readability could be achieved. Randle's later assessment likewise concluded that further physical scanning, irrespective of improvements in scanning equipment, was unlikely by itself to settle the memo's wording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

A useful way to understand the ceiling is to separate three stages:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **The scene contained the information.** The physical sheet in Ramey's hand presumably had ordinary readable characters.
2. **The 1947 photograph sampled that information imperfectly.** The camera lens, distance, focus, exposure and photographic emulsion determined how much character structure reached the negative.
3. **The modern scan sampled the surviving negative.** It could preserve the analogue record extremely faithfully, but it could not return to stage one and photograph the sheet again.

</div>

The 2015 effort dramatically improved the third stage. Its failure to deliver a definitive transcription indicates that the main bottleneck lay increasingly in the second.

## Grain noise and letters occupied the same territory

This is why the Ramey memo is better described as a signal-to-noise problem than as an ordinary blurry-document problem. A conventional blurred page may still contain enough underlying spatial information for deconvolution or sharpening to recover useful edges. With the memo, previous specialist analyses found that the noise occupied spatial frequencies comparable to those of the text itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That overlap matters because filtering depends on distinguishing unwanted structure from wanted structure. If noise is predominantly much finer than the letters, a low-pass filter can suppress it while retaining the larger character forms. If unwanted variations and meaningful character strokes occur at similar scales, aggressive filtering can erase portions of the letters along with the grain.

Conversely, aggressive sharpening can make both stronger. A density fluctuation created by grain may acquire an apparently significant edge; two neighbouring grain clusters may begin to resemble a crossbar or curved stroke. The resulting image can look more decisive without actually containing more reliable linguistic evidence.

A contemporary sceptical assessment of the 2015 scans made essentially this point, describing the difficulty as signal being lost amid film-grain noise rather than simple defocus. Although that commentary came from a critic of pro-Roswell interpretations, it closely matches the physical limitation identified in Schollum's report: the text and granular structure were competing at similar spatial scales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/SUNlite7_6.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOShedding some light on UFOlogy and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 1, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2015</span></span></span>

The practical consequence is that the grain ceiling is also an **interpretation ceiling**. Once a character consists of only a marginal pattern of photographic density, several different letters may plausibly explain it. Context then begins doing work that the image itself cannot do.

## Why ambiguity increased the danger of seeing expected words

The physical grain limit helps explain a separate result from Ramey-memo research: readers can sincerely perceive different words in the [same image]({{ 'same-image/' | relative_url }}).

In a 2002 experiment, James Houran and Kevin Randle gave 176 participants Ramey-memo material under three different descriptions. One group was told the document might concern Roswell and an extraterrestrial crash, another that it concerned atomic-bomb testing, and a third received no contextual suggestion. Participants produced readings influenced by the context they had been given, although a small number of words appeared consistently across conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The later 2022 review records the striking contrast. Words including “Fort Worth TEX”, “Story” and “Balloons” appeared across conditions, but the atomic-testing group also reported context-compatible terms such as “Flash”, “Atomic” and “Laboratory”. The researchers concluded that some parts of the document might genuinely be legible while interpretation of less certain regions was strongly susceptible to suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

Photographic grain does not itself cause psychological priming. What it does is create the visual ambiguity in which priming can operate. If an unmistakable capital E is preserved, contextual expectations have little room to change it into another letter. If only several irregular density patches survive where the original E stood, language, expectations and knowledge of the Roswell story can influence how the observer joins those patches into a character.

This is particularly important for disputed words that would dramatically change the memo's significance. A high-resolution enlargement can make a candidate reading easier to illustrate, but the proper evidential question is whether independent observers or algorithms can distinguish that reading from alternatives **using surviving image structure rather than expected vocabulary**.

That is why the absence of consensus after better scanning is not a trivial failure. It is evidence about the quality of the surviving signal.

## The rescanning ceiling was physical, not merely digital

The strongest result of the 2015 work was therefore not a newly deciphered sentence. It was a clearer identification of where the information bottleneck lay.

The archival negative was reportedly neither grossly deteriorated nor badly out of focus. Modern equipment could sample it finely enough to reveal individual grains. HDR processing could alter tonal separation and make grain and candidate character structures easier to inspect. Yet visual readability stopped improving once that physical structure became exposed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That outcome is consistent with the earlier independent laboratory work, which found that sophisticated restoration methods could make modest improvements without producing clearly interpretable text. It is also consistent with the long-running pattern in which a few regions attract relatively broad agreement while the crucial phrases continue to produce competing readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

This does not mean that every pixel in the Ramey memo is useless, nor that no additional characters could ever be classified probabilistically. Pattern-recognition methods could compare uncertain shapes against carefully constructed reference material. The 2022 discussion, for example, proposed photographing known letters under matched conditions to establish how particular characters might appear after undergoing the same photographic degradation. But it also acknowledged the danger of feeding an algorithm expected Roswell vocabulary, which could reproduce the same kind of interpretive bias found in human readers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The crucial limit remains unchanged: computational methods can infer the most probable explanation for ambiguous recorded structure, but inference is not equivalent to recovering detail that was physically preserved. The more completely a letter's distinctive strokes disappeared into the granular sampling of the original photograph, the more any reconstruction must depend on assumptions about what the missing information probably was.

For the 2015 archival rescanning, that was the failed breakthrough in its most precise sense. The researchers succeeded in seeing the negative at extraordinarily fine scale. What they discovered was that **seeing the negative better was no longer the same thing as seeing the memo better**. Once individual photographic grains became visible, further enlargement increasingly magnified the structure of the 1947 recording medium itself. The missing letter strokes were not necessarily waiting beneath another layer of insufficient scanner resolution; some had apparently never survived as separately recoverable photographic information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>
