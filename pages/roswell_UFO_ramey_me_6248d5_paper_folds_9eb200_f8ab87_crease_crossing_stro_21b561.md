## Introduction

A letter printed across a sharp paper fold can lose more than its neat shape in a photograph: it can lose an entire stroke as usable image information. That is especially important for the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), photographed in Brigadier General Roger Ramey’s hand in July 1947. The surviving image shows a small text-bearing sheet that is folded and angled rather than presented flat to the camera; the original Fort Worth Star-Telegram [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) is preserved in the University of Texas at Arlington collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_paper_folds_9eb200_f8ab87_crease_crossing_stro_21b561-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Crease Loss illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_paper_folds_9eb200_f8ab87_crease_crossing_stro_21b561-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_paper_folds_9eb200_f8ab87_crease_crossing_stro_21b561-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

At a crease, part of a printed character can turn sharply away from the camera, become extremely foreshortened, fall into reduced contrast or shadow, or disappear behind the nearer face of the fold. Modern document-imaging research treats strong [folds]({{ 'folds/' | relative_url }}) as a substantially harder problem than ordinary perspective distortion for precisely this reason.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/multiview-rectification-folded-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: microsoft.com">[Microsoft]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">microsoft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents - Microsoft Research...</span></span></span>

The crucial distinction is between **distortion of information that was recorded** and **information the photograph never resolved**. The first may sometimes be corrected. The second cannot simply be recovered by making the picture larger.

## How a fold can erase a stroke

Imagine a printed capital letter whose vertical stroke happens to cross a sharp crease. On the original sheet, the ink remains continuous. To the camera, however, the two-dimensional letter has effectively been divided between surfaces facing in different directions.

Away from the crease, a small patch of paper can behave approximately like an ordinary tilted plane. Its letters may look squashed or slanted, but there is still a geometric relationship between the printed form and its photographic projection. At the bend itself that assumption breaks down: the surface orientation changes rapidly over a very short distance. Research specifically concerned with folded documents therefore reconstructs or models the paper's three-dimensional surface rather than treating the entire sheet as one flat plane. Microsoft Research's multiview method, for example, uses several viewpoints and explicitly models ridges in folded paper before digitally unwrapping it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/multiview-rectification-folded-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: microsoft.com">[Microsoft]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">microsoft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents - Microsoft Research...</span></span></span>

That geometry produces several ways for a character stroke to become photographically insignificant.

* **[Foreshortening]({{ 'foreshortening/' | relative_url }}):** a section of ink running across a surface that turns towards edge-on presentation occupies much less width in the image than the same stroke on a front-facing portion of the sheet.
* **Abrupt orientation change:** opposite sides of the crease project differently, so what was one continuous stroke on the paper can appear displaced, narrowed or interrupted in the photograph.
* **Occlusion:** if the fold turns far enough, the nearer portion of the sheet can physically conceal a small part of the farther surface.
* **Low contrast near the bend:** illumination changes with the paper's orientation. Shadows are known to reduce document-text contrast and OCR legibility, making an already tiny stroke harder to distinguish from its background.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877050924009086" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Towards Removal of Shadows Caused due to Object Interferences in Smartphone Captured Document Images using Multiple Mask Gen...</span></span></span>
* **Blur and sampling:** once the projected stroke becomes comparable with the smallest detail the photographic system can distinguish, its signal merges with neighbouring tones, grain or blur rather than remaining a clean line. Spatial-resolution theory makes the basic point explicit: details below the resolving capability of an imaging system cease to be separately distinguishable, irrespective of later magnification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/mathematics/sampling-theorem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Sampling Theorem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sampling Theorem - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

The result can be visually deceptive. A letter need not merely look like a narrower version of itself. A short horizontal bar, loop closure or vertical stem can become so compressed or concealed that the photographed character resembles a *different* letter.

That matters greatly in attempted transcription. Distinguishing an `E` from an `F`, for example, may depend on one short horizontal stroke; distinguishing a closed form from an open one may depend on only a few tiny image features. If such a diagnostic feature crosses the wrong part of a crease, the photograph may provide much weaker evidence for the letter than the apparently large enlarged character suggests.

## Distorted information is not the same as missing information

A useful way to understand the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo problem is to separate **recoverable geometric distortion** from **loss at capture**.

Suppose a letter is photographed obliquely but remains fully visible and sufficiently resolved. Its shape may be compressed, yet the image still contains measurements from which a rectification algorithm can estimate a more front-facing appearance. Modern document-dewarping systems perform exactly this sort of remapping, using estimated surface geometry, control points or text-line structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10543" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Document Dewarping with Control Points</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Document Dewarping with Control PointsMarch 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 20, 2022</span></span></span>

A fold can create a fundamentally different case. If a short stroke occupies such a tiny projected area that the lens, film and subsequent reproduction do not distinguish it from its surroundings, there is no clean stroke waiting inside the image to be “unfolded”. Likewise, geometric rectification cannot reveal ink on a surface that was physically hidden from the camera.

This is why modern folded-document research is revealing in relation to the Ramey photograph. The 2017/2018 multiview work by You and colleagues uses **multiple photographs from different viewpoints** to reconstruct folded documents. Different views provide additional observations of the paper surface; one viewpoint may therefore contain information that another does not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/multiview-rectification-folded-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: microsoft.com">[Microsoft]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">microsoft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents - Microsoft Research...</span></span></span> A single historical exposure cannot acquire a second view retrospectively.

Even contemporary research describes large folds and complex creases as unusually difficult cases for document rectification. Luo and Bo note that methods designed for smoothly curved pages perform unsatisfactorily on substantial creases and folds, which require explicit modelling of the document surface and its textual features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08365" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span> Research specifically targeting once-folded documents similarly treats the crease as a boundary between different document planes rather than as ordinary page skew.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00467" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unfolder: Fast localization and image rectification of a document with a crease from folding in halfDecember 1, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2023</span></span></span>

That distinction prevents a common conceptual mistake. **Flattening the apparent geometry of a fold does not mean reconstructing every character originally printed across it.** Dewarping changes where recorded pixels are placed. It cannot guarantee that the pixels contained enough evidence to identify a missing diagnostic stroke in the first place.

## Why the Ramey photograph is especially vulnerable

The problem is not simply that the Ramey memo is folded. The text itself is a small secondary object inside a much larger photograph. The surviving image was not a purpose-made document scan in which the sheet filled the frame and was carefully illuminated; it was a press photograph of Ramey, with the paper in his hand. The University of Texas at Arlington provenance attached to reproductions identifies the original Fort Worth Star-Telegram negative, while photographer J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}) reportedly used a Speed Graphic camera with 4 × 5 film.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

Consequently, a letter stroke on the memo begins with only a small share of the photograph's available spatial resolution. A crease can reduce that share still further. A stroke that would have been measurable had the sheet faced the camera squarely can be compressed into a much smaller photographic feature when its local patch of paper turns away.

This provides a mechanism for one of the persistent difficulties in Ramey-memo readings: apparently plausible words can depend on exceptionally small distinctions. Researchers have proposed substantially different transcriptions, while even specialist attempts at digitisation have not produced an uncontested reading. A paper examining the psychological and methodological difficulties of deciphering the memo quoted the US Air Force investigation's conclusion that digitisation still left the photographs of insufficient quality to visualise the details it sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on noufors.com.</span></span></span> Contemporary reporting likewise noted the dispute between claimed readings such as “victims of the wreck” and the official conclusion that the photograph did not support such visualisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/air-space-magazine/case-closed-2267596/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Case Closed</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Case Closed</span></span></span>

This does not establish that every disputed letter near a fold is unknowable, nor does it decide between competing Roswell interpretations. It establishes a narrower point: **where a proposed reading requires a particular small stroke to cross a strongly bent portion of the paper, absence of a clearly visible stroke is not automatically evidence that the original character lacked it**.

The fold itself can be responsible for the apparent absence.

## Why enlargement cannot recreate an unresolved stroke

Digital enlargement is useful because it makes existing differences between neighbouring image samples easier for a human observer to inspect. It does not, by itself, increase the amount of historical optical information recorded by the camera.

The underlying principle is well established in imaging science. Spatial resolution describes whether fine features remain separately distinguishable. If sampling is too coarse, detail is lost or misrepresented; merely representing the resulting image with more output pixels does not restore the missing original measurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/mathematics/sampling-theorem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Sampling Theorem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sampling Theorem - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span> Optical blur imposes another limit: sufficiently close features overlap in the recorded image instead of appearing as independently resolved structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2835776/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Super resolution fluorescence microscopy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Central (PMC)Super resolution fluorescence microscopy</span></span></span>

For a crease-crossing letter, consider three progressively worse situations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Compressed but resolved.** The stroke is narrow, yet several genuine tonal measurements still trace it. Careful rectification and contrast adjustment may improve interpretation.
2. **Marginally resolved.** The stroke contributes some signal but is mixed with blur, grain, background and neighbouring ink. Processing may make competing interpretations easier to compare, but certainty can remain low.
3. **Unresolved or hidden.** The photographic record does not distinguish the stroke as an independent feature, or the fold physically blocked it. Enlargement can make the ambiguity bigger; it cannot turn that ambiguity into a direct observation.

</div>

The third case is the critical one. Interpolation can produce additional pixels between recorded samples, and modern restoration or artificial-intelligence systems can infer likely shapes from learned patterns. Such methods can generate a plausible reconstruction, but plausibility is not the same as recovering direct evidence of which stroke was actually present on Ramey's sheet.

Even genuine super-resolution methods illustrate the qualification rather than removing it. Advanced imaging research can exceed conventional resolution limits when extra information is supplied through multiple observations, specialised measurements or strong prior constraints. Information-theoretic treatments explicitly connect such reconstruction to measurement accuracy and prior knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41566-025-01839-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Super-resolution imaging of limited-size objects &#124; Nature Photonics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Super-resolution imaging of limited-size objects &#124; Nature Photonics</span></span></span> Those conditions are very different from repeatedly enlarging one tiny region of a 1947 press photograph.

## A missing stroke can change the apparent letter

This is the practical consequence for Ramey-memo transcription. Human reading depends heavily on diagnostic fragments. We rarely need every part of a clean printed character because familiar combinations let the brain complete patterns. With severely degraded text, however, the same ability can work against objective identification.

Suppose two candidate letters differ principally by one small bar. If that bar lies across the fold and is absent from the photograph, both hypotheses may fit the surviving pixels. One interpretation says the bar never existed; the other says it existed on the paper but was compressed, blurred or concealed at capture. Image enhancement cannot choose reliably between those explanations unless some independent recorded feature distinguishes them.

This is why a crease should be treated as a **local uncertainty zone**, not simply as an inconvenience to be straightened. The farther a character lies from the bend, the more reasonable it may be to treat ordinary perspective correction as the dominant problem. As a critical stroke approaches or crosses the ridge, confidence should depend on whether that stroke remains independently visible in the source image, not on how convincing the enlarged reconstructed letter looks.

The physical fold visible in the Ramey photograph therefore has evidential significance without telling us what the memo says. Modern document-imaging research confirms that folds divide pages into differently oriented surfaces and make rectification substantially harder; imaging theory explains why sufficiently compressed details cease to be independently resolved; and the historical attempts to read the memo demonstrate that digitisation has not produced a universally accepted transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/multiview-rectification-folded-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: microsoft.com">[microsoft.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">microsoft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents - Microsoft Research...</span></span></span>

## The key limit

The most important question for a crease-crossing Ramey-memo letter is not, “How large can we make this character?” It is, **“Did the original photograph separately record the stroke needed to distinguish this character from its alternatives?”**

If the answer is yes, geometric correction, careful tonal processing and comparison with neighbouring characters may extract useful evidence. If the stroke survives only weakly, the appropriate result may be a probability or a set of plausible letters rather than a single transcription. If the stroke was hidden by the fold or projected below the effective resolution of the photographic record, no amount of ordinary enlargement can reveal it as an observed feature.

That is the sense in which a fold can erase part of a letter without removing any ink from the original paper. The ink existed in three dimensions; the surviving evidence is only a two-dimensional projection. At the sharpest bends in the Ramey memo, the difference between those two things is precisely where certainty can disappear.
