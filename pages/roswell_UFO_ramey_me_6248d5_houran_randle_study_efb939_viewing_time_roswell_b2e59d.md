## Introduction

In the Houran–Randle experiment, the participants told that the blurred [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) concerned Roswell spent the longest time trying to decipher it: about **20 minutes on average**, compared with **16 minutes** for participants primed with an atomic-bomb [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) and **14 minutes** for participants given no suggested context. The Roswell group also reported words fitting the Roswell narrative. That pairing is important, but it does not show that extra viewing time uncovered more genuine Roswell text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_houran_randle_study_efb939_viewing_time_roswell_b2e59d-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Viewing Time illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_houran_randle_study_efb939_viewing_time_roswell_b2e59d-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_houran_randle_study_efb939_viewing_time_roswell_b2e59d-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

The experiment instead raises a more subtle possibility: a compelling story may encourage people to persist with an ambiguous image, giving them more opportunities to organise uncertain marks into meaningful patterns. Because Houran and Randle did not experimentally hold viewing time constant, however, their study cannot cleanly separate the effects of **[expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }})**, **motivation** and **additional inspection**. The longer viewing time is therefore best understood as part of the priming mechanism, not independent proof that the Roswell readings were either correct or mistaken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## How viewing times differed across the three groups

All 176 participants examined essentially the same difficult photographic material, but they were randomly assigned different descriptions of what it might contain. Fifty-nine were given a Roswell/UFO interpretation, 58 an atomic-bomb interpretation and 59 no proposed subject. Crucially for the viewing-time question, the researchers imposed **no time limit** on the deciphering task. Participants could continue examining the enlarged memo and computer images for as long as they chose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Their average inspection times then separated in the same broad direction as the strength or interest of the supplied narrative:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Roswell condition:** approximately **20 minutes**
* **Atomic-bomb condition:** approximately **16 minutes**
* **Blind condition:** approximately **14 minutes**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

</div>

The six-minute difference between the Roswell and blind averages amounts to roughly **43 per cent more viewing time**. The atomic-bomb group also persisted longer than the blind group, though not as long as the Roswell group. Houran and Randle interpreted this pattern in motivational terms: a story about a crashed UFO and cover-up could make the deciphering exercise more intriguing, while an atomic-bomb scenario could likewise be more engaging than simply being handed an old, blurred document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That interpretation fits another important result. The Roswell and atomic-bomb groups both produced substantially more deciphered words than the blind controls. The reported means were 4.6 words for the Roswell condition, 4.8 for the atomic condition and only 1.6 for the blind condition; the overall group effect was statistically significant. In other words, **more words were not unique to the group that spent the most time**. The atomic group actually had a slightly higher mean word count despite spending, on average, four fewer minutes on the task.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That detail matters. It argues against a simple formula in which more minutes automatically produce more decipherment. What changed was a combination of how long people looked and what interpretive framework they brought to those extra minutes.

## Why extra inspection can reinforce ambiguous letter patterns

The most revealing feature of the experiment is that the additional readings tended to follow the story supplied to the participant. Roswell-primed observers reported terms such as “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”, alongside readings also found in other conditions. Atomic-bomb participants instead supplied context-fitting terms including “glasses”, “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. The blind participants generated relatively few interpretations and lacked the same strong thematic pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That makes viewing time difficult to treat as a neutral increase in evidence gathering. With a sharply photographed document, another six minutes might simply allow a careful reader to notice more objectively visible letters. With the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) image, the underlying signal is sufficiently degraded that prolonged inspection also creates more occasions for interpretation. Houran and Randle themselves described the memo as very difficult to read and noted weak agreement among earlier investigators attempting to reconstruct it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The experiment therefore suggests a feedback mechanism:

**suggested context → greater interest or motivation → longer inspection → more candidate patterns → more opportunities to fit those patterns to the suggested context.**

The atomic-bomb condition is particularly useful here because it shows that this was not simply a “UFO believers see UFO words” phenomenon. Changing the suggested story changed the vocabulary people perceived. Participants encouraged to think about atomic testing found atomic-related language in precisely the same visual material from which Roswell-primed participants extracted Roswell-compatible language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

At the same time, the image was not necessarily devoid of recoverable information. Across conditions, participants converged at identical locations on several readings, including “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”; “land” also attracted substantial agreement in the two primed conditions. Houran and Randle therefore did **not** conclude that everything perceived in the memo was an artefact of suggestion. They argued instead that some portions might be genuinely legible while the larger reconstruction remained vulnerable to expectation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This distinction is essential when considering prolonged viewing. Repeated inspection can plausibly improve detection of a relatively strong visual signal while simultaneously increasing opportunities to impose meaning on weaker marks. The experiment does not provide a reliable way to determine, word by word, which process was operating.

## Viewing time and Roswell words cannot be separated cleanly

The study's design leaves an important causal [ambiguity]({{ 'ambiguity/' | relative_url }}). Participants chose how long to inspect the material; the researchers did not randomly assign them to, say, 10-, 15- and 20-minute viewing periods. Consequently, the experiment establishes an **association** between experimental condition, viewing behaviour and reported interpretations, but it does not establish that the additional six minutes themselves caused the Roswell group to perceive Roswell-related words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Several causal sequences remain compatible with the findings. Roswell priming could have directly shaped perception while independently making participants look longer. Alternatively, the Roswell framing could have increased motivation, with longer searching then giving participants additional opportunities to discover apparently meaningful patterns. Most plausibly, the processes could have reinforced one another: expectation guides attention, an apparently relevant fragment encourages further inspection, and further inspection produces more candidate fragments.

The surviving data cannot resolve those alternatives particularly well. The paper reports the three groups' average viewing times, but its principal statistical analysis concerned the number of words deciphered and other participant variables rather than a controlled test of viewing duration as an independent causal factor. More seriously, the researchers reported that the original score sheets had been discarded after responses were entered into a database. They therefore could not provide the detailed participant-by-participant frequencies for every condition-specific word when a referee requested them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That loss [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) what can safely be inferred about the interaction between persistence and particular Roswell readings. For example, the published evidence does not establish that the people who personally spent the longest looking were specifically those who supplied “crash”, “UFO” or “remains”. The group averages are suggestive, but they cannot substitute for that individual-level analysis.

## What longer viewing does and does not prove

The viewing-time result strengthens the case that **motivation was part of the Houran–Randle effect**. The least contextualised task produced the shortest average inspection and fewest deciphered words; both supplied narratives encouraged more decipherment, while the particularly engaging Roswell framing produced the longest inspection. Houran and Randle themselves highlighted this possibility in their discussion, arguing that Roswell participants may simply have worked harder because the alleged crash-and-cover-up scenario was more interesting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

But three stronger conclusions would go beyond the evidence.

First, **20 minutes of inspection does not validate the Roswell transcription**. Time spent looking is not a measure of photographic information. If a letter pattern is below the image's reliable resolution, persistence cannot restore information that the photograph failed to record.

Second, **longer inspection does not prove that Roswell words were hallucinated or invented**. Some words appeared across experimental conditions at corresponding positions, which is exactly why Houran and Randle regarded at least parts of the document as potentially legible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Third, **the experiment cannot tell us how the Roswell group would have performed if every participant had been forced to inspect the image for exactly the same duration**. That is the missing comparison most directly relevant to viewing time. A stronger design for this particular mechanism would cross the three contextual primes with fixed inspection periods and then use independent, blinded scoring of every reported character and word.

The broader methodological lesson is therefore narrower than either side of the Ramey memo controversy might prefer. Persistence can be useful when examining degraded material, but persistence under a strong expectation is not equivalent to independent confirmation. Houran and Randle consequently recommended standardised enhancement procedures and multiple independent, **blind** laboratories rather than relying on analysts who already knew what the memo was expected to say.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That recommendation gained added relevance from later work discussed by Randle. In a 2022 review, he reported that a follow-up project arranged by Houran sent high-quality memo scans to three independent specialist laboratories using different methods; according to that account, the laboratories obtained only limited improvements and independently judged the text still insufficiently legible for a dependable reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

For the specific question of longer viewing, then, the Houran–Randle experiment supplies an intriguing warning rather than a verdict. The Roswell story apparently made people **look longer**, and people supplied with meaningful stories generally extracted **more story-compatible language** from the blurred memo. Yet because viewing duration was allowed to vary naturally rather than experimentally controlled, the study cannot say how much of the Roswell-word effect came from the extra minutes and how much came directly from the expectation that participants carried into those minutes. The safest conclusion is that the two factors were entangled — precisely the situation that makes independent, blinded decipherment so important.
