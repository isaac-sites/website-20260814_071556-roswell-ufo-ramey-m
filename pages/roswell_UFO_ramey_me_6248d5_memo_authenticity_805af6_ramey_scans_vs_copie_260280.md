## Introduction

Better scans can make the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) photograph easier to inspect, but they cannot recover detail that J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})’s camera never recorded in 1947. That is the central distinction between first-generation archival imaging and the sharpened, enlarged or contrast-adjusted Ramey images commonly circulated online. The University of Texas at Arlington (UTA) preserves the underlying *Fort Worth Star-Telegram* negative, while later researchers have repeatedly returned to that source because each additional photographic or digital generation introduces another opportunity for lost detail, artefacts or interpretive processing. UTA’s catalogue describes the original format as a black-and-white negative and identifies the photograph by its archival collection and date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/specialcollections_startelegram1940s/35502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mavmatrix.uta.edu">[MavMatrix]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mavmatrix.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mav Matrix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">General Ramey holding enlargement of telegram...</span></span></span>

Modern work has extracted considerably more useful visual structure from that [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) than an ordinary web JPEG conveys. Yet sophisticated rescanning has also exposed the limiting factor: the memo occupies a tiny area of Johnson’s original frame, and its letter forms approach the scale of the [film grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}) itself. Once scanning resolves those grains, simply creating more pixels does not create more documentary information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

## What researchers gained from first-generation scans

The important improvement was not merely making the familiar photograph bigger. It was shortening the chain between the researcher and Johnson’s original photographic record.

The US Air Force had already recognised the value of early-generation material during its 1990s Roswell investigation. Its researchers obtained what the Air Force described as original, first-generation prints from UTA and subsequently obtained first-generation negatives. The photograph was then sent to an unnamed “national-level organization” for digitisation and interpretation. According to the Air Force report, that organisation concluded on 20 July 1994 that the photographs remained of insufficient quality to reveal the details being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span> The 1995 report itself remains preserved through the US Government Publishing Office and the Department of the Air Force's historical collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/GOVPUB-D301-PURL-LPS71655" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB D301 PURL LPS71655</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Roswell Report: Fact Versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert - GOVPUB-D301-PURL-LPS71655 &#124; Content Details &#124; GovInfoJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

That finding did not end attempts to obtain better source data. From 1998 onwards, researchers used large enlargements, computer processing and newly requested material from UTA. The resulting readings differed substantially. Neil Morris, John Kirby, David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}) and others agreed on some apparent features while disagreeing over much of the text. Kirby, for example, accepted “At Fort Worth, Tex.” but offered a markedly different reading of the line that other investigators associated with “victims”. Rudiak proposed another reconstruction again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

A 2002 study by James Houran and Kevin Randle is particularly useful for understanding what “better source material” meant at this stage. Its participants were given an 8-by-10-inch enlargement derived from UTA material plus Stanton Friedman’s collection of four high-resolution images. These included a negative image, a UTA enlargement produced for Friedman, an older enlargement made before further handling of the negative, and another enlargement made in 1998. The researchers described Friedman's scans as, to their knowledge, the best raw data then available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

That is a fundamentally different evidential position from examining an unknown JPEG downloaded from a website. A first-generation scan or carefully documented archival reproduction allows an analyst to know what physical source was digitised, to preserve much more tonal information and to compare alternative exposures or processing methods. An online enhancement may still be useful for illustration, but unless its source and processing history are documented, it is much harder to determine whether a striking letter-like feature comes from the negative, the scan, compression, sharpening or later manipulation.

## The negative became the real test

The most informative later work therefore returned directly to UTA rather than trying to extract endlessly more information from existing web copies. A renewed examination began at UTA in April 2015 under controlled access conditions. Its stated objectives included measuring the negative’s definition and resolution, distinguishing genuine image density from film base and fog, identifying the silver particles that formed possible characters, and separating them from particles or “bleed” that did not belong to letter forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

This examination produced an important finding that is easily lost when discussion focuses only on proposed words. The relevant negative was not simply too damaged to be useful. The reported inspection found its emulsion reasonably well fixed, with no significant degradation specifically destroying the memo area. There were age- and handling-related marks — including dust, scratches and minor chemical effects — but exposure was considered adequate, there was no evident subject or camera shake, and the memo lay within the photograph’s focus zone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The fundamental problem was **scale**.

Johnson reportedly used a 4-by-5-inch negative, which sounds generous by modern photographic standards. But the memo is only a small object within the overall scene. In one analysis cited in the 2022 *Journal of Scientific Exploration* review, a full-frame digital image measured roughly 3,663 by 2,743 pixels while the message itself extended only about 148.5 pixels across. The decisive issue was therefore the relationship between the dimensions of the typed characters and the distribution of the silver-halide grains making up the photographic image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

This explains why an apparently enormous enlargement can remain frustratingly ambiguous. Enlarging 148 pixels to 1,480 or 14,800 display pixels changes how large the information appears on a screen; it does not necessarily turn those original samples into ten or one hundred times as much evidence about the shape of a letter.

## How archival images differ from online enhancements

“High resolution” can describe two very different things in the Ramey memo debate. One is **capture resolution**: how finely the original negative has actually been sampled. The other is merely **output resolution**: how many pixels exist after an image has been enlarged or resampled.

That distinction matters because an enhanced web crop can look much cleaner than an archival scan while being evidentially poorer. Common processing operations can increase local contrast, brighten dark regions, sharpen edges, suppress grain or interpolate additional pixels. Those operations may make genuine structures easier for a human observer to notice. But they can also exaggerate marginal patterns and make ambiguous clusters resemble familiar characters.

The later archival programme deliberately tried several routes around this problem. Investigators used a Nikon SMZ1500 biological microscope, UTA's digital microfiche equipment, bracketed exposures, RAW files, macro and micro photography, and ultra-high-resolution recording film. High Dynamic Range processing was used to exploit tonal differences, while image stacks attempted to suppress film-grain interference, separate the paper tone from the typed text and isolate tonal values associated with possible characters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

The reported final sampling exceeded 65,000 pixels per inch, reaching below one-micron resolution and making individual silver-halide grains visible. Those grains were estimated at roughly 3–10 microns. That is a crucial milestone: at that point the scanner was no longer merely producing a bigger picture of the memo; it was resolving the physical photographic structure from which the image itself was made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

A web JPEG produced downstream from such material cannot exceed that information ceiling. A 10,000-pixel-wide enlargement of the memo may be convenient to study, but its extra pixels are mathematically derived from the recorded image. They are not newly photographed strokes of type.

## Enhancement can clarify structure without settling words

This does not mean enhancement is useless. Processing can make several genuine features much easier to evaluate: the number and position of text lines, approximate word lengths, spaces, contrast transitions and recurring character-like shapes. It can also help analysts reject proposed readings that simply do not fit the visible structure.

That was already apparent in earlier high-resolution work. Rob Belyea of ProLab was asked to examine scans made from the negative and use software to assess such things as character counts and possible letter combinations. According to Randle’s later review, Belyea rejected at least one proposed word, “Magdalena”, and cautioned that researchers were deriving readings that the image did not reliably support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

More importantly, independent technical processing did not converge on a complete transcription. A later summary of work funded by the Fund for UFO Research describes three laboratories applying different restoration techniques to the highest-quality scans then available. Nikolas Galatsanos used blind deconvolution; André Marion used Fourier-based signal-processing methods; and Hong Yan used techniques including homomorphic filtering, wavelets and deblurring. The results varied in method but not in their basic limitation: processing produced at most modest improvement, while no clearly interpretable body of text emerged. Marion specifically found that image noise existed on approximately the same frequency scale as the text signal, making clean separation particularly difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That result helps explain an apparent paradox surrounding the Ramey images. Enhanced versions can look dramatically better than a small newspaper reproduction, yet specialists can still disagree over what individual words say. Visual improvement and information recovery are not the same thing.

## Why different enhancements produce different “readings”

The history of proposed [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) demonstrates the danger particularly clearly. Johnson's late-1990s team, Morris, Kirby, Rudiak, Don Burleson and other investigators generated readings that overlapped in places but diverged sharply elsewhere. Some thought they could identify “victims”; others proposed “remains” or disputed the letter forms. Several investigators accepted some version of “Fort Worth, Tex.”, while larger phrases remained unstable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

This instability is not merely a disagreement over Roswell history. It is evidence about the image itself. If a high-quality source contained clearly separable characters, different competent observers and processing methods should increasingly converge as image quality improves. Instead, the most consequential portions continue to permit competing letter and word assignments.

Houran and Randle tested this interpretive problem experimentally in 2002. Participants were divided into groups told that the image concerned Roswell, atomic-bomb testing, or no specified subject. Their proposed readings were influenced by those contextual suggestions. Some words appeared across conditions — notably “Fort Worth TEX”, “Story” and “Balloons” — but participants also perceived vocabulary associated specifically with the scenario they had been given. The authors concluded that portions of the document might genuinely be legible while the broader interpretation remained vulnerable to suggestion and cognitive expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That result is especially relevant to enhanced online images. Once a crop is labelled with a proposed transcription — perhaps with letters outlined, words printed beneath them or contrast chosen to favour particular strokes — a viewer is no longer approaching an ambiguous image blindly. Enhancement and interpretation can become entangled.

For evidential purposes, therefore, the safest order is the reverse: begin with the least processed high-quality archival capture available, preserve the RAW or equivalent source data, document every transformation, and compare interpretations from independent observers who have not been shown the preferred reading.

## Where enhancement stops adding real information

The 2015 and subsequent work provides the clearest answer yet to the question of whether simply making another, still-higher-resolution scan will solve the Ramey memo.

Direct examination reportedly reached the point where individual grains in the photographic emulsion could be resolved. Processing of RAW and enhanced files produced different contrast states and somewhat clearer grain structure, but the investigators concluded that no further increase in **visual readability** could be obtained by physical scanning alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

A further scanning effort in February 2020 used specially designed equipment operated by Gene Cooper of GIGAmacro. The work attempted to separate levels of information in the negative and rebuild the image while reducing noise attributable to handling, dust and other unwanted structures. Again, the resulting image appeared somewhat clearer, but not sufficiently clearer to establish a definitive transcription or even determine confidently whether the document was military or civilian.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

This is the point at which the difference between **resolution** and **recoverable information** becomes decisive. Once the scanner resolves structures smaller than the film grains, increasing its sampling rate mostly describes those grains in still finer digital detail. It cannot reconstruct a typewriter stroke whose optical image was never distinctly encoded among them.

The same restriction applies more strongly to downstream copies. A JPEG derived from a scan has, at best, the information contained in that scan minus whatever was discarded through cropping, tonal conversion, resizing and compression. Enlarging it again cannot restore its connection to the negative. Sharpening may emphasise boundaries; denoising may suppress distracting texture; interpolation may make curves appear smoother. None of those operations independently establishes that an ambiguous dark patch represents an original typed “V”, “R” or any other character.

## The best image is not necessarily the clearest-looking image

For readers comparing Ramey memo pictures online, visual crispness is therefore a poor measure of evidential quality. A highly processed enlargement may be the easiest version to look at while being the worst version from which to decide whether a contested stroke genuinely exists.

The more useful hierarchy is provenance-based:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Johnson's original negative at UTA** is the primary photographic source.
2. **Direct, documented high-resolution captures of that negative** preserve the closest available digital relationship to the 1947 exposure.
3. **Archival prints and well-documented early-generation enlargements** remain valuable but incorporate another photographic reproduction stage.
4. **Processed crops derived from those scans** can help expose contrast or structure, provided their processing is known.
5. **Recompressed web JPEGs, screenshots and annotated enhancements** are useful mainly for illustration unless their source chain can be traced backwards.

</div>

## What better scans actually changed

The strongest achievement of the first-generation rescanning work is more modest — and more useful — than a spectacular recovered message.

It established that the Ramey memo problem is not adequately represented by the tiny newspaper reproduction or by low-resolution internet copies. Researchers have examined materially better data, including direct recordings of the original negative. Those efforts established more about the negative's condition, focus, grain and information scale, and they produced better working images than earlier generations possessed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

At the same time, the better data weakened the idea that unlimited enhancement will eventually reveal a perfectly crisp hidden document. Three independent laboratory analyses found severe blur and grain to be limiting factors, and the later direct-negative work reached grain-resolving resolutions without obtaining a reliable full transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That leaves a narrower but defensible conclusion. Some structures — and perhaps some words — appear stable enough to deserve continued analysis. The 2002 experiment found cross-group agreement on several words even without relying solely on computer enhancement, suggesting that the photograph contains at least some recoverable textual signal. But proposed reconstructions of the decisive passages have not achieved comparable agreement, and suggestion measurably influences what observers report seeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For the photographic provenance of the Ramey memo, that distinction is crucial. First-generation scans are preferable because they minimise generational loss and give analysts the fullest surviving record of Johnson's exposure. Enhanced online images are downstream interpretations of that record. They can make existing information easier to see, but once the original negative's grain and blur have become the limiting factors, enhancement cannot manufacture trustworthy letter forms that were never photographically resolved in the first place.
