## Introduction

A single deskew cannot flatten the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) because the photographed sheet is not behaving like one flat plane. The paper in Brigadier General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }})’s hand is folded and locally warped, so different parts of the text face the camera at different orientations. A rotation may make one line look horizontal, and a perspective transform may improve one approximately flat patch, but neither operation can simultaneously restore regions lying on differently tilted surfaces.

That distinction matters whenever an enhanced Ramey image is used to judge ambiguous letters. The original negatives are held in the University of Texas at Arlington Libraries’ Fort Worth Star-Telegram Collection, and UTA itself says the memo has yet to be definitively deciphered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span> The geometric problem is therefore not merely cosmetic. A processing choice that improves one portion can leave another compressed, stretched or locally warped — and more aggressive correction requires assumptions about the page shape that the photograph does not fully establish.

## When one planar correction works

For an ordinary flat document photographed at an angle, the geometry is comparatively manageable. Computer vision uses a **homography**, a projective transformation relating one plane to another. Given suitable reference points, an obliquely photographed rectangular sheet can be transformed towards the appearance it would have had if viewed more directly. OpenCV's technical documentation specifically lists perspective correction as an application of homography and defines the operation in terms of a transformation between planes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docs.opencv.org/4.5.1/d9/dab/tutorial_homography.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docs.opencv.org">[OpenCV Documentation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docs.opencv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OpenCV DocumentationOpenCV: Basic concepts of the homography explained with codeDecember 21, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 2020</span></span></span>

This is more powerful than simply rotating an image. Rotation corrects an overall slant: if every text line rises ten degrees from left to right, the entire image can be turned ten degrees in the opposite direction. A planar perspective correction can additionally compensate for effects such as one side of a flat page appearing shorter than the other.

Both approaches nevertheless depend on an important assumption: the points being corrected belong to the same physical plane. That assumption is what breaks down in the Ramey photograph.

Imagine a sheet folded into three broad panels. The camera sees the upper panel at one angle, the centre at another and the lower panel at a third. Each panel may be approximately planar on its own, but there is no single plane containing all three. Consequently, the transformation that correctly rectifies the centre cannot also be the geometrically correct transformation for both neighbouring panels.

This is why modern document-imaging research distinguishes ordinary perspective rectification from **dewarping** deformed documents. Carnegie Mellon researchers, for example, developed a method that estimates a document's three-dimensional deformation before rectifying its image, using the regular structure of text to constrain the reconstructed surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ILIM/projects/IM/document_rectification/document_rectification.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cs.cmu.edu">[CMU School of Computer Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cs.cmu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CMU School of Computer ScienceRectification and 3D Reconstruction of Curved Document ImagesJune 1, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2011</span></span></span> Research specifically addressing folded documents goes further still, reconstructing the paper surface and then computationally unwrapping it rather than treating the whole sheet as one plane.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.00166" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded DocumentsJune 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2016</span></span></span>

## The Ramey sheet contains several local orientations

The surviving photograph provides the concrete reason this distinction matters. Ramey is holding the document rather than presenting it flat to the camera. UTA identifies the original July 1947 negatives as part of its Special Collections, while published research on the memo describes the sheet as not facing the camera straight on.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span> Close examination and subsequent reconstruction efforts also identify [folds]({{ 'folds/' | relative_url }}) and differing orientations across the visible paper.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/the-missing-u/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span></span></span>

The useful way to think about the photograph is therefore not as **one tilted rectangle**, but as a collection of local surface patches.

On a relatively flat patch, nearby characters undergo roughly similar perspective distortion. As the paper approaches a fold, however, its surface normal — the direction perpendicular to the paper — changes. Beyond the fold, the next section faces the camera differently. Near a curved or sharply bent transition, orientation can change over a very short distance.

That produces several distinct effects in the memo image:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Different vertical compression.** Letters on a surface tilted further away from the camera can appear more foreshortened than letters elsewhere.
* **Different apparent line slopes.** Making one line horizontal does not guarantee that another line crossing differently oriented paper will also become horizontal.
* **Changing character proportions.** A letter can be narrower or shorter in the photograph because of projection rather than because its original typewritten shape differed.
* **Local bending around creases.** Straight text on the physical sheet can acquire a changing image trajectory where the paper itself changes orientation.
* **Warping near the held edge.** Ramey's grip introduces deformation that need not follow the geometry of the major folds.

</div>

The central point is that these effects cannot all be represented by one rotation angle. Nor, once the sheet departs significantly from a plane, can they all be represented exactly by one global homography.

This is not a peculiarity invented for the Ramey memo. Folded-document research treats large folds and creases as a substantially harder rectification problem than ordinary camera perspective. A 2022 study devoted specifically to creased documents notes that complex creases and large folds remain challenging and models the document's developable three-dimensional surface before flattening it computationally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08365" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span> Other dewarping systems estimate mappings at control points or even pixel-wise displacement fields, precisely because a single global planar mapping is insufficient for general warped documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10543" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Document Dewarping with Control Points</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Document Dewarping with Control Points</span></span></span>

## Why fixing one region can damage another

Suppose an analyst chooses the middle of the Ramey memo as the reference region. Rotating the photograph until those lines are horizontal may make that portion easier to inspect. If the letters there also appear vertically compressed, the analyst might stretch them until their proportions resemble the expected typeface.

The resulting image can look much more natural. But that does not establish that the same correction is valid above and below the folds.

If an upper section originally faced the camera at a different angle, applying the centre's correction to it can overcompensate or undercompensate its dimensions. A global vertical stretch has the same limitation: it assumes that the amount of vertical [foreshortening]({{ 'foreshortening/' | relative_url }}) is essentially uniform across the page. On a folded sheet, it need not be.

The problem becomes especially important when analysts move beyond simple deskewing and attempt to make every line straight and every character similarly proportioned. That requires a **spatially varying warp**: different parts of the source photograph must be moved by different amounts.

Ramey-memo researcher David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}) has described doing exactly this. In a 2018 discussion of his processing, he reported using morphing software to flatten and straighten the message, correct perspective and letter distortion, regularise character size and spacing, straighten lines and compensate for folds. Crucially, he also acknowledged that the process introduced artefacts, especially in heavily distorted regions above the upper fold, around the middle fold and along the left side where Ramey's hand deforms the paper. He further noted that poorly visible letters required judgement about their positions, which in turn affected the warp model.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

That acknowledgement captures the core methodological issue. Once rectification becomes local rather than global, the analyst is no longer merely turning the photograph into a more convenient orientation. The analyst must estimate where points on the distorted page ought to move.

Where the page structure is clearly visible, that estimate can be well constrained. Where the source pixels are blurred, shadowed or extremely small, the appropriate mapping is less certain.

## A flattened image is a model, not a second photograph

This distinction is easy to miss because successful dewarping looks intuitively convincing. Straight lines become straight; letters acquire familiar proportions; the page begins to resemble a scan. Yet the flattened result remains a transformation of the original photograph.

Modern document-dewarping methods illustrate how much information the operation can require. Some infer a three-dimensional page surface from text structure. Others use multiple photographs from different viewpoints to reconstruct folds before unwrapping the paper. Still others predict dense displacement fields specifying where individual image locations should move.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://snsinha.github.io/pdfs/YouOrigami2014-TR.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: snsinha.github.io">[github.io]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">snsinha.github.io</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">folded paper sheets from a small num- ber of images captured from different viewpoints...</span></span></span> Recent work continues to treat complicated real-world deformation as a substantially richer problem than a one-step rotation or perspective correction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06988v1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The Ramey memo presents an unusually difficult version of that task because the document itself is tiny within a 1947 photograph and the disputed characters are already near the limit of useful detail. UTA describes the obscured wording as not definitively deciphered, despite decades of scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span> A 2002 study of attempts to read the memo likewise treated [ambiguity]({{ 'ambiguity/' | relative_url }}) in the image as a central problem rather than assuming that [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) had yielded an uncontested transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Geometric correction can rearrange the recorded information into a more interpretable form, but it cannot recover strokes that the negative failed to resolve distinctly. If two candidate letters have already collapsed into nearly the same handful of blurred pixels, dewarping may change the shape of those pixels without supplying the missing original detail.

This produces an important difference between **geometric confidence** and **textual confidence**. An analyst might be reasonably confident that a particular line was foreshortened, yet much less confident about exactly how severely it was foreshortened at each character. A plausible correction can therefore improve legibility without making every apparent stroke equally reliable.

## What remains distorted after a global deskew

A useful global deskew can still be worthwhile. It can put the memo into a convenient overall orientation, make lines easier to compare and remove an obvious source of visual distraction. Rudiak, for example, argued that rotating the memo towards horizontal and compensating for obvious compression made inspection easier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span> The mistake would be treating that operation as though it had geometrically flattened the physical sheet.

After a single rotation, local perspective compression remains. After a single planar perspective transform, departures of the paper from the chosen reference plane remain. After a uniform stretch, differences in foreshortening between differently oriented patches remain. And around folds or hand-induced curvature, the required correction may change continuously across the image.

A technically stronger workflow would therefore distinguish three stages rather than treating them as interchangeable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Global orientation correction** removes the overall rotation of the memo in the photograph.
2. **Local geometric rectification** attempts to compensate for the differing orientations and curvature of individual regions.
3. **Text interpretation** asks what character shapes remain supported after those transformations, while checking them against the unwarped source.

</div>

The second stage is the difficult one. Folded-document research shows why: reliable flattening generally requires information about the document's local geometry, whether inferred from text lines, control points, a reconstructed three-dimensional surface or additional viewpoints.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08365" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, there is no clean modern scan of the sheet in its original flat state against which such a reconstruction can simply be checked. The photograph is the evidence from which both the text and much of the paper geometry must be inferred.

## The practical limit of deskewing the memo

The safest interpretation of a deskewed Ramey memo is therefore regional rather than global. A correction can be judged by how well it handles a particular approximately planar portion of the sheet, not by whether the entire transformed page looks satisfyingly flat.

This also explains why competing processed versions need not differ because one analyst simply “rotated it wrongly”. They may embody different assumptions about which region provides the reference orientation, how much perspective compression exists, where folds begin and end, and where poorly resolved text ought to sit in the flattened reconstruction. Rudiak's own description is revealing here: his sophisticated warp was intended to correct folds and character proportions, yet he explicitly reported artefacts and acknowledged that uncertain character locations affected the model.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

The resulting limitation is straightforward. **One rotation can make one overall direction horizontal; one planar transform can rectify one plane; the Ramey memo contains neither just one orientation nor one clean plane.** Proper dewarping therefore has to vary across the document. Where enough visual structure survives, that can produce a more geometrically plausible rendering. Where folds, shadow, blur and low spatial resolution have removed the constraints needed to determine the mapping confidently, flattening becomes partly model-dependent.

That is why deskewing should be treated as an aid to examining the Ramey memo, not as a procedure that automatically converts the 1947 photograph into the equivalent of a flatbed scan. The folds are not merely visual clutter to be rotated away. They divide the document into differently oriented surfaces, and those local orientations are themselves part of the missing information that any attempted reconstruction has to estimate.
