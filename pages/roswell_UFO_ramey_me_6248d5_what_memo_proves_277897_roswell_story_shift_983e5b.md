## Introduction

The Roswell announcement reversal was a rapid change in the US Army Air Forces’ public explanation in July 1947: a local military statement first described the recovery of a “flying disc”, but within hours senior officers in Fort Worth presented the material as debris from a weather balloon. The [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) photograph is important because it sits at the centre of that short transition window. It does not independently prove what the recovered object was, but it provides evidence that senior officials were actively managing the public response at the moment the explanation changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

The key historical question is therefore not simply whether the [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) changed — it clearly did — but what evidence the change itself provides. The sequence of announcements, photographs, press activity and surviving documents shows a military effort to replace the initial “flying disc” description with a conventional debris explanation. The disputed issue is what that sequence means: evidence of ordinary correction after a mistaken identification, or evidence of a deeper concealment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo THE PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoTHE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

## The first flying disc announcement

On 8 July 1947, Roswell Army Air Field issued a statement announcing that personnel had recovered the remains of what was described as a “flying disc”. The announcement quickly moved through news networks and turned a local incident into a national story. The statement’s significance lies in its timing: it was not a later recollection or a decades-old claim, but an official military announcement that was publicly circulated before being withdrawn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo THE PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoTHE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

The announcement created immediate pressure for clarification because “flying disc” was a highly charged phrase in the summer of 1947. Reports of unusual aerial objects had already attracted public attention, and the Roswell statement appeared to confirm that the military had recovered something matching those reports. Within the same day, however, senior officers at Fort Worth Army Air Field began presenting a different interpretation of the material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/roswell5.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 8, 1947: The Press Conference...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

The surviving evidence from this stage is strongest on the question of public communication. The record shows that the Army Air Forces initially allowed the flying disc description to enter the public domain, rather than beginning with the weather balloon explanation that later became official. What it does not establish by itself is why the first description was issued or whether it reflected a mistaken identification, incomplete information, or something else.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo THE PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoTHE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

## The Fort Worth explanation replaces the first account

Later on 8 July, Brigadier General Roger Ramey, commander of the [Eighth Air Force]({{ 'command-control/' | relative_url }}), held a press event at Fort Worth Army Air Field. Photographs from the event show Ramey, Colonel Thomas DuBose and Major [Jesse Marcel]({{ 'marcel/' | relative_url }}) with pieces of debris that were presented to journalists as the remains of a weather balloon and associated equipment. The images became central evidence because they capture the moment when the Army Air Forces publicly replaced the flying disc claim with a conventional explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

The reversal was unusually fast. The same day contained two conflicting official messages: the Roswell announcement describing a recovered flying disc and the Fort Worth presentation describing balloon debris. This compressed timeline is why the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo photograph remains historically significant. The document visible in Ramey’s hand belongs to the exact period when officials were coordinating the new explanation, making it evidence of the response process even though the memo’s text remains disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

The Fort Worth explanation also introduced a new visual narrative. Instead of an unknown object, journalists were shown ordinary-looking fragments associated with a balloon. Photographs from the event were widely reproduced, helping establish the Army’s revised account in public memory. The evidence therefore demonstrates a successful communication shift: within hours, the official story moved from an extraordinary description to a familiar military explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGeneral_Ramey_with_Roswell_Memo.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:General Ramey with Roswell Memo.png</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:General Ramey with Roswell Memo.png - Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

## The memo’s place in the timeline

The Ramey memo photograph anchors the document to the most important moment of the announcement reversal. The surviving photographs, taken by Fort Worth Star-Telegram photographer J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}), show Ramey holding a sheet of paper during the press response. The original photographic material is preserved through archival collections, including the University of Texas at Arlington’s holdings of Fort Worth Star-Telegram photographs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

What the image can establish is limited but valuable. It confirms that Ramey was handling written information during the public explanation, that the photograph belongs to the Fort Worth response, and that the military leadership was actively communicating about the Roswell material. These points help reconstruct the timeline of the reversal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

The main dispute concerns attempts to read the tiny writing on the page. Some researchers have argued that enhanced versions of the photograph reveal words that contradict the weather balloon explanation, while others argue that the image quality is insufficient for a reliable transcription. Because the memo text cannot be independently recovered with certainty, the strongest evidence remains the broader timeline: an initial announcement, a rapid correction, and a documented press presentation of replacement material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

## What the reversal evidence can and cannot show


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The announcement reversal is one of the clearest documented parts of the Roswell case. The evidence shows a genuine change in the Army Air Forces’ public position within a very short period. The Ramey memo adds detail by placing senior officers and written communication inside that transition, but it does not by itself reveal the full contents of the decision-making process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>

</div>

The main evidence-based interpretations remain divided. One interpretation is that the first statement resulted from confusion or premature identification of unusual debris, followed by a correction once officers examined the material more closely. Another interpretation argues that the speed and organisation of the reversal suggest that officials were controlling information rather than simply correcting an error. The surviving photographs and documents demonstrate the change in messaging, but they do not alone resolve the motive behind it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/press-conference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo THE PRESS CONFERENCE –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey MemoTHE PRESS CONFERENCE – JULY 8, 1947 – UnlockingTheMystery...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 8, 1947</span></span></span>

For understanding what the Ramey memo contributes to Roswell history, its strongest role is as a timeline marker. It places a real document, real officers and a real press response at the centre of the moment when the official account changed. The memo helps establish how the public story shifted; it does not independently settle every claim about what happened before that shift.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGen_Ramey_Roswell_memo_1947.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Gen Ramey Roswell memo 1947.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 8, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: [July 8](&amp;#123;&amp;#123; &#x27;july-8/&#x27; &#124; relative_url &amp;#125;&amp;#125;), 1947</span></span></span>
