## Introduction

The missing score sheets are one of the clearest limitations of James Houran and Kevin D. Randle’s experiment on attempts to read the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}). Their atomic-test condition produced an intriguing result: participants told that the blurred document concerned secret atomic-bomb testing reported nuclear-themed words including “flash”, “glasses”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. That supports the broader finding that prior context could influence what people perceived in the ambiguous photograph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_atomic_priming_362a6b_missing_score_sheets_72cd13-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Missing Records illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_atomic_priming_362a6b_missing_score_sheets_72cd13-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_atomic_priming_362a6b_missing_score_sheets_72cd13-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

But the evidential trail stops short of what later researchers would ideally want. Participants originally recorded their readings line by line on individual score sheets. After those responses had been entered into a computer database and summarised, the primary research assistant discarded the sheets. When a referee subsequently asked for participant-by-participant frequencies for each reported word, Houran and Randle discovered that the originals no longer existed. They therefore retained detailed frequency information only for words common across the three experimental conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That loss does not erase the experiment or automatically invalidate its priming result. It does, however, make the particularly interesting atomic-themed readings substantially harder to audit.

## What records were discarded

The experiment involved 176 participants randomly assigned to Roswell, atomic-bomb and blind conditions. Each participant examined the memo imagery and wrote down, line by line, whatever individual characters or complete words they believed they could perceive in its ten lines of text. Research assistants then aggregated those responses into a computerised database.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This distinction between the **original records** and the **aggregated data** is crucial. The study did not lose everything. Its database survived, allowing Houran and Randle to analyse such outcomes as the number of words deciphered and relationships with experimental condition and participant characteristics. What disappeared were the underlying handwritten score sheets that preserved each participant's original transcription in its fullest form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The problem surfaced during peer review. According to the published paper, a referee requested counts showing how many participants in each experimental condition had deciphered each particular word. The researchers contacted their primary research assistant because those figures were not immediately available in the computerised material being used for analysis. They then learnt that the assistant had disposed of the sheets after preparing notes on words shared between conditions and compiling the database.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This was therefore not a case in which no records had ever been made. Records capable of answering a later, more detailed question had existed but had not been retained. That matters because the referee's question went directly to the strength of the claimed context effect: not merely whether an atomic-themed word appeared somewhere in a condition, but **how often it appeared there**.

## Which word data survived?

Some frequency information remained available. Houran and Randle reported substantial agreement on several readings found across conditions: “Fort Worth TEX” was associated with 52 participants, “[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})” with 61 and “weather balloons” with 27. “Land”, reported by participants in the two primed conditions, had a count of 47. These surviving figures helped the authors argue that at least portions of the photograph might contain genuinely discernible visual information rather than being wholly unconstrained noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

The atomic-specific material is different. Later summaries of the experiment identify “glasses”, “morning”, “flash”, “atomic”, “laboratory” and “land” with the atomic-test condition. The blind condition, by comparison, yielded a much thinner set of readings, while the Roswell-primed participants produced their own context-compatible interpretations. Randle's later review continued to regard this pattern as evidence that suggestion influenced interpretations of the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

What cannot now be reconstructed from the discarded sheets is the complete participant-level frequency distribution behind most of those distinctive readings. There is a large evidential difference between, for example, one atomic participant reporting “laboratory” and twenty independently reporting it. Both circumstances permit the statement that the word occurred in the atomic condition, but they provide very different evidence for a systematic priming effect involving that particular word.

The missing sheets therefore make it important to distinguish two claims. The experiment provides evidence that **experimental condition affected the overall deciphering behaviour of the groups**. It provides less auditable evidence for the stronger, word-level proposition that particular atomic terms repeatedly appeared because of the [atomic prime]({{ 'atomic-prime/' | relative_url }}). Those propositions are related, but they are not identical.

## Why the atomic words needed frequency counts

The atomic condition was valuable precisely because its prime was unrelated to the Roswell interpretation being tested. If people told an atomic-testing story began supplying nuclear vocabulary while people told a Roswell story supplied Roswell-compatible vocabulary, the contrast would be difficult to explain solely by assuming that everyone was accurately recognising the same objectively legible text.

Yet the strength of that inference depends partly on **replication within each group**. A context-specific word reported independently by many participants is stronger evidence of a systematic priming pattern than an unusual reading generated by a single participant. Without the individual score sheets, an outside reader cannot reconstruct that distinction for most words simply from the published examples.

This is especially significant because the experiment used free responses. Participants were not choosing “atomic”, “flash” or “laboratory” from a prepared menu; they wrote what they believed they saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span> Free responses make spontaneous context-compatible words interesting, but they also create potentially large numbers of idiosyncratic answers. Knowing the frequency of each response therefore becomes particularly useful for separating a recurring group tendency from isolated guesses.

The surviving overall results still provide an independent line of evidence. Atomic-primed participants averaged about 4.8 deciphered words, compared with roughly 4.6 in the Roswell condition and 1.6 in the blind condition, and the published analysis reported significant differences associated with suggestion. Randle's later review also notes that atomic participants spent about 16 minutes on the task, compared with about 20 minutes for Roswell participants and 14 minutes for blind participants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those results support a broader conclusion that supplying an interesting interpretive context affected participants' behaviour. They cannot, however, substitute perfectly for the lost word-by-word evidence. A higher number of proposed readings establishes that primed participants produced more material; it does not by itself demonstrate how consistently they converged on “atomic”, “flash” or any other particular prime-compatible word.

## What can no longer be independently checked

Had the original score sheets survived, later investigators could have performed several useful checks. They could have reconstructed the exact frequency of every proposed word within each condition, examined whether a supposedly atomic-specific term also appeared occasionally in Roswell or blind responses, checked where on the memo each participant placed it, and compared variant spellings or uncertain character sequences against the researchers' coding decisions.

The records would also have permitted re-coding. That is important in an experiment involving barely legible text because converting handwritten guesses into categories inevitably involves judgement. “Atomic”, a fragment resembling it and a string of uncertain characters might be treated differently under different coding rules. Retaining the source sheets would allow another analyst to test whether reasonable alternative coding changed the pattern.

Such verification is particularly relevant to the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo because disagreement over transcription is the very phenomenon the experiment was investigating. Earlier analysts examining the photograph had produced markedly different readings of the same character shapes. Houran and Randle themselves stressed weak agreement among earlier interpreters and concluded that suggestion and cognitive style complicated attempts to establish the document's meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span> Preserving the experiment's own original [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) would therefore have been unusually valuable.

The study's subsequent history reinforces that point. Houran and Randle later acknowledged a separate transcription typo in their published discussion of an earlier investigator's reading: “Fort Worth, Tex” had accidentally appeared as “Fort Work, Tex”. They presented the correction as an illustration of how errors can enter transcription processes even when researchers are attempting to work carefully.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/501208456/jse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; Falsifiability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; FalsifiabilityJanuary 1, 2002...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2002</span></span></span> That episode is not evidence that the experimental database was wrong, but it demonstrates why access to original records is useful when tiny textual distinctions carry unusual weight.

## How much does the loss weaken the result?

The fairest assessment lies between two extremes. The discarded sheets do **not** justify treating the Houran–Randle experiment as though it produced no usable evidence. The published study still describes random assignment, its three suggestion conditions, its procedures, overall numbers of deciphered words and surviving frequencies for readings common across conditions. Later analysis by Randle continued to report the same central pattern: participants given different contextual primes tended to produce different interpretations, while blind participants produced fewer readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Nor does losing the sheets logically show that the atomic-themed words were fabricated or incorrectly reported. There is no evidence in the sources reviewed here establishing that. The problem is **verification**, not proof of misconduct.

At the other extreme, it would be too strong to present the published list of atomic words as though a complete raw dataset remained available from which anyone could independently reproduce every frequency. The original paper explicitly says otherwise. Its authors could not provide the referee with the requested condition-by-[word counts]({{ 'word-counts/' | relative_url }}) because the sheets needed to recover them had been destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Contemporary criticism seized on precisely this vulnerability; one 2003 UFO Updates exchange criticised the inability to supply underlying data supporting disputed conclusions, although such commentary came from participants in the Roswell debate rather than an independent methodological review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gbppr.net/ufoupdates/pdf/2003-02.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gbppr.net">[GBPPR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gbppr.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO UpDates: Feb 2003December 10, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2011</span></span></span>

The most defensible reading is consequently narrower. The atomic condition remains useful evidence that changing the suggested context could change how participants approached and interpreted the ambiguous Ramey memo. But the missing records reduce [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) in any highly specific claim about **how strongly individual atomic-themed words replicated within that group**.

## The surviving evidence supports a pattern, not a reconstruction

Later discussion of the experiment adds some support to its broad interpretation without repairing the missing-data problem. Randle's 2022 review reported ancillary findings that more interesting contexts encouraged longer examination, produced more perceived words and generated more words exclusive to individual experimental conditions than words shared across conditions. He also noted that critics had not produced a direct or conceptual replication of the Ramey-memo experiment itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

That means the original study occupies an awkward but still informative position. Its principal psychological point — that expectations can shape attempts to decipher highly ambiguous photographic text — is supported by several aspects of the reported group pattern. The atomic prime is especially informative because it demonstrates that the effect was not confined to giving participants Roswell information: substituting a different story produced a different family of apparent readings.

But the most memorable atomic examples should be handled with greater caution than the broad experimental effect. The surviving publication can tell us that words such as “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory” were recorded in the atomic condition. It cannot now provide the complete underlying score sheets needed to let a new analyst determine independently exactly how frequent, independent and consistently located most of those readings were.

For the Ramey memo debate, that is the lasting significance of the lost records. The experiment remains evidence against treating every apparently recognisable word in the photograph as context-free perception. What disappeared was the material that could have made its most striking alternative-prime evidence much more transparent, reproducible and resistant to dispute.
