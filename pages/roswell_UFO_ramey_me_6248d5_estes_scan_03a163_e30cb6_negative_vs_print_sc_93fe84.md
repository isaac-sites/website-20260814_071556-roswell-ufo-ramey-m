## Introduction

For the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), the crucial distinction is not between an ordinary scan and an enormous scan. It is between **scanning the original camera [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) and scanning a photographic print made from that negative**. Russ Estes's much-discussed 1998 examination used a 16-by-20-inch print made by the University of Texas library. He scanned it at 9,000 dpi and subjected it to extensive optical and digital examination, yet still reported that he could not reliably decipher the text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That outcome makes sense once the reproduction chain is considered. A print is already a second-stage photographic representation of whatever the 1947 negative recorded. Its sharpness, contrast and tonal rendering have been filtered through the printing process before a scanner ever sees it. A very dense scan can describe that print extremely precisely, but it cannot automatically recover information that failed to survive the negative-to-print step. The Library of Congress explicitly notes that scanning original negatives can produce higher-quality digital images than scanning prints because prints are one generation removed from the original exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/static/data/hh/resources/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congresswww.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congresswww.loc.gov</span></span></span>

For attempts to read tiny, borderline letter forms in the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) photograph, that difference is potentially decisive.

## What Is Lost When a Print Replaces the Negative?

The original negative is the camera-recorded photographic element: the closest surviving physical source to the moment when J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}) photographed General Roger Ramey holding the sheet. In archival terminology, an original negative is the first generation of the photographic image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/products/definitions/film-glossary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesGlossary of National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) Film Element Acronyms &#124; National ArchivesOctober 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 25, 2023</span></span></span> A print made from it is a derivative.

That distinction may matter little when the purpose is simply to recognise Ramey, the office or the debris on the floor. The memo presents a much harsher test. Its characters occupy only a tiny portion of the original photograph, and investigators are trying to distinguish small differences in blurred shapes — whether a mark is the vertical stroke of one letter, part of another letter, photographic grain or merely an artefact.

Every extra reproduction stage creates another opportunity to weaken those distinctions. During conventional photographic printing, the negative has to be projected or otherwise transferred onto photographic paper. The resulting print has its own resolving limits, tonal response, surface characteristics and contrast. Consequently, two neighbouring features that remain marginally distinct on the negative can become less distinct in the print.

This is not merely a theoretical preference among Ramey-memo researchers. The Library of Congress uses precisely the same source-generation principle in digitising historical photographic collections. For its HABS/HAER/HALS holdings, it states that direct scanning of original negatives and transparencies produces higher-quality digital images than scanning prints, which are “one generation removed” from the original exposure. Its archival capture is also deliberately designed to preserve highlight and shadow information rather than produce a more visually dramatic but less information-rich picture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/static/data/hh/resources/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congresswww.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congresswww.loc.gov</span></span></span>

The Library follows the same approach elsewhere. Its Harris & Ewing digitisation project scanned original glass-plate negatives directly, capturing them at 10,000 pixels on the long dimension and retaining 16-bit greyscale highest-resolution TIFF files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/hec/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Harris &amp; Ewing Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressHarris &amp; Ewing Collection - Digitizing the Negatives - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span> The point is not that those exact specifications should be applied mechanically to the Ramey negative. It is that professional archival practice treats **source quality and source generation as separate from scanner resolution**.

That is the central weakness in treating Estes's 9,000-dpi figure as though it describes the information content of the Ramey photograph itself. It describes how finely his later source was sampled. His source was still a print.

## Why 9,000 dpi Cannot Undo an Earlier Reproduction

Dpi — dots per inch, although pixels per inch is generally the more precise term for digital image sampling — tells us how densely a scanner samples its physical source. Increasing that number can be valuable while the scanner is still resolving genuine additional detail. But the number alone does not tell us how much independent information exists in the material being scanned.

Imagine that two faint portions of adjacent typewritten characters were distinguishable on the original negative but became blended into one grey patch when the enlargement was printed. A 9,000-dpi scan might represent that grey patch with thousands of pixels. Those pixels would provide an exceptionally detailed record **of the grey patch**. They would not reveal which two original strokes had produced it.

The same limitation applies to blur. If a letter was already blurred by focus, lens performance, movement, depth of field or the curvature and angle of the paper when Johnson exposed the negative, no later scan can simply restore a perfectly focused character. Likewise, if additional distinction was lost while the negative was printed, scanning the print more densely cannot retrieve information that is no longer physically represented there.

This is why file size can be misleading in historical-image arguments. Estes reportedly produced a 1.7-gigabit image from his 9,000-dpi scan. That gave him abundant digital samples to enlarge and manipulate, but Houran and Randle report that he nevertheless could not reliably decipher the memo. His tentative impressions were explicitly low-confidence: after examining an enlargement of the message area, he offered possibilities such as “Fort Work, Tex” and “ELA*”, while regarding the shapes as fundamentally ambiguous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The experiment therefore demonstrated something narrower, but more useful, than “technology cannot read the memo”. It showed that **extreme digital sampling of that particular print did not make the disputed characters reliable**.

That distinction is reinforced by US archival practice. The National Archives requires true optical resolution rather than simply resizing or interpolating lower-resolution material to a larger nominal resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/initiatives/digital-photo-records.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Digital Photographic Records &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesDigital Photographic Records &#124; National ArchivesNovember 12, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2003</span></span></span> The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative likewise treats effective resolution as something measurable in the captured image rather than something guaranteed by a headline scanner setting; its current tools include methods specifically intended to measure effective image resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digitization Guidelines Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitization Guidelines Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative</span></span></span>

In other words, pixel count is not synonymous with evidential detail.

## The Ramey Case Already Shows Why Source Generation Matters

The history of attempts to decipher the memo itself contains an instructive comparison. Houran and Randle describe Estes as working from the University of Texas library's 16-by-20-inch print. In the same paper, however, they report that Stanton Friedman arranged for the **original negatives** from Special Collections to be hand-carried to a computer laboratory for high-resolution scanning. Those negative-derived scans were subsequently supplied to another examiner, Rob Belyea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That does not mean the negative scans solved the memo. They did not establish a generally accepted transcription. Indeed, Belyea reportedly rejected at least some readings proposed by other investigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> The important point is methodological: the move from enlarged prints towards direct negative scans was an attempt to remove an unnecessary reproduction generation from the evidence chain.

The distinction also prevents an easy but mistaken inference from Estes's failure. His examination is strong evidence that simply throwing extraordinary scan resolution and enhancement tools at an enlargement does not guarantee readable text. It is **not**, by itself, proof that no additional useful information could exist on the original negative.

Nor does access to the negative guarantee success. The US Air Force's Roswell investigation reported obtaining first-generation prints and negatives of the Fort Worth photographs and sending imagery for digitisation and interpretation. The unnamed national-level organisation reported in July 1994 that the photographs remained of insufficient quality to visualise the sought details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span> Later private investigators likewise continued to disagree substantially over supposed words in the memo. Houran and Randle's study found that suggested context influenced what participants believed they could read, although some locations produced more consistent perceptions than others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The negative therefore represents a **better evidential starting point**, not a promised solution.

## What Could a Better Negative Scan Realistically Improve?

A carefully controlled modern capture of the original negative could potentially improve several things at once. It could avoid whatever resolution was lost in making an enlargement; preserve a wider range of density information; minimise artefacts introduced by photographic paper; and permit processing to begin with a source one generation closer to Johnson's original exposure.

There is also a practical advantage to high-bit-depth capture. When very faint differences in density matter, retaining a broad tonal range during digitisation gives later analysis more room to adjust contrast without beginning from an already compressed print rendition. The Library of Congress's practice of retaining high-resolution, 16-bit greyscale scans of original negatives illustrates the archival value placed on preserving both spatial and tonal information before producing lower-resolution service copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/hec/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Harris &amp; Ewing Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressHarris &amp; Ewing Collection - Digitizing the Negatives - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, realistic improvements might therefore include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* slightly cleaner separation between adjacent character strokes;
* better distinction between genuine image structure and print texture;
* more reliable measurement of character widths and spacing;
* improved tonal discrimination in faint portions of letters;
* stronger comparison of repeated character shapes elsewhere on the sheet; and
* a more defensible basis for deciding that particular marks are genuinely unresolved.

</div>

The last point is especially important. A superior source does not have to yield a complete transcription to be valuable. It could instead establish more confidently that some proposed letters cannot be distinguished from their alternatives.

That would be genuine progress because the Ramey-memo controversy has repeatedly suffered from the opposite tendency: turning ambiguous marks into increasingly specific words and then using those words to interpret other ambiguous marks. Houran and Randle found substantial disagreement among investigators and experimentally demonstrated that expectations could influence proposed readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Better source material matters partly because it can narrow the space in which that interpretative feedback operates.

## The Negative Still Has Its Own Hard Ceiling

The preference for the negative should not be exaggerated into a claim that the negative contains a perfectly recoverable version of the document. The original photographic exposure remains the ultimate bottleneck.

Johnson was photographing Ramey and the surrounding scene, not making a document reproduction. The paper occupies a small part of the frame and is neither flat nor square to the camera. Houran and Randle note its orientation while describing why investigators initially thought some text might nevertheless be recoverable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> Any optical blur, [film-grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}) limitation, poor focus, movement, exposure problem or perspective compression recorded at that instant became part of the negative itself.

Once neighbouring letter features have been merged at that stage, even a perfect digitisation cannot separate them from direct evidence alone. Image enhancement can increase contrast, suppress noise or make patterns easier to inspect, but processing cannot turn genuinely absent spatial information into authenticated typewritten strokes.

This is why the Library of Congress comparison is useful but must be applied carefully. Its own collections show that direct negative capture is normally preferable when maximum photographic information is wanted, while also recognising exceptions: deteriorated negatives may sometimes make prints more useful research surrogates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/csas/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Carnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCarnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South - Digitizing the Collection - Prints &amp; Photographs Online Catalog...</span></span></span> Source condition therefore has to be evaluated alongside source generation.

Nothing about the general negative-versus-print principle establishes that a modern scan would suddenly make the entire Ramey memo readable. It establishes a more modest proposition: **if additional recoverable photographic information survives anywhere, the original negative is the most logical place to look for it**.

## Why the Source Matters More Than the Headline Resolution

The Estes episode is memorable because 9,000 dpi sounds like overwhelming resolution. Yet for evidential purposes, “9,000 dpi” is incomplete without the next question: **9,000 dpi of what?**

In Estes's case, the answer was a large photographic print.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That print could be sampled extraordinarily densely, enlarged on screen and processed repeatedly, but it remained a reproduction of the negative. The Library of Congress's own digitisation guidance provides the straightforward reason the distinction matters: original negatives can yield higher-quality digital images because prints stand one generation farther from the original exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/pictures/static/data/hh/resources/digitizing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congresswww.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congresswww.loc.gov</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, therefore, a smaller but well-controlled capture directly from the original negative can be more evidentially valuable than an enormous file generated from a derivative print. The important variables are effective resolution, tonal information, focus, source condition and — above all — how close the scanned object is to the original photographic exposure.

That does not invalidate Estes's work. It clarifies what his experiment actually established. He demonstrated that spectacular scan density could not make his print support a reliable transcription. The remaining question is narrower: whether the original negative preserves marginal character information that the print failed to carry forward. Direct examination of the best surviving negative can test that possibility; endlessly enlarging the print cannot.
