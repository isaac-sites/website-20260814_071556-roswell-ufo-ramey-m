## Introduction

The [Ramey-memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) experiment produced a much smaller core of apparently stable text than the famous full-message reconstructions suggest. When James Houran and Kevin Randle gave different readers sharply different expectations about the blurred document, only **“Fort Worth TX”, “[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})” and “weather balloons” were reported in corresponding locations across all three conditions**. A fourth item, **“land”**, appeared in the same location in the two groups that had been given a suggested context, but not in the blind group. The researchers therefore treated four items as the limited residue left after controlling for context, while recognising that only the first three crossed all three experimental conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_cross_group_shared_w_88047b-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Shared Words illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_cross_group_shared_w_88047b-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_cross_group_shared_w_88047b-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That distinction is important. Cross-group agreement offers evidence that particular patches of the photograph may impose genuine visual constraints. It does **not** establish the surrounding sentences, identify what kind of document [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) was holding, or decide whether its message supports any particular explanation of Roswell. The experiment's most useful contribution is therefore not a recovered narrative but a shortlist of locations where [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) repeatedly found similar words despite being encouraged to expect different stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## The small nucleus of cross-group agreement

Houran and Randle randomly assigned 176 participants to three conditions. Fifty-nine were told that the photographed document might concern the Roswell crash and a government cover-up; 58 were told it might concern secret atomic-bomb testing; and 59 were simply asked to read a photographed document without being told what it was supposedly about. Participants worked from an enlarged print and several high-resolution scans and recorded their readings line by line.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The contextual manipulation worked strikingly well. Roswell-primed participants reported words such as “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”, while the atomic-bomb group generated alternatives including “glasses”, “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. The Roswell and atomic groups nevertheless claimed to decipher roughly the same number of words overall—means of 4.6 and 4.8 respectively—and both substantially exceeded the blind group's mean of 1.6. In other words, supplying *either* story made the image appear richer in readable language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Against that background, the overlapping words stand out precisely because they did not depend completely on one supplied narrative:

* **“Fort Worth TX”** was reported by 52 participants in total: 24 in the Roswell condition, 17 in the atomic condition and 11 in the blind condition.
* **“Story”** was reported by 61: 43 Roswell, 11 atomic and seven blind.
* **“Weather balloons”** accounted for 27 readings: 20 Roswell, five atomic and two blind. The paper counted “weather” and “balloons” as separate words when calculating word totals, but treated the expression as a shared reading in its comparison.
* **“Land”** was reported by 47 participants, but only in the two prompted conditions: 41 Roswell and six atomic. The authors therefore described it as a word of “relative agreement”, rather than one found across all three conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The later 2022 review by Randle summarised the genuinely three-condition overlap as “Fort Worth TEX”, “Story” and “Balloons”, while reiterating that the memo experiments had not produced definitive results capable of settling the competing explanations of Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

There is also an important limitation in the underlying dataset. A referee asked the original researchers for participant-by-participant frequencies for every proposed word, but the primary research assistant had discarded the original score sheets after creating the computer database and notes on common words. Consequently, the published paper says detailed data survived only for the readings common across conditions. That makes this small consensus set unusually important, but it also prevents a modern reanalysis of the complete raw transcription record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why agreement in the same location matters

Agreement on a word is more persuasive when readers independently put it in the **same physical position**. If one person sees “story” near the middle of a line and another finds “story” somewhere entirely different, the coincidence could simply reflect a familiar word being imposed on noisy marks. Houran and Randle instead reported that the common readings occupied corresponding locations and also matched locations where earlier memo investigators had reported the same words. They regarded that positional agreement as evidence that at least portions of the photographed text may genuinely be legible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That result is more interesting because the earlier specialist [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) were far from uniform. The paper's comparison table shows broad agreement among several previous interpreters on “AT FORT WORTH”, “WEATHER BALLOONS” and some other fragments, while much of the surrounding wording diverges dramatically. For example, one disputed line was reconstructed variously with references to a “DISC”, Los Alamos and an atomic laboratory. Another line acquired substantially different phrases concerning Roswell, emergency powers or military flights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The experimental overlap therefore provides a useful hierarchy of confidence. A fragment independently identified by readers who have been told incompatible stories deserves more attention than a fragment appearing only inside one elaborate reconstruction. The strongest experimental candidates are not necessarily *correct*, but they have passed a test that narrative-specific readings such as “UFO”, “atomic”, “laboratory”, “crash” and “glasses” did not.

There is a further reason for caution. The three groups did not engage with the task equally. Roswell participants spent about 20 minutes examining the material, compared with 16 minutes for the atomic group and 14 minutes for the blind group. The authors thought motivation could therefore have contributed to the differences. “Land”, which appeared only in the two contextual groups, is particularly difficult to separate from this effect: its absence from the blind group might reflect weaker visual evidence, lower engagement, contextual priming, or some combination of the three.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Shared words do not reconstruct a shared message

The crucial mistake would be to join the surviving fragments together and treat the result as a transcription. **“Fort Worth”, “story”, “weather balloons” and perhaps “land” do not constitute a coherent recovered sentence.** They are isolated points of agreement separated by regions in which readers produced very different language.

That is exactly why the words are compatible with competing interpretations. “Fort Worth” is unsurprising in material connected with the photographed scene, which took place at Fort Worth Army Air Field. “Weather balloons” could occur in a mundane account of balloon debris, but it could also occur in a hypothetical discussion of a balloon explanation. “Story” is even less diagnostic: without reliable surrounding syntax, it does not tell the reader what story is being affirmed, rejected, transmitted or discussed. “Land” has still less interpretive force in isolation. Houran and Randle explicitly stressed that their surviving words were ambiguous enough to fit opposing Roswell interpretations—or potentially something unrelated to the case altogether.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This separates **perceptual evidence** from **narrative evidence**. The experiment provides some evidence for the proposition that particular letter-shaped patterns repeatedly evoke the same words. It provides much weaker evidence for claims about what the complete document says. Moving from “several readers identify WEATHER BALLOONS here” to a paragraph explaining *why* weather balloons are mentioned requires filling in uncertain material around that relatively stable fragment.

The earlier specialist readings illustrate the problem. Several investigators agreed on “AT FORT WORTH” and “WEATHER BALLOONS”, yet their reconstructions of neighbouring text differed substantially. The paper also notes that those investigators were not fully independent: they had exchanged information while deciphering the message, and some had worked together. Their agreement therefore cannot be treated in the same way as agreement produced by experimentally separated reader groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## “Victims” fails the expectation test

One of the most consequential contrasts is between the experimentally surviving words and **“victims”**. Earlier Roswell-oriented transcriptions had repeatedly proposed a phrase resembling “VICTIMS OF THE WRECK” on the second line. The comparison assembled in the 2002 paper shows Neil Morris, David Rudiak, Don Burleson and Tom Carey converging to varying degrees on that reading, although another investigator, John Kirby, instead proposed a very different phrase involving “REMAINS OF THE MATERIAL”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Yet “victims” does not appear in the published list of words common across Houran and Randle's three experimental conditions. Nor does it appear among the Roswell group's listed readings in Table 3. That matters because “victims” would carry far more evidential weight than “story” or “Fort Worth”: if securely recovered, it would demand an explanation for whom or what the victims were.

The experiment does not prove that “victims” is absent from the original document. Its image quality, participant expertise and procedure leave room for criticism, and proponents of detailed Ramey-memo readings have argued that participants lacked sufficient contextual knowledge or time to make a proper transcription. Randle's 2022 review records David Rudiak's objections on those grounds and also notes his criticisms of aspects of the original paper's statistics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

But that defence changes the evidential status of the word. If specialised [Roswell knowledge]({{ 'knowledge-effect/' | relative_url }}) is necessary before “victims” becomes visible, then “victims” cannot simultaneously be offered as a straightforward example of text that survives readers' expectations. The experimental evidence places it in a different category from “Fort Worth”, “story” and “weather balloons”.

## What should count as a robust Ramey-memo word?

The experiment suggests a conservative standard for evaluating future decipherment claims: **reward independent positional replication before rewarding narrative coherence**. A proposed word becomes more persuasive when readers who do not know the expected subject independently identify substantially the same letters, word length and location.

Houran and Randle accordingly proposed that future work use the best available raw imagery, standardised enhancement procedures and at least three independent laboratories experienced in archival-document transcription. Crucially, those laboratories should be blind to the supposed content. Their outputs could then be compared for agreement on specific words at precise positions rather than for whether each laboratory could construct a plausible Roswell narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

They also identified useful structural constraints: establish character spacing from clearer regions, estimate word lengths before guessing words, and test whether proposed sentences satisfy ordinary grammar and syntax while remaining alert to possible military shorthand. These safeguards do not magically recover missing pixels. Their value is that they narrow the freedom an interpreter has to make an attractive candidate word fit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The 2022 reassessment did not overturn the central uncertainty. Randle concluded that attempts to decipher the memo still had not yielded definitive results capable of excluding competing explanations, while arguing that improved digital scans might justify further work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

## The safest reading is smaller than the famous transcription

For readers trying to distinguish visible signal from Roswell-informed expectation, the most defensible takeaway is deliberately modest. **“Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” form the clearest experimentally demonstrated nucleus because readers found them in corresponding locations across Roswell, atomic-bomb and blind conditions. “Land” is a weaker fourth candidate because it crossed the two prompted groups but not the blind control.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Even those fragments should be treated as probable perceptual anchors rather than certified transcription. The experiment had methodological limitations, the complete original response sheets no longer survived when the paper was prepared, and later debate has not produced an independently replicated full text. What the dataset does provide is a useful governance rule for claims about the Ramey memo: confidence should rise when a reading survives incompatible expectations **and** appears at the same physical location, and fall sharply when an interpretation depends on supplying a surrounding story first.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

On that standard, the most interesting finding is almost the opposite of a dramatic decipherment. Different readers could apparently converge on a few islands of text while disagreeing about the sea of language around them. Those shared islands are evidence that the photograph may contain recoverable information. They are not, by themselves, evidence that any of the celebrated full-message reconstructions has recovered what General Ramey's document actually said.
