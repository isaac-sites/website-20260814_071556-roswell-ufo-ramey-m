## Introduction

The Ramey photograph can look reasonably sharp as a press photograph and still fail as a photograph of tiny typewritten characters. Those are different standards of sharpness. J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }}) photographed Brigadier General Roger Ramey during the Fort Worth press episode of 8 July 1947; Johnson later recalled carrying a 4-by-5-inch Speed Graphic, and contemporary reporting confirms that he made a series of photographs in Ramey's office. The paper now called the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) was therefore an incidental detail inside a much wider composition, not a document deliberately copied at close range.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: J. Bond Johnson&#x27;s Statement on His Visit to General RameyNovember 9, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 9, 2015</span></span></span>

That distinction is crucial. A face, uniform or sheet of paper can appear acceptably focused even when the lens has not preserved enough contrast at the much finer scale needed to distinguish one small typed letter from another. Optical resolution is fundamentally about retaining contrast as details become finer; Nikon's explanation of modulation transfer function (MTF), for example, explicitly distinguishes the reproduction of coarse and fine spatial detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://imaging.nikon.com/imaging/lineup/lens/mtf_chart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imaging.nikon.com">[Nikon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imaging.nikon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MTF Chart &#124; NIKKOR Lenses &#124; Nikon Consumer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MTF Chart &#124; NIKKOR Lenses &#124; Nikon Consumer...</span></span></span> For the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo, even modest loss of fine-detail contrast can turn a recognisable line of typing into ambiguous strokes and smudges.

## Character-Level Sharpness Is a Much Harder Test

Ordinary photographic sharpness answers a forgiving question: does the photograph look focused at its intended size? Reading the memo asks something much more demanding: did the optical system preserve the particular edges and gaps that distinguish tiny characters after that small part of the [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) is greatly enlarged?

The distinction can be expressed in terms of spatial frequency. Broad features such as Ramey's outline, facial structure or the boundary of the sheet change relatively slowly across the negative. Individual typewriter strokes change over much shorter distances. Nikon describes MTF as the ability of a lens to reproduce contrast at different numbers of lines per millimetre, noting that performance at the higher spatial frequency is more indicative of resolution. A lens can therefore render coarse structures convincingly while transferring much less contrast from very fine structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://imaging.nikon.com/imaging/lineup/lens/mtf_chart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imaging.nikon.com">[Nikon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imaging.nikon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MTF Chart &#124; NIKKOR Lenses &#124; Nikon Consumer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MTF Chart &#124; NIKKOR Lenses &#124; Nikon Consumer...</span></span></span>

That is directly relevant to the memo. Houran and Randle's review describes the paper as tilted rather than square to the camera and records an early examination by former NASA researcher Richard Haines in which vague word-like forms could be perceived but individual letters generally could not. Haines reportedly thought better material might reveal more, but did not expect the existing image to yield much reliable text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The striking point is that **seeing typing is easier than identifying letters**. A row of alternating light and dark marks can survive enough optical degradation to announce, unmistakably, "there is text here". Yet recognising whether one particular mark is an `R`, `B`, `P` or `8` requires preservation of much smaller features: a loop closing, a vertical stroke continuing, a diagonal surviving, or a tiny patch of white separating two black strokes. The photograph may preserve the first level of information while losing the second.

## Focus Does Not Have a Simple Sharp-or-Blurred Boundary

A lens focuses perfectly at one object distance. Objects slightly nearer or farther away produce progressively larger blur spots on the image plane. Photographers normally tolerate some of that blur and call a range of distances "acceptably sharp"; this is depth of field. ZEISS explains that points outside the exact plane of focus become circles of confusion, or defocus blur spots, and that whether those spots are considered acceptably small depends partly on the eventual enlargement and viewing conditions. It specifically warns that conventional depth-of-field assumptions become inadequate for unusually large or demanding reproductions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[Lenspire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

The Ramey memo is an unusually demanding reproduction. Researchers are not merely viewing Johnson's whole photograph at newspaper size. They are effectively asking a minute crop of it to withstand enormous enlargement. A degree of defocus that would be invisible in the complete press photograph can consequently become important when examining individual characters.

There is also no abrupt optical boundary at which one object is "in focus" and another suddenly is not. Sharpness falls gradually as an object moves away from the exact focus distance. This means that descriptions of the original photograph as simply "sharp" do not establish that the memo itself was recorded at maximum attainable resolution. The relevant question is much narrower: **how much fine-detail contrast survived at the memo's position in the scene?** General visual inspection of the whole photograph cannot answer that precisely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[Lenspire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

This also explains why the memo's angle matters without requiring it to be dramatically out of focus. Houran and Randle observed that the paper was not facing the camera straight on, although they considered the tilt modest enough that reading it might conceivably be possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> A tilted or locally curved sheet places different portions of its surface at slightly different distances from the camera. Those differences need not visibly blur the paper as an object; at character scale, however, they can contribute to unequal sharpness from one region to another.

## Lens Resolution and Focus Loss Work Together

Focus is not an isolated limit. The photographed letter has to survive an entire imaging chain. The lens must first reproduce its fine structure with useful contrast, that structure must fall sufficiently close to the plane of best focus, and the film must then record what reaches it. Later copying, enlargement and scanning cannot restore distinctions that disappeared during that original exposure.

This is why a large 4-by-5 negative is an advantage but not a guarantee. Johnson recalled owning a four-by-five Speed Graphic, giving the original photograph a comparatively generous film area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/11/j-bond-johnsons-statement-on-his-visit.html?showComment=1447183915509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: J. Bond Johnson&#x27;s Statement on His Visit to General RameyNovember 9, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 9, 2015</span></span></span> Large format can record substantial detail under favourable conditions. But the memo occupies only part of Johnson's composition, and each character occupies a tiny part of the memo. What matters for decipherment is therefore not the nominal dimensions of the negative alone, but how many reliably differentiated image structures were recorded across each character after lens performance, focus and film response had all taken their toll.

A useful way to picture the mechanism is to imagine two adjacent black typewriter strokes separated by a narrow white gap. Perfect reproduction would preserve two dark features with the bright separation between them. Slight optical blur spreads light and dark information sideways. The strokes become softer, their contrast falls and the gap narrows. With enough degradation, all three structures merge into one broader mark.

That process is especially damaging to characters because letters are differentiated by exactly those small structures. Once a loop in `P` no longer closes clearly, or the diagonal in `N` becomes inseparable from its verticals, enlargement does not resolve the uncertainty. It merely produces a larger representation of the already blurred pattern.

Technical discussion of the Ramey photograph has long recognised this cumulative problem. Tim Printy's critical review lists lens resolution, camera focus and film resolution among the original photographic limitations, followed by further losses introduced by processing, printing and scanning. More importantly for this page's narrow question, it describes the characters themselves as blurry in places and potentially not accurately recorded on the film.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span></span></span> The exact contribution of defocus cannot be isolated retrospectively from all those other components, but neither can it safely be treated as irrelevant merely because the overall photograph looks usable.

## Tiny Letters Make Modest Blur Decisive

The consequences become clearer when comparing large and small features. Suppose a blur spot is small compared with Ramey's eye or the edge of his sleeve. Those objects can remain visually well defined. The identical blur spot might be comparable in width to a significant portion of a typewriter stroke. At that scale it can erase the very feature needed to classify the character.

That is why the memo presents an unusually unforgiving threshold problem. If a character is represented generously enough on the negative, slight softness merely rounds its edges. If the same character is already close to the resolving limit, the identical amount of softness can change its apparent identity. `M`, `N`, `W` and `V`, for example, depend heavily on the survival of diagonals and internal separations. `B`, `P`, `R`, `S` and similar forms depend on small curves, openings and junctions. A blur that removes one of those cues leaves several plausible letters rather than one identifiable letter.

This is not just a hypothetical difficulty inferred from photographic theory. McNeff and Fishbine, in work quoted by Printy, reported problems distinguishing character forms in processed versions of the Ramey image, including confusion between letter groups such as `W`, `V`, `N` and `M`. They also warned that some purported characters were being inferred from grain patterns rather than securely recorded letter structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span></span></span>

Houran and Randle's review provides a complementary real-world result. Investigators using different equipment and methods repeatedly disagreed about the same passages. John Kirby could identify little with [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) beyond limited portions; Russ Estes examined enlarged material with high-end imaging equipment and a 9,000-dpi scan yet said he could not reliably decipher the text, apart from tentative guesses. Other researchers produced much more extensive readings, but those readings often disagreed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That pattern is exactly what one expects when character information lies near an imaging threshold. Some stronger combinations of strokes survive well enough to constrain a word. Others retain only enough structure to invite competing interpretations.

## Why More Magnification Cannot Repair Missed Focus

Digital enlargement can make the surviving pattern easier to inspect, but it cannot retroactively refocus the 1947 exposure. If a point on the original document was recorded as a blur spot rather than a sharply localised point, scanning that spot into hundreds of modern pixels simply describes the blur more precisely.

This is why scanning resolution and photographic resolution must be kept conceptually separate. A very dense scan may be valuable because it avoids throwing away information that actually exists in the negative. It can also reveal film grain and permit more controlled processing. But once the scan samples the negative adequately, adding further digital pixels cannot recreate high-frequency letter detail that the lens-and-film system failed to record.

The history of Ramey-memo examination illustrates the point. The US Air Force reported that a national-level organisation digitised the available photographs in 1994 but found their quality insufficient to visualise the sought details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/The-DAF-Legacy-Vault/DAF-History-Publications/igphoto/2003789023/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF History The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert</span></span></span> Later investigators obtained high-resolution scans from original-negative material and applied much more elaborate enlargement and enhancement, yet substantial disagreement over individual characters remained. Houran and Randle ultimately recommended a controlled experiment using an equivalent camera, known period messages, different distances and different angles to determine empirically how much text such a photographic setup could preserve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That proposed experiment goes directly to the focus question. Rather than assuming that a generally sharp large-format photograph must contain readable characters, it would test the actual transition between readable and ambiguous type at the relevant image scale.

## What the Photograph Can and Cannot Establish

There is no strong basis for claiming that Johnson simply "missed focus" and thereby explaining the entire Ramey-memo problem. Some commentary has argued explicitly that the memo is not obviously out of focus and that film grain or signal-to-noise limitations dominate instead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/SUNlite7_6.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOShedding some light on UFOlogy and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 1, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2015</span></span></span> The surviving photograph does not permit the separate contributions of focus error, lens performance, film response and subsequent reproduction to be measured cleanly enough to assign a precise percentage to each.

The more defensible conclusion is narrower and more useful. **Overall photographic sharpness does not demonstrate character-level sharpness.** The memo was a small, angled incidental object in a wider press photograph. Its typed characters therefore demand much finer spatial information than is required for the photograph itself to look properly focused. Optical theory predicts that small departures from ideal focus reduce fine-detail contrast before they necessarily make coarse objects look conspicuously blurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/en/article/depth-of-field-and-manual-focusing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lenspire.zeiss.com">[Lenspire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lenspire.zeiss.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lenspire Depth of Field and Manual Focusing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Depth of Field and Manual FocusingMarch 6, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2018</span></span></span>

The surviving evidence behaves consistently with that limitation. Some larger textual patterns appear stable enough that independent readers have converged on particular words: Houran and Randle found cross-group agreement on readings including "Fort Worth TX", "story" and "weather balloons". Yet the same study found that interpretation of the document's broader meaning was strongly affected by suggestion, while earlier specialists disagreed substantially over many individual letters and words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That mixed result is more informative than calling the photograph simply sharp or blurred. The image plainly contains **some** textual information. What remains uncertain is whether it contains enough original character-level information to resolve the disputed passages uniquely. For the smallest typewritten characters, even modest optical softness can be the difference between a recorded letter and an ambiguous dark shape. No amount of later enlargement can guarantee recovery of the distinction if the 1947 lens-and-film image never preserved it.
