## Introduction

For anyone trying to extract information from the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), the University of Texas at Arlington (UTA) holdings are the key surviving source material because they preserve the Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* photographic record close to its original 1947 form. UTA’s catalogue identifies the relevant [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) material as black-and-white negatives dated 8 July 1947, while the US Air Force’s 1994 Roswell investigation says it obtained both original first-generation prints and first-generation negatives from the university.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/specialcollections_startelegram1940s/35502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mavmatrix.uta.edu">[MavMatrix]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mavmatrix.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mav Matrix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">General Ramey holding enlargement of telegram...</span></span></span>

That distinction is more important than whether a later online image merely looks sharper. Newspaper halftones, photographic enlargements, copy photographs and compressed web files can all introduce another reproduction stage. The UTA negatives therefore set the evidential ceiling: a careful modern scan can capture the surviving film more faithfully, but it cannot reconstruct letter shapes that J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})’s camera never resolved. That is why direct access to the UTA material has improved the Ramey memo debate without producing an agreed transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What the Star-Telegram archive preserved

The Ramey photograph is not simply an unidentified image that circulated through UFO literature until somebody found a better copy. It belongs to an identifiable newspaper archive. UTA describes its Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* Collection as a vast photographic holding containing roughly 179,500 print photographs and an estimated six million film and glass-plate negatives. UTA also records that the newspaper donated more than one million photographs, negatives, clippings and other records from its older holdings to the university in 1985.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/specialcollections_startelegram/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mavmatrix.uta.edu">[MavMatrix]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mavmatrix.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uta.edu.</span></span></span>

Within that archive, UTA catalogues the Ramey memo image under identifier 10002018. Its description calls the item an enlargement of the telegram held by General Roger Ramey concerning the Roswell incident; the archival date is 8 July 1947, the collection is the Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* Collection, and the listed original format is “Negatives, Black & White”. The online derivative is a JPEG, which is an important distinction: the downloadable web image is not itself the archival [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) even though its catalogue record points back to negative source material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/specialcollections_startelegram1940s/35502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mavmatrix.uta.edu">[MavMatrix]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mavmatrix.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mav Matrix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">General Ramey holding enlargement of telegram...</span></span></span>

UTA itself has highlighted the memo as a distinctive part of its Special Collections. In 2017, *UTA Magazine* described the Ramey memo photograph as being housed in the university’s Special Collections and noted a $10,000 reward then being offered for a definitive decipherment. The institutional interest is significant less because of the reward than because it confirms where the relevant physical photographic record resides.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span>

This [provenance]({{ 'provenance/' | relative_url }}) gives the UTA material an advantage that many familiar reproductions lack. A newspaper clipping or a JPEG copied from another website may preserve the general scene perfectly well, but it does not necessarily reveal how many reproduction stages separate the viewer from Johnson’s exposure. With the UTA material, researchers can work from an identified archival collection and compare derivatives against the physical photographic source.

## Why the negatives outrank later reproductions

The principle is straightforward: the camera negative is the first photographic generation. The US National Archives defines an original negative as the film element exposed in the camera and explicitly calls it the first generation of the image. Its archival guidance also distinguishes original negatives, duplicate negatives and reference copies as separate physical occurrences rather than treating them as interchangeable versions of the same photograph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/products/definitions/film-glossary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesGlossary of National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) Film Element Acronyms &#124; National ArchivesOctober 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 25, 2023</span></span></span>

That hierarchy becomes unusually important with the Ramey photograph because the evidence under examination is not the principal subject of Johnson’s frame. Researchers are trying to distinguish tiny typewritten features on a sheet of paper held within a much larger photograph. A reproduction can therefore remain an excellent picture of Ramey while becoming a poor source for the memo.

The Library of Congress's present duplication specifications illustrate the technical point. Its standard for a 4-by-5-inch black-and-white negative is a 1,200 dpi, 8-bit greyscale, uncompressed TIFF scan. The exact specification is not a prescription for the Ramey negative, but it demonstrates why archives treat direct film capture differently from ordinary document copying: small photographic negatives can justify comparatively dense sampling when fine image information matters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/duplicationservices/products-pricing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Products &amp; Pricing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressProducts &amp; Pricing - Duplication Services (Library of Congress)...</span></span></span>

A high-resolution scan of a later print cannot necessarily recover information already sacrificed while that print was made. Printing introduces its own focus, contrast and tonal characteristics; copying the print introduces another transfer. Newspaper reproduction can add halftone structure. Subsequent resizing, sharpening and JPEG compression can further change the appearance of tiny ambiguous marks. For memo analysis, therefore, “high resolution” and “close to the original” are separate properties.

This is also why the physical negative should not be confused with any one digital file derived from it. A new scan can be technically superior to an older scan because of better optics, sampling, focus or handling of tonal information. But both remain representations of the same finite photographic record. The National Archives describes high-specification preservation masters as captures made from the most original or complete source available and notes that such files can retain information beyond the main picture, including markings along film edges.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/products/definitions/preservation-copy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Preservation Master &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Preservation Master &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

## The Air Force examination confirms the source hierarchy

One of the strongest pieces of evidence for the importance of UTA comes from a source that was not trying to establish an extraordinary interpretation of the memo: the US Air Force’s 1994 Roswell investigation.

The report states that investigators obtained from UTA a set of original, first-generation prints of the Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* photographs depicting Ramey and Major [Jesse Marcel]({{ 'marcel/' | relative_url }}) with the debris. It then says that first-generation negatives were subsequently obtained as well. When investigators noticed visible text on the paper in Ramey’s hand, they sent the photograph to what the report described as a national-level organisation for digitisation and photographic interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

The result is crucial to understanding both the value and the limitation of the archive. According to the report, the organisation advised on 20 July 1994 that even after digitisation the photographs were of insufficient quality to resolve the details being sought. The Air Force was simultaneously attempting to examine the memo and disputed markings on the photographed debris.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

That finding does not prove that no character or word can ever be recognised. Technology, scanning procedures and analytical methods can improve, and later researchers have indeed returned to the source. What it does establish is that the memo's difficulty cannot reasonably be blamed only on the poor newspaper reproductions or small web images familiar to the public. Investigators had already moved upstream to UTA’s early-generation photographic material and still encountered a fundamental resolution problem.

In other words, access to the negative removes one major source of uncertainty — unnecessary copying — but exposes another: limitations already present in the original exposure.

## Successive scans kept returning to UTA

The importance of the UTA material becomes even clearer when the history of later memo research is considered. Researchers did not simply keep enlarging the same newspaper clipping. They repeatedly sought better derivatives of the *Star-Telegram* source.

A 2002 study by James Houran and Kevin Randle described its raw material as an enlarged print obtained from the Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* Photograph Collection at UTA together with Stanton Friedman’s CD-ROM of high-resolution material. The study listed four relevant images: a negative rendering of the memo portion, an 8-by-10 enlargement made by UTA for Friedman, an older 8-by-10 enlargement, and an 11-by-14 enlargement made earlier in 1998.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That list is useful because it reveals something often lost when enhanced Ramey images circulate online: there has never been just one “Ramey memo image”. Researchers have worked with different physical enlargements, digital captures and processing generations, all ultimately competing to represent the information on the archival photograph.

The same research also demonstrated why a superior source does not automatically yield superior certainty. Houran and Randle gave 176 participants Ramey memo material under different contextual conditions. Participants told that the document concerned Roswell or atomic-bomb testing reported more perceived words than participants given no suggested context. The authors concluded that the memo remained extremely difficult to read and found evidence that contextual suggestion influenced what viewers thought they saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That matters directly to the status of the UTA negative. Obtaining the best available source controls one class of error — degradation through copying — but it does not control interpretation bias. The cleaner the source chain becomes, the easier it is to see that image quality and human perception are separate problems.

## The 2015 rescanning effort tested the negative again

Researchers returned directly to UTA in April 2015 for another attempt using contemporary photographic techniques. Kevin Randle's account says UTA staff helped arrange access, including use of a photographic microscope, while David Rudiak and specialists in photography and forensic analysis produced dozens of scans under different conditions. Randle reported that some resulting files approached 250 MB. These accounts come from an involved researcher rather than an independent technical audit, so the procedural details should be treated as participant reporting rather than institutional certification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/09/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

The outcome is nevertheless instructive because it did not produce the dramatic breakthrough that might be expected if previous failures had simply resulted from inadequate digital resolution. Randle wrote that the newer work improved the memo only marginally and did not yield universal agreement about its wording. He attributed some of the difficulty to the physical geometry of the photographed document, including its fold and its orientation relative to the camera.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/09/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blogspot.com.</span></span></span>

The physical negative itself also creates a preservation problem. Photographic film is not an infinitely reusable data carrier. The National Archives warns that photographic emulsions can be scratched or otherwise damaged through handling and advises maintaining clean viewing and digitisation equipment to avoid abrasion. Digitisation is therefore valuable partly because it allows researchers to work repeatedly with a high-quality surrogate rather than continually handling an irreplaceable original.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/holdings-maintenance/photographs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Photographs &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Photographs &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, the ideal research arrangement is consequently not unlimited physical access to the negative. It is careful, controlled capture of the negative at the highest useful quality, preservation of minimally processed master files and open comparison of analytical results derived from those masters.

## Better source material did not create an agreed transcript

The history of proposed readings is itself evidence of the negative’s limitations. In the 2002 study, previously published investigators sometimes agreed on isolated words or structures while disagreeing sharply elsewhere. The comparison table recorded proposed readings involving terms such as “victims”, “wreck”, “Fort Worth”, “weather balloons” and competing signatures or names, but the surrounding text varied substantially among interpreters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The study's experimental component supplied a warning against treating such apparent words as self-authenticating. People given different contextual suggestions produced context-compatible readings from the same ambiguous visual material. That does not demonstrate that every proposed Ramey word is imaginary. It does mean that a persuasive transcription requires more than the claim that a particular sequence “looks obvious” after enhancement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Later work has not closed that gap. Randle's 2022 review in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration* — a source sympathetic to continued Roswell investigation — nevertheless states that attempts to decipher the Ramey memo have not produced definitive results capable of ruling competing explanations in or out. It identifies higher-quality digital scans as an area where further empirical work may still be possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

That combination is revealing. Researchers who believe the memo remains potentially important have continued to pursue the UTA source precisely because it is the best material available. Yet repeated access to better derivatives of that source has not generated a stable, independently accepted full reading.

## What closer source access can establish

The UTA negatives substantially improve what can responsibly be said about the Ramey photograph, even without solving the memo.

First, they establish a strong photographic provenance. The relevant image belongs to the Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* collection, is catalogued by UTA with the 8 July 1947 date, and is associated with black-and-white negative material rather than appearing only as an unattributed later reproduction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/specialcollections_startelegram1940s/35502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mavmatrix.uta.edu">[MavMatrix]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mavmatrix.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mav Matrix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">General Ramey holding enlargement of telegram...</span></span></span>

Second, they allow researchers to minimise generational loss. Working from the archival negative, or from a carefully produced preservation-quality scan of it, is preferable to treating a newspaper halftone, book reproduction or unknown web JPEG as equivalent evidence. That principle is consistent with normal archival distinctions between original negatives, duplicate elements and reference copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/lcdrg/elements/copy-status" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Copy Status &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Copy Status &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

Third, the archive permits claims about enhancements to be tested against a common source. If an alleged letter appears only after aggressive sharpening or processing but cannot be located consistently in minimally processed captures of the negative, confidence in that letter should fall. Conversely, a feature that persists across independently produced direct scans deserves more attention than one found only in a late derivative.

Most importantly, UTA access helps separate two questions that are frequently conflated: **Is this the best surviving photographic source?** and **Is the memo therefore readable?** The evidence strongly supports the first proposition. It does not establish the second.

## What the negative cannot settle

An original negative can preserve the fullest surviving photographic record while still being inadequate for a definitive transcription. The Ramey memo occupies only a small part of Johnson’s photograph, is not square to the camera and is affected by folds and perspective. Once a typewritten stroke falls below the optical resolving power of the original exposure, no amount of later enlargement can recover its exact shape.

This is the central limit of enhancement. Digital processing can enlarge differences already recorded in the film, alter contrast, suppress some noise and make comparisons easier. It can also make ambiguous structures look more letter-like. It cannot create new historical optical information. The failure of the 1994 Air Force analysis, the disagreements documented in the 2002 experiment and the absence of consensus following later high-resolution work all point towards the same practical boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[ufotransparency.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

Nor should “original negative” be treated as a guarantee that every modern scan captures exactly the same useful information. Focus, scanner optics, sampling, film flatness, illumination and processing choices matter, while the physical condition of ageing film matters as well. This is why preserving high-quality, minimally processed masters is more useful scientifically than relying on a single spectacular-looking enhancement. Archival practice similarly favours high-specification master captures from the most original source available and warns that handling photographic originals itself carries risks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/products/definitions/preservation-copy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Preservation Master &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Preservation Master &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

The most defensible status of the UTA material is therefore both stronger and narrower than some claims made about the Ramey memo. The Fort Worth *Star-Telegram* negatives are the essential source against which later copies and enhancements should be judged. They bring analysis as close as surviving evidence allows to Johnson’s 1947 exposure. But what survives on that exposure remains partly ambiguous.

## The UTA negatives set the evidential ceiling

The real importance of UTA is not that its archive secretly contains a perfectly readable memo awaiting sufficiently clever software. It is that the archive preserves the source material capable of testing that proposition.

A newspaper reproduction may lose detail because it is several generations removed. A digital enlargement may improve visibility while simultaneously introducing processing artefacts. An online JPEG may have an uncertain lineage altogether. The UTA negative provides the reference point from which those derivatives can be ranked.

That makes the negative both the strongest source and the hardest constraint on the Ramey memo debate. If a feature survives clearly and repeatedly in independent, high-quality captures of the original film, it has a stronger evidential claim than a feature visible only in a later reproduction. If the original film contains only unresolved grain or overlapping tonal structures at a disputed character position, no downstream copy can legitimately transform that uncertainty into historical certainty.

UTA's preservation of the *Star-Telegram* material therefore moved Ramey memo research beyond the weakest versions of the photograph. It did not move it beyond the limits of the photograph itself. The continuing absence of a generally accepted transcription, despite first-generation material, specialised digitisation and repeated rescanning, is not a reason to dismiss the archive. It is precisely what makes the archive so important: the UTA negatives show where reproduction loss ends and the unresolved information in Johnson's original exposure begins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>
