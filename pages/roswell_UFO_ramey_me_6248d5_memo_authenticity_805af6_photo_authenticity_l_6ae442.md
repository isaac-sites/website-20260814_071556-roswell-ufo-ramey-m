That evidence can establish an important minimum claim: the sheet was not created by a modern Roswell researcher and digitally inserted into a later picture. A genuine 1947 photographic [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) contains the image of Ramey holding a text-bearing sheet. But provenance does not make the camera an infallible document scanner. The photograph was a press photograph of Ramey, other officers and debris; the memo occupied only a comparatively small region of the frame and was not photographed square-on as a document-copying exercise.

The difference can be expressed simply:

* **[Authenticity]({{ 'authenticity/' | relative_url }}):** Is this genuinely the historical photograph it purports to be?

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Presence:** Does the photograph genuinely show a sheet carrying lines or marks consistent with text?
* **Legibility:** Were individual character shapes captured with enough information to discriminate reliably between possible letters and words?
* **Interpretation:** If some characters or words can be recognised, is there enough reliable text to reconstruct the message and its meaning?

</div>

Success at one level does not automatically produce success at the next. A photograph can provide excellent evidence that a particular person was holding a particular piece of paper while providing weak evidence about whether one blurred character was an **R**, **B**, **P** or something else.

The history of attempts to read the Ramey memo illustrates precisely that separation. In 1991, former NASA researcher Richard Haines examined an available image and reportedly found vague word-like forms and occasional possible letters but insufficient information for a useful reading. During the US Air Force's later Roswell investigation, a national-level organisation digitised photographic material in an attempt to examine both the text and purported markings on the debris. According to the Air Force report, it concluded in July 1994 that the photographs were of insufficient quality to visualise the requested details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why enlargement cannot restore missing detail

The intuitive mistake in discussions of the Ramey memo is to treat magnification as though it were equivalent to moving closer to the original sheet in 1947. It is not.

When a photographer physically moves closer, uses greater optical magnification or records the document on a larger portion of the negative, additional spatial information can reach the recording medium. Enlarging an already recorded photograph happens afterwards. It can spread the existing information over a larger display area, but it cannot repeat the exposure.

This matters enormously for tiny type. Each letter is made distinctive by small features: a gap, curve, diagonal stroke, serif or junction. When those features approach the resolving limit of the original image, several different letters can produce very similar recorded patterns. Film grain, optical blur, focus, exposure, the paper's angle to the camera and subsequent copying or scanning can further obscure the differences. The result may still look tantalisingly like writing because the larger structure — lines, spaces and dark clusters — survives even when the information needed to identify individual characters does not.

Modern forensic guidance makes the limitation explicit. The Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE) explains that digital enlargement requires adding pixels through interpolation. Those additional pixels are calculated from already recorded neighbouring values. Enlargement can improve *visual acuity* — making subtle existing information easier for a viewer to inspect — but it does not introduce detail absent from the original recording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/22-v-001-swgde-fundamentals-of-resizing-imagery-and-considerations-for-legal-proceedings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDE Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings - SWGDESeptember 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 22, 2022</span></span></span> NIST similarly defines resampling as changing image size or resolution by adding or subtracting pixels through interpolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/40251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">glossary term</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Resampling &#124; NISTJanuary 15, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 15, 2025</span></span></span>

That distinction explains why a very large Ramey-memo enlargement can look much clearer without necessarily becoming more evidentially informative. At ten-times enlargement, for example, one source pixel may influence a block of many displayed pixels. SWGDE notes that with a factor-of-ten resize, information represented by one original pixel may be interpolated into 100 pixels. The result is physically larger and potentially easier to examine, but those 100 displayed pixels do not represent 100 independent observations made by Johnson's camera.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/22-v-001-swgde-fundamentals-of-resizing-imagery-and-considerations-for-legal-proceedings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDE Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings - SWGDESeptember 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 22, 2022</span></span></span>

Different enlargement methods can also make the same source data look different. Nearest-neighbour enlargement largely exposes the block structure of the recorded pixels. Bilinear interpolation blends nearby values and can introduce blur or halos around edges. Bicubic interpolation uses a larger neighbourhood to produce smoother transitions. None changes the fundamental evidential ceiling imposed by the source image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/22-v-001-swgde-fundamentals-of-resizing-imagery-and-considerations-for-legal-proceedings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDE Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings - SWGDESeptember 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 22, 2022</span></span></span>

This is why the often-heard instruction to “scan the negative at a higher resolution” needs qualification. A better scan can prevent the digitisation stage from throwing away information that really exists in the photographic negative. That is valuable. But once scanning becomes fine enough to capture essentially all useful information present in the relevant part of the negative, still more scanner pixels cannot force the negative itself to contain sharper letter forms.

The Ramey debate has repeatedly reached this boundary. Even researchers sympathetic to further attempts at decipherment have acknowledged it. Kevin Randle wrote in 2016 that improved scans still had not reached the point at which a disputed reading could confidently be declared the exact wording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/04/ramey-memo-update.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: Ramey Memo Update</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Ramey Memo UpdateApril 26, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 26, 2016</span></span></span> The continuing absence of a consensus is therefore compatible with both an authentic negative and progressively better digitisation.

## How enhancement can create false confidence

Enhancement is not inherently illegitimate. Proper image processing can expose contrast relationships that are difficult to see in an unprocessed scan. Adjusting brightness or contrast, examining tonal ranges and applying carefully controlled sharpening can all make genuine recorded information more perceptible.

The danger arises when **better visibility is mistaken for better underlying resolution**.

SWGDE's forensic image-processing guidelines draw exactly this distinction. Sharpening, deblurring and edge enhancement increase the contrast of small details, but strong enhancement can also amplify noise and artefacts. Severe contrast adjustment can cause loss of detail or introduce artefacts, while excessive noise reduction can remove relevant detail. The guidelines therefore stress preservation of the original, documentation of processing steps and caution against processing that creates misleading information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/15-m-002-swgde-image-processing-guidelines/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Image Processing Guidelines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDE Image Processing Guidelines - SWGDE...</span></span></span>

That problem becomes acute with barely resolved text because human perception is extremely good at turning incomplete patterns into meaningful forms. Once an investigator expects a particular word, a collection of ambiguous light and dark patches can begin to look strikingly letter-like. Alternative processing settings may strengthen some apparent strokes while weakening others, producing several superficially plausible readings from the same underlying data.

The Ramey memo has provided an unusually direct demonstration. In a 2002 study, James Houran and Kevin Randle gave 176 participants versions of the memo under three different suggestion conditions: one group was told that it concerned Roswell, another that it concerned atomic-bomb testing, and a third received no suggested context. They found evidence that portions of the document may indeed contain recognisable information, but also concluded that interpretations were affected by suggestion and individual responses to ambiguity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is more informative than simply declaring the memo either “readable” or “unreadable”. **Legibility can exist on a continuum.** Some relatively strong features may support a particular letter or word; others may allow several alternatives; still others may contain too little discriminating information for any responsible transcription. A few recurring words therefore do not validate every neighbouring word in a proposed reconstruction.

The same principle is important when evaluating modern computational enhancement. SWGDE advises caution with newer computer-assisted and machine-learning interpolation because it can be difficult to determine precisely what processing has been applied and to reproduce it. For evidential work, a visually convincing reconstruction is not automatically evidence that every reconstructed edge existed in the source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/22-v-001-swgde-fundamentals-of-resizing-imagery-and-considerations-for-legal-proceedings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDE Fundamentals of Resizing Imagery and Considerations for Legal Proceedings - SWGDESeptember 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 22, 2022</span></span></span>

With the Ramey memo, that means a processed image should ideally be treated as an aid to interrogating the original data, not as a replacement for it. If an apparent **V** becomes unmistakable only after an algorithm predicts what a sharp letter probably ought to look like, the output may be an excellent reconstruction but poor evidence that the original negative uniquely encoded a **V**.

## The real limit is character discrimination

The most useful question is consequently not “How high-resolution is this scan?” but **“Does the source distinguish competing readings?”**

Suppose a processed region appears compatible with the word “WRECK”. The evidential task is not finished when an observer can see shapes resembling those five letters. A robust reading should survive comparison with plausible alternatives. Do the source features actually distinguish **W** from another cluster of strokes? Is the supposed **R** supported by a visible leg, or is that feature an interpolation or grain pattern? Would a different contrast setting make an alternative letter equally plausible? Do independent readers who have not been told the expected phrase converge on the same transcription?

This is where the Ramey memo becomes difficult. Houran and Randle reported some agreement between participants and earlier investigators at particular locations, suggesting that the photograph is not simply a featureless field into which anything whatsoever can be projected. Yet their experiment also showed substantial interpretative ambiguity. Their conclusion was therefore narrower than either extreme: there appears to be some legible information, but contextual meaning remains vulnerable to suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A useful comparison comes from a later Ramey-memo replication exercise reported by Randle. A deliberately prepared text was photographed at progressively greater distances until its apparent resolution resembled the memo. At that scale, some phrases remained apparently readable, especially when their content was already known, while other letters merged into ambiguous forms. Randle regarded the experiment as illustrating how easily prior knowledge could assist — and potentially contaminate — attempted readings of very small text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hjkc.de/_blog/5851-ufo-forschung---ufo-absturz-bei-roswell-1947--teil-40/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hjkc.de">[CENAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hjkc.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CENAPRaumfahrt+Astronomie-Blog von CENAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blog: UFO-Forschung - UFO-Absturz bei Roswell 1947? The replication is not a laboratory measurement proving the exact optical resolution of Johnson's 1947 negative. Camera, lens, film and document conditions differed. Its value is narrower: it demonstrates the mechanism at issue. Text can be plainly present, and even partly recoverable, while remaining below the threshold required for an unambiguous complete transcription.

## Authenticity does not settle the transcription

The strongest conclusion from the photographic evidence is therefore also the most restrained one.

The archival provenance of Johnson's photograph gives good reason to treat the photographed sheet as a genuine component of the 8 July 1947 scene. UTA's custody of the original negatives supports that historical proposition independently of later attempts to decipher the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span> But neither archival custody nor authenticity changes the optical information captured when the shutter was released.

Conversely, failure to obtain a definitive transcription does not count as evidence that the photograph is fabricated. It is exactly what can happen when an authentic photograph records an object well enough to establish its presence but not finely enough to preserve every feature needed for identification.

That leaves the Ramey memo in a more precise evidential category than either “solved” or “worthless”. Some broad features and perhaps some individual words may survive in the photographic information; proposed readings can therefore be tested against the source. But claims about consequential phrases require more than recognisable-looking enlargements. They require character-level features capable of discriminating the proposed wording from reasonable alternatives, preferably with independent readers and processing methods that do not depend upon knowing the desired result.

The enduring lesson of the Ramey photograph is consequently straightforward: **provenance can authenticate an image, but it cannot manufacture resolution**. Once the photograph has reached the limit of what Johnson's negative actually recorded, making the memo larger can make uncertainty larger too.
