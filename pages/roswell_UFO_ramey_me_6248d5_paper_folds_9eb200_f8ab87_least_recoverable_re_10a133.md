## Introduction

The hardest parts of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) to recover are not simply the blurriest-looking ones. They are the places where weak photographic detail coincides with severe paper deformation: the area above the upper fold, the text crossing the central fold, and the warped left margin beside Brigadier General Roger Ramey’s hand. David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }}), whose reconstruction work argues that substantial portions of the memo can be deciphered, has himself identified these three areas as especially prone to distortion and processing artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_paper_folds_9eb200_f8ab87_least_recoverable_re_10a133-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Worst Regions illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_paper_folds_9eb200_f8ab87_least_recoverable_re_10a133-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_paper_folds_9eb200_f8ab87_least_recoverable_re_10a133-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

Of the three, the upper and central [folds]({{ 'folds/' | relative_url }}) present the strongest geometric problem because the paper changes orientation rapidly across a small distance. The left margin adds a different difficulty: curvature, shadow and partial obstruction make it uncertain where some characters actually lie. That distinction matters. Software can enlarge recorded detail and can attempt to undo a known geometric deformation, but it cannot reliably reconstruct letter strokes that were never distinctly resolved in the photograph. Modern research on folded-document rectification therefore relies on estimating the paper's three-dimensional surface, often from multiple viewpoints, rather than treating the whole page as a single tilted rectangle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28362582/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents - PubMedMarch 2, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2017</span></span></span>

## Why the upper fold is exceptionally difficult

The region above the upper fold undergoes one of the largest changes in orientation visible on the memo. A recent independent reconstruction project describes the sheet as folded twice and estimates substantial changes in the angle of the upper portion. Those particular angle estimates are model-dependent and should not be treated as measurements established from the original paper, which no longer exists as an accessible physical document. The underlying photographic observation, however, is straightforward: the top portion is not coplanar with the section below it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/the-missing-u/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey Memo The missing U – Unlocking The Mystery</span></span></span>

That makes the upper fold qualitatively different from ordinary perspective skew. If an entire flat page is photographed at an angle, its geometry can often be approximated with one perspective transformation. Across a sharp fold, however, the surface on one side of the crease faces the camera differently from the surface on the other. The transition itself can contain curvature rather than a clean mathematical hinge. Folded-document research consequently models creases as ridges within a three-dimensional paper surface before attempting to unwrap the page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28362582/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents - PubMedMarch 2, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2017</span></span></span>

The [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) photograph has a further disadvantage: analysts do not have several purpose-made photographs of the exposed text from carefully chosen viewpoints. Research methods that successfully reconstruct folded documents from multiple images gain information because each viewpoint constrains the shape of the paper differently. The principal Ramey image instead records the readable face incidentally, while the memo occupies only a small portion of a 1947 photograph. The surviving source therefore offers far fewer geometric clues than a modern document-reconstruction system would normally seek. The original [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) is nevertheless important because researchers have worked from first-generation material preserved by the University of Texas at Arlington rather than solely from later newspaper reproductions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/05/the-ramey-memo-negative.html?showComment=1462514194505" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo NegativeMay 5, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 5, 2016</span></span></span>

Rudiak's own description of his digital reconstruction illustrates the practical consequence. He reported morphing the image to correct perspective, straighten lines, equalise character dimensions and remove the apparent folds. Yet he specifically cautioned that this procedure introduces artefacts, especially in highly distorted letters above the top fold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

That qualification is more important than the apparent neatness of a finished reconstruction. Once a severely foreshortened patch is stretched into a front-facing shape, a few ambiguous source pixels may occupy many pixels in the corrected image. The result can look clearer geometrically without containing more original letter information. A straightened character is therefore not necessarily a better-observed character.

## The central fold changes characters within a line

The central fold creates a slightly different problem because lines of text appear to run through a region whose orientation changes across the crease. Here the difficulty is not merely deciding how far to rotate an entire line. Different parts of the same line may require different corrections.

That distinction is fundamental to document dewarping. Modern systems explicitly use text lines and page boundaries as constraints because nonlinear paper deformation causes the geometry to vary across an image. Research has shown that correcting those local distortions can improve character-recognition performance, but doing so requires a sufficiently reliable estimate of the underlying deformation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/html/Jiang_Revisiting_Document_Image_Dewarping_by_Grid_Regularization_CVPR_2022_paper.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openaccess.thecvf.com">[CVF Open Access]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openaccess.thecvf.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jiang Revisiting Document Image Dewarping by Grid Regularization CVPR 2022 paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CVF Open AccessCVPR 2022 Open Access Repository...</span></span></span>

The Ramey memo provides weak evidence for exactly that estimate. Rudiak notes that where letters and words are already indistinct, locating them involves what he calls “educated guesswork”; their assumed positions then influence the warp model used to straighten the page. He singles out the centre of the page around the middle fold as one of the areas where distorted characters can become smeared during processing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

This produces an important circularity risk. To undo the fold accurately, an analyst needs good control points showing where features on the distorted paper ought to map. But when those features are themselves blurred characters, deciding where their strokes or baselines belong may depend partly on what the analyst thinks the characters are. Contemporary dewarping research similarly treats reliable control points, text-line structure or reconstructed surface geometry as central to the rectification problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10543" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Document Dewarping with Control Points</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Document Dewarping with Control PointsMarch 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 20, 2022</span></span></span>

The central fold should therefore be regarded as a transition zone rather than as one uniformly unreadable strip. Characters well away from the bend may retain enough structure to constrain the surrounding line. Characters lying directly across the rapidly changing surface are less secure. A proposed transcription that happens to cross the fold should consequently carry less evidential weight than an equally plausible reading from a relatively flat, well-exposed portion of the memo.

## The warped left margin combines geometry with missing detail

The left margin is difficult for another reason: several degradations overlap there. Rudiak identifies Ramey’s thumb as warping the paper at the left side and reports that shadow makes it difficult to establish exactly where some letters are positioned. Other descriptions of the photograph likewise note that portions of the left margin are obscured by Ramey’s hand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

A warped margin is particularly troublesome because the edge of the paper would otherwise be a valuable geometric reference. In ordinary document rectification, page boundaries and text lines help establish how the sheet has been distorted. Modern dewarping research explicitly exploits those features to improve the mapping from a warped photograph to a flattened page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/html/Jiang_Revisiting_Document_Image_Dewarping_by_Grid_Regularization_CVPR_2022_paper.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openaccess.thecvf.com">[CVF Open Access]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openaccess.thecvf.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jiang Revisiting Document Image Dewarping by Grid Regularization CVPR 2022 paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CVF Open AccessCVPR 2022 Open Access Repository...</span></span></span>

At Ramey’s hand, however, that reference geometry becomes less dependable. The paper bends under or around the grip; the margin does not follow the same projection as a flat central patch; shadow reduces local contrast; and the hand can physically hide information. Those are different kinds of loss. Curvature can sometimes be modelled. Shadow may sometimes be mitigated through tonal processing. Occlusion is absolute: if the hand covered a stroke when the shutter fired, no later [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) can retrieve that stroke from the photograph.

This makes the left edge especially vulnerable to uncertain estimates of word beginnings. Suppose only the middle and end of a word are reasonably visible while its first characters approach the warped, shadowed margin. Flattening the line can produce a plausible-looking sequence, but the reconstruction may depend strongly on assumptions about spacing and character position. Rudiak explicitly acknowledges that uncertainty over where indistinct words lie affects his warp model.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

The problem is therefore not that every letter near the left edge is necessarily illegible. It is that confidence can change abruptly along the same line. A recognisable cluster in the relatively flat interior does not automatically validate letters inferred nearer the thumb.

## Which region is worst?

There is no defensible single numerical ranking because no published controlled experiment has measured character-recovery accuracy separately for the three Ramey regions. The evidence supports a more useful comparison.

**Above the upper fold**, the dominant problem is severe [foreshortening]({{ 'foreshortening/' | relative_url }}) and a sharp change in surface orientation. Rudiak identifies this area as one where rectification can smear highly distorted characters. It is among the poorest regions for recovering exact letter shapes from geometry alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

**Across the central fold**, the problem is changing geometry within individual text lines. A single global rotation or perspective correction cannot restore such a region accurately. Because uncertain character locations can also influence the warp model, readings immediately across the crease deserve particularly cautious treatment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

**Along the warped left margin**, the problem is compounded: local curvature occurs alongside shadow and physical obstruction by Ramey’s hand. This is arguably the most intrinsically unrecoverable zone wherever characters actually disappear behind the hand, because rectification cannot recreate information that the camera never saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span>

The distinction between *hard to rectify* and *impossible to recover* is crucial. A severely angled but still resolved character may be recoverable after a well-founded transformation. A partly occluded or insufficiently resolved character may remain indeterminate regardless of enlargement.

## Why enhancement cannot make the three zones equivalent

The history of attempts to read the memo demonstrates that better source material and better processing do not automatically produce agreement. Houran and Randle's 2002 study used an enlarged print and several high-resolution versions derived from University of Texas at Arlington material. Their experiment found substantial disagreement among readers and warned about perceptual and contextual confounds in deciphering the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

This does not establish that every proposed word is wrong. It does establish that the source is ambiguous enough for expectations and contextual inference to matter. That problem becomes most acute in the folded zones, where geometric processing introduces another layer of interpretation between the negative and the proposed letters.

The broader computer-vision literature reinforces the point. Successful dewarping methods seek to estimate surface geometry, boundaries and text-line structure rather than simply sharpening pixels. For genuinely folded sheets, researchers have gone further and reconstructed three-dimensional surfaces using several views specifically because folds cannot always be represented adequately by simple planar transformations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28362582/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Multiview Rectification of Folded Documents - PubMedMarch 2, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2017</span></span></span>

The Ramey memo lacks that luxury. There is no opportunity to re-photograph the sheet under controlled lighting, flatten it physically, photograph the text square-on, or acquire additional viewpoints optimised for three-dimensional reconstruction. Consequently, some uncertainty is locked into the 1947 exposure itself.

## The safest way to judge readings across the folds

The strongest reading of a disputed passage is not necessarily the one produced by the most elaborate enhancement. In these three regions, confidence should instead depend on how much of the proposed character structure survives across different treatments of the source.

A reading deserves more confidence when recognisable strokes are present before aggressive warping, when the interpretation remains stable under modest changes in brightness and contrast, and when neighbouring characters provide reliable geometric references. Confidence should fall when the proposed letters appear only after strong stretching, when changing the control points materially changes their shapes, or when the transcription fills areas compromised by a crease, deep shadow or Ramey’s hand.

That standard is particularly important because Rudiak — an advocate of a comparatively extensive reading of the memo — openly acknowledges the artefacts introduced by flattening precisely the upper fold, middle fold and left-margin regions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html?showComment=1546118226661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: David Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey MemoDecember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2018</span></span></span> His caution provides a useful boundary between two claims that are sometimes blurred together: that software can produce a geometrically straighter representation of the memo, and that every newly straightened mark can therefore be identified confidently as a particular letter.

They are not equivalent. The upper fold and central fold are chiefly difficult because the paper's changing orientation makes the transformation uncertain; the warped left margin is worse wherever curvature is compounded by shadow or occlusion. Those are the Ramey memo's principal missing-information zones. Any transcription that depends critically on characters inside them should therefore be treated as less secure than one supported by comparatively flat, exposed portions of the photographed sheet.
