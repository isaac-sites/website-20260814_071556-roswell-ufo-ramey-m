## Introduction

For the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), a credible character stroke is not simply a dark mark that can be made to look like a letter. It is a feature that stays in the same place, keeps broadly the same shape and remains connected to the same neighbouring marks when the underlying photograph is subjected to several reasonable image treatments. By that standard, the surviving evidence supports **some stable word-level structure, but not the confident recovery of every disputed letter**.

That distinction is important because processing can make weak features easier to see while simultaneously making them easier to overinterpret. The strongest published evidence for persistence comes from places where different working copies, different observers and different methods repeatedly recover similar structure. The 2002 [Houran–Randle study]({{ 'priming-study/' | relative_url }}) found notable agreement around “Fort Worth”, “[story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }})” and “weather balloons”, whereas highly consequential proposed readings elsewhere in the memo remained much less stable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What persistence across versions actually means

The Ramey photograph has not been examined through a single digital file. Houran and Randle reported using an enlarged print plus four high-resolution working images derived from Fort Worth Star-Telegram material: a [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) image of the memo region, an enlargement made by the University of Texas at Arlington, an older enlargement and another enlargement produced in 1998. They described these as the best raw data available to their participants at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

Later work returned to the photographic negative. Kevin Randle reported that a team made new scans at the University of Texas at Arlington in April 2015 with the involvement of specialists in forensic photography; some resulting files approached 250 MB. He also reported that the team examined the negative directly under magnification and regarded it as the original 4-by-5-inch negative made by photographer J. Bond Johnson. Crucially, the improved acquisition did **not** produce an immediate, universally accepted transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: October 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2015</span></span></span>

That history suggests a useful definition of a surviving stroke. Suppose one disputed character seems to contain a vertical stem, a short cross-stroke and a darker patch at its lower right. A strong case exists only if those components recur at corresponding coordinates when the image is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* viewed from more than one good source or scan;
* enlarged using different reasonable interpolation settings;
* displayed at different contrast and brightness levels;
* examined both with little processing and with moderate sharpening;
* compared with the neighbouring character spacing rather than isolated from its word.

</div>

The test is not whether every rendering looks identical. Contrast changes necessarily alter apparent thickness, and sharpening alters edge prominence. The question is whether the **same underlying geometry keeps reappearing**. A vertical stroke that remains vertical and anchored to the same baseline position is much more interesting than a tiny hook that appears only after one aggressive filter.

This approach also follows ordinary archival practice. The US National Archives warns that image enhancement can improve readability but can also remove minute image data, and recommends retaining unenhanced scans of intrinsically valuable material. Its guidance therefore treats enhancement as a derivative view rather than a replacement for the captured source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/technical/imaging-storage-report.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

## The memo has different levels of stroke survival

The available evidence does not justify treating every alleged word in the Ramey memo equally. Some regions have produced substantially more agreement than others.

The clearest published example is the phrase interpreted as “Fort Worth, Tex.” John Kirby reportedly identified “At Fort Worth, Tex.”, while documentarian Russ Estes, after extensive optical and digital examination, made essentially the same limited-confidence identification. Estes had used a large University of Texas enlargement, professional video equipment, microscopy and a very high-resolution scan, yet still declined to claim that he could reliably decipher the document as a whole.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That combination is revealing. A methodologically cautious observer could fail to read most of the page while still recognising enough persistent geometry in one region to propose “Fort Worth, Tex.” The important evidence is therefore not simply that the phrase makes contextual sense. It is that observers using different viewing arrangements repeatedly found compatible character structure at the same location.

Houran and Randle's larger experiment strengthened that point. They divided 176 participants among three conditions: one group was told the document concerned Roswell, another was told it concerned atomic-bomb testing, and a third was given no suggested subject. Despite measurable effects from suggestion, the researchers reported agreement between participants and earlier investigators on several words in corresponding positions. Their published discussion specifically highlighted “Fort Worth”, “story” and “weather balloons” as examples of material that appeared genuinely legible to some degree.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This is closer to a meaningful survival test than selecting the most attractive enhanced image. If differently primed observers looking at several source versions repeatedly locate broadly the same characters in the same coordinates, there is evidence that real photographic signal exists there.

It does **not**, however, follow that every proposed character in those words is equally secure. “Fort Worth” may survive at the word-pattern level because its letter spacing, overall shape and several stronger strokes recur, while one faint crossbar or curve within the phrase remains uncertain. Persistence should therefore be recorded at the smallest defensible scale: whole word, character, or individual stroke.

## Why sharpening can manufacture confidence

Sharpening does not recover the original typewritten page. In conventional digital sharpening, the processing increases local edge contrast. When the source contains both real character edges and photographic noise, both can be emphasised.

This is a recognised technical problem rather than something peculiar to the Roswell photograph. Research on image sharpening notes that widely used unsharp-masking techniques can generate halo artefacts and amplify noise. High-pass edge enhancement can likewise strengthen noise alongside genuine edge information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1049/iet-ipr.2013.0563" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[IET Research Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IET Research JournalsEfficient image sharpening and denoising using adaptive guided image filtering - Pham - 2015 - IET Image Processing...</span></span></span>

That matters unusually strongly for the Ramey memo because its characters occupy a tiny part of a scene photograph. The disputed information is already close to the scale at which photographic texture and genuine type can compete visually. A processing sequence can therefore transform a weak irregularity into something that looks remarkably like a serif, crossbar or closed loop.

Contrast manipulation poses a related problem. Raising contrast can separate dark type from lighter paper, but it can also force intermediate tones towards black or white. A broken stroke may appear continuous because several weak patches become similarly dark; alternatively, a genuine faint connection may disappear. The National Archives explicitly cautions that enhancement can remove minute image information even while increasing apparent legibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/technical/imaging-storage-report.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

Resizing adds another layer. The National Archives defines interpolation as calculating additional or fewer pixels from existing pixels when resizing an image. Those extra display pixels make a feature larger, but they are computed from the recorded pixels rather than being fresh observations of the photographed document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/initiatives/digital-photo-records.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Digital Photographic Records &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesDigital Photographic Records &#124; National ArchivesNovember 12, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2003</span></span></span>

For the Ramey memo, the practical consequence is simple: **a beautifully shaped letter visible only in one aggressively sharpened enlargement is weak evidence**. A less impressive fragment that remains recognisable through several restrained renderings is stronger.

## The disputed words show why the test matters

The difference between persistent structure and interpretive completion becomes particularly important around the famous proposed readings such as “disc” and “victims”.

Advocates of those readings argue that the relevant characters can be recovered repeatedly and that agreement from [independent readers]({{ 'reader-convergence/' | relative_url }}) supports them. David Rudiak, for example, has argued strongly that “VICTIMS” and “DISC” are recoverable from the photographed message and has developed a much larger reconstruction around those readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/05/the-ramey-memo-negative.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog A Different Perspective: The Ramey Memo Negative</span></span></span>

But the published comparison of competing readings shows why those claims require a higher evidential threshold. In the region where one interpretation found “Disk”, Estes reported seeing something closer to “ELA*”; Kirby and other researchers likewise produced readings that diverged substantially in important portions of the memo. Around another disputed passage, Kirby reportedly read “remains” where a more sensational reconstruction used “victims”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

These disagreements are not minor spelling differences. They imply different underlying stroke assignments. If one reader's vertical mark is the beginning of a “D”, another reader's interpretation may require that same tonal feature to belong to a completely different character or to the space between characters. That instability is precisely what a stroke-survival procedure is intended to expose.

By contrast, the repeated recognition of “Fort Worth”, “story” and “weather balloons” provides a useful internal control. The memo is evidently not uniformly featureless: some regions contain enough stable visual structure for observers to converge. Therefore, failure to obtain comparable convergence elsewhere cannot simply be dismissed by saying the whole photograph is difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The cautious conclusion is consequently asymmetric. Some portions appear to contain persistent recoverable structure; the controversial passages have not demonstrated the same degree of stability in the published evidence.

## How to compare one disputed stroke properly

A useful comparison should begin with the least processed, highest-quality source available and treat every enhanced version as a test image rather than an independent piece of evidence. The same original photographic information cannot become five separate observations merely because five filters have been applied to it.

For any claimed character, the comparison can be organised in four passes.

**1. Fix the coordinates first.** Mark the suspected character's position relative to features that are not in dispute: the line of text, neighbouring character centres, the paper edge or another stable landmark. This prevents the eye from unconsciously moving a candidate stroke between different grain clusters as processing changes.

**2. Compare restrained variants side by side.** Use the unenhanced scan, modest tonal adjustments, moderate sharpening and perhaps an alternative resampling method. Do not begin with the most dramatic rendering. The National Archives' recommendation to retain unenhanced images is especially relevant here because the untouched capture supplies the reference against which enhancement must be judged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/preservation/technical/imaging-storage-report.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

**3. Score components rather than guessing the word.** Ask whether the same vertical, diagonal, curve, gap or junction survives. A proposed “R”, for example, should not receive high confidence merely because the word containing it seems plausible. Its stem, bowl and diagonal leg should each have identifiable photographic support. If the diagonal leg exists only after strong sharpening, the character should remain uncertain.

**4. Look for failure as deliberately as success.** Reduce the sharpening, reverse the contrast change, inspect another source version and temporarily ignore the proposed transcription. A real stroke may become weaker, but its location and basic geometry should not migrate unpredictably. A feature that splits, changes orientation or vanishes whenever processing parameters move slightly is a candidate for grain or enhancement artefact rather than stable type.

The result should be expressed in graded terms. “Persistent vertical stroke; upper curve uncertain” is more informative than declaring an entire letter certain. Likewise, “word pattern repeatedly recognised, internal characters partly ambiguous” better describes the evidence than forcing every mark into a finished transcription.

## Independent readings are part of the survival test

Image persistence alone cannot remove human expectation. The same ambiguous mark can survive every contrast setting yet still permit two plausible character interpretations. That is where blind or semi-blind reading becomes important.

Houran and Randle's experiment is especially relevant because it demonstrated both sides of the problem. Participants showed significant agreement on some parts of the memo, suggesting genuine legibility, while suggestion about what the document supposedly concerned also influenced interpretations. Their conclusion was therefore not that the entire memo was unreadable, but that meaning assigned to its ambiguous portions was vulnerable to expectation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The sensible implementation is to separate **stroke detection from language interpretation**. An examiner should first record visible geometry without being shown a proposed sentence: for example, “dark vertical; possible upper horizontal; gap; two adjacent vertical marks”. Only afterwards should candidate letters or words be tested against that description.

Independent laboratories would strengthen the procedure further. Houran and Randle themselves advocated independent examination, and their subsequent correction of a transcription error concerning Estes's “Fort Worth, Tex.” reading was presented as an illustration of how errors can enter even careful transcription work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/501208456/jse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; Falsifiability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jse 16 2 &#124; PDF &#124; Parapsychology &#124; Falsifiability</span></span></span>

This is particularly important for the Ramey memo because historical context is unusually powerful. Once an examiner knows that another researcher expects “victims”, “disc”, “wreck” or “weather balloons”, ambiguous fragments become difficult to see neutrally.

## What the surviving strokes can actually establish

The strongest conclusion available from the processing history is narrower than either “the memo has been decoded” or “nothing can be read”.

The 1994 US Air Force investigation reported that a national-level organisation digitised the relevant photograph but found its quality insufficient to visualise the details sought. Later researchers obtained better scans, larger files and more sophisticated working copies, yet the 2015 rescanning effort still did not produce a universally accepted transcription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/national-level-organization-failed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[Ramey Memo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ramey Memo National-Level Organization FAILED! – Unlocking The Mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ramey Memo National-Level Organization FAILED! – Unlocking The Mystery</span></span></span>

At the same time, Houran and Randle's results indicate that some portions contain reproducible visual information. “Fort Worth”, “story” and “weather balloons” are important not because they settle the Roswell controversy, but because they demonstrate what comparatively strong persistence looks like: multiple observers recover similar structures at matching positions despite different expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That provides a practical standard for every more controversial proposed stroke. It should be considered increasingly credible when its position and geometry survive different source copies, restrained processing choices and independent inspection. It should be downgraded when its shape depends on one enhancement, when neighbouring characters have to be rearranged to accommodate it, or when equally qualified observers consistently derive incompatible letters from the same marks.

On that standard, the Ramey memo contains **recoverable fragments surrounded by substantially greater uncertainty**, rather than a uniformly readable hidden document. Image processing is most useful when it reveals which marks survive that uncertainty. It becomes least trustworthy when it turns the most unstable marks into the most confident-looking letters.
