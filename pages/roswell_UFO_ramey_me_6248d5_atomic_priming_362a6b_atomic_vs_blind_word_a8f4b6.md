## Introduction

The sharpest numerical result in James Houran and Kevin D. Randle’s [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) experiment was not a difference between Roswell believers and atomic-test readers. It was the gap between **readers who were given a [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) and readers who were given none**. Participants told that the blurred document concerned secret atomic-bomb testing reported an average of **4.8 deciphered words**, while the genuinely blind group averaged only **1.6**. In other words, the atomic-primed participants produced about **three times as many supposed words** from the same ambiguous photographic material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_atomic_priming_362a6b_atomic_vs_blind_word_a8f4b6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Word Counts illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_atomic_priming_362a6b_atomic_vs_blind_word_a8f4b6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_atomic_priming_362a6b_atomic_vs_blind_word_a8f4b6-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That 4.8-versus-1.6 comparison is crucial because the atomic condition supplied an alternative narrative rather than Roswell information. The blind group, not the atomic group, therefore provides the cleanest baseline for asking what readers could extract when nobody first told them what the memo was supposed to mean. The result supports a limited but important conclusion: contextual suggestion substantially increased the amount participants believed they could read. It does **not**, by itself, establish which individual readings were false or whether every part of the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo is illegible.

## The 4.8 versus 1.6 word-count gap

Houran and Randle recruited 176 participants and assigned them to three conditions: 59 received a Roswell-related suggestion, 58 were told that the document might concern secret atomic-bomb testing, and 59 received no proposed subject. The University of Adelaide repository identifies the resulting 2002 paper, *“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO Case*, as a *Journal of Scientific Exploration* article, volume 16, pages 45–66.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/3334" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digital.library.adelaide.edu.au">[Adelaide Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digital.library.adelaide.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

The average word counts were remarkably clear:

Reading conditionParticipantsMean words reportedAtomic-bomb suggestion584.8Roswell suggestion594.6Blind/no suggestion591.6

The overall effect of experimental condition on the number of words deciphered was reported as **F(2,173) = 63.08, p<.001**. A Tukey post-hoc comparison found that the Roswell and atomic groups did not differ significantly from one another in their word totals, while **both primed groups produced significantly more words than the blind group**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For this particular comparison, the atomic result is unusually informative. The researchers did not merely show that people primed with Roswell found more words than people given no context. A completely different story produced essentially the same increase. The atomic group actually had a slightly higher numerical mean than the Roswell group — 4.8 against 4.6 — although the study found no significant difference between those two means.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002...</span></span></span>

The useful contrast is therefore not **“Roswell makes people see words”**. It is broader: **providing an interpretive frame was associated with substantially more reported words than providing no frame**.

The size of the raw difference makes the point easy to see. The atomic group averaged 3.2 more words per person than the blind group. Expressed as a ratio, 4.8 divided by 1.6 is exactly 3. That does not mean suggestion made every individual participant see precisely three times as much, but at group level the atomic readers generated three times the mean number of proposed words.

## Why the blind group is the true control

Calling the atomic-test condition a “control” can obscure the experimental logic. It was a useful **comparison condition**, because its suggested subject had nothing to do with the extraterrestrial interpretation of Roswell. But it was not neutral. Those participants had already been told what sort of information they might encounter. The study explicitly described the third condition — in which participants were simply asked to study and try to read a photograph of a document — as the blind condition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span>

That distinction changes how the 4.8-word result should be interpreted. If the atomic group alone were compared with Roswell readers, the experiment would show that both groups could generate several apparent words, but it would provide no clean estimate of what happens without contextual prompting. The [blind readers]({{ 'blind-readers/' | relative_url }}) supply that baseline.

The actual outputs reinforce the distinction. Atomic-primed readers proposed scenario-compatible readings including **“glasses”, “morning”, “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”**. By contrast, the researchers said the blind participants produced only a few interpretations and that these did not strongly reflect a particular scenario.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noufors&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo from the Roswell UFO CaseMay 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2026</span></span></span>

Some words nevertheless appeared across conditions. The study reported cross-group agreement on readings including **“Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons”**, while “land” appeared in the two primed conditions. This is an important qualification: Houran and Randle did not conclude that the photograph contained no recoverable information whatsoever. They argued instead that some portions might genuinely be legible while the wider interpretation remained highly vulnerable to expectation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The blind group consequently performs two jobs at once. Its low word count shows what happened when the researchers withheld a proposed subject, while its limited areas of agreement with the other groups help identify candidate readings that might be less dependent on a particular narrative. That is why blind agreement is potentially more evidentially interesting than simply accumulating more words from primed readers.

## More time may explain part of the difference

The 4.8-versus-1.6 result should not automatically be treated as a pure measure of perceptual suggestion. The groups also differed in how long they worked. Atomic-test participants spent an average of **16 minutes** trying to decipher the memo, compared with **14 minutes** for the blind participants. Roswell-primed readers spent still longer, averaging 20 minutes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002...</span></span></span>

Houran and Randle themselves raised motivation as a possible explanation. A story involving an atomic-bomb accident or secret testing could simply make an otherwise obscure photograph more interesting. Greater interest might encourage someone to look harder, try more alternatives and consequently submit more candidate words. Their later discussion therefore linked more interesting contexts with greater time spent examining the memo and more perceived words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This matters because “priming” can operate through more than one route. The atomic narrative may have supplied expectations about **what** words ought to appear, but it may also have altered **how persistently** participants searched for them. The experiment does not cleanly separate those mechanisms.

The time difference also cannot simply explain away the finding. Atomic readers spent only about 14 per cent longer on the task than blind readers — 16 minutes rather than 14 — yet their mean reported word count was 200 per cent higher. That arithmetic does not establish causation, because the study was not designed to hold viewing time constant, but it shows why elapsed time alone is not an obviously sufficient explanation for the threefold word-count difference.

A later review by Randle acknowledged criticism of the original experiment, including arguments that participants had not spent enough time studying the images or had insufficient contextual information. It also reiterated that no direct or conceptual replication had been carried out by those critics to resolve the disputed design questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2713/1663" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

## What higher word counts can and cannot prove

The atomic-versus-blind comparison gives strong evidence for an **experimental effect on reported decipherment**: when readers received the atomic-test framing, they reported substantially more words than readers who received no framing, and the statistical analysis found the difference between the primed and blind conditions significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

What it does not provide is a truth test for every proposed word. A higher number of readings could arise from several overlapping processes: participants might search more intensively, lower their threshold for accepting uncertain letter shapes, fit ambiguous marks to words suggested by the supplied narrative, or experience some combination of these effects. Because there is no complete ground-truth transcript against which every response can be scored, “more words” cannot automatically mean either “more accurate” or “more imaginary”.

Nor does the 1.6-word blind average demonstrate that only 1.6 words in the real memo are readable. These were volunteers performing a difficult photographic-decipherment task, not specialist archival laboratories conducting a definitive reconstruction. Houran and Randle explicitly regarded the document as difficult to read and ultimately recommended analyses by independent, blind laboratories using standardised [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) procedures and comparing whether specialists independently identified the same words in the same positions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There is also a limitation in the surviving dataset that matters when scrutinising individual words. The researchers reported that the original participant score sheets had been discarded by a research assistant after the computerised database and notes had been prepared. Consequently, they no longer possessed complete participant-by-participant frequency information for every proposed word; detailed counts survived principally for readings recorded as common across conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That loss does not erase the reported group means or the statistical comparison, but it restricts what later investigators can independently reconstruct from the experiment. The **4.8 versus 1.6** finding is therefore more secure as a published group-level observation than the experiment is as a fully recoverable dataset for reanalysing every claimed decipherment.

## Why the threefold gap matters for the Ramey memo


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

For assessing claims about the Ramey memo, the most useful lesson is methodological rather than pro- or anti-Roswell. When people knew nothing about the supposed subject, they proposed relatively few words. Give another group an atomic-test story, and the average rose from 1.6 to 4.8 while atomic-compatible readings such as “flash” and “atomic” appeared. Give a third group a Roswell story, and the average similarly rose to 4.6 while different context-compatible readings appeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

That pattern makes sheer **quantity of deciphered words** a poor measure of reliability. A reader who extracts five or six words from a degraded photograph has not necessarily obtained more information than somebody who cautiously accepts only one or two. In ambiguous material, greater output may partly reflect a greater willingness or motivation to turn uncertain visual forms into meaningful language.

At the same time, the experiment leaves room for a smaller, more defensible category of evidence: words independently identified in the **same physical locations by readers who were not told what the document should say**. Houran and Randle themselves treated cross-condition agreement as evidence that portions of the memo might genuinely be legible, while warning that the document's broader meaning remained vulnerable to suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The atomic-test group's value, then, comes from its contrast with the blind readers. Its 4.8-word average shows how much apparent decipherment could emerge after an unrelated narrative had been supplied. The blind group's 1.6-word average establishes the more demanding benchmark. For claims about the Ramey memo to rise above the priming problem demonstrated by this experiment, the strongest evidence would not be ever-longer reconstructions produced by informed investigators, but **repeatable agreement among genuinely independent, blind readers about the same words in the same places**.
