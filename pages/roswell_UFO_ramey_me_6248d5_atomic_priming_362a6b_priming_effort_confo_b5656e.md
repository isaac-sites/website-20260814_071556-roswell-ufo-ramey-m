## Introduction

Yes — the 2002 [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) experiment leaves good reason to think that priming changed **effort as well as interpretation**. Participants told that the blurred document concerned Roswell spent about 20 minutes examining it; those given an atomic-bomb [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}) spent about 16 minutes; the blind group, told only that it was an old document, spent about 14 minutes. That creates an important qualification. The experiment clearly showed that the two primed groups reported far more words than the blind group, and that their proposed words tended to fit their respective stories. But the design does not cleanly separate **perceptual priming** — expectations altering the interpretation of ambiguous marks — from **motivational priming**, in which an interesting story makes people search harder and longer. The most defensible reading is that both processes may have contributed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Viewing Time Changed With the Story

Houran and Randle randomly assigned 176 participants to three conditions. Fifty-nine were told that the document might concern the Roswell crash, retrieval and cover-up of an extraterrestrial craft; 58 were told that it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing; and 59 received no proposed subject. Everyone worked with essentially the same visual material, including an enlarged print, several high-resolution scans and a magnifying glass. Crucially for the effort question, participants were allowed to continue for as long as they wished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The same broad ordering appeared in the number of proposed words, although not perfectly. Roswell participants reported an average of 4.6 words and atomic-test participants 4.8, compared with only 1.6 in the blind condition. The difference in [word counts]({{ 'word-counts/' | relative_url }}) across suggestion conditions was statistically significant, and both primed conditions produced significantly more words than the blind group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those figures make the effort issue difficult to dismiss. A participant who examines a degraded image for 20 minutes has more opportunities to inspect letter-like shapes, switch between scans, revisit doubtful passages and generate possible completions than somebody who stops after 14 minutes. More reported words therefore need not arise solely because a prime changed the visual interpretation of an otherwise identical amount of evidence.

There is an important statistical restraint, however. The published paper reports the average viewing times, but in its principal results it does **not report an inferential test establishing that the 20-, 16- and 14-minute differences themselves were statistically significant**. The strong statistical result reported there concerns the number of words deciphered. Consequently, the viewing-time pattern is evidence of a plausible motivational difference, not a separately demonstrated treatment effect of the same statistical status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The Motivation Confound

Houran and Randle themselves recognised this problem rather than treating viewing time as irrelevant noise. In their discussion they proposed motivation as one of the factors influencing how many words participants reported. They reasoned that a story involving a UFO crash and cover-up was intrinsically more engaging to many participants than an unexplained photograph of an old document, and that an atomic-bomb scenario could likewise be more interesting than the neutral description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That means the experiment potentially changed two variables together:

**[Expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) changed.** A Roswell participant had concepts such as crashes, debris and cover-ups readily available; an atomic-test participant had a different set of associations.

**Search persistence changed.** The cover story also gave participants a reason to keep looking. Because viewing duration was self-selected rather than fixed, greater curiosity could translate directly into additional examination.

This distinction matters because the dependent measure — how many characters or words participants wrote down — can plausibly respond to both mechanisms. Imagine two readers receiving exactly the same degraded line. One searches for five minutes and gives up; another spends considerably longer testing alternative letter combinations. Even without any difference in underlying eyesight, the second reader has more opportunities to construct something word-like from the noise.

The later Roswell literature has explicitly preserved this interpretation. In a 2022 review, Randle described ancillary analysis of the Houran–Randle work as indicating that more interesting contexts encouraged participants to spend more time trying to read the memo, while those contexts also produced more perceptions of specific words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362854511_A_Grounded_Theory_Update_on_the_Roswell_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO IncidentAugust 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2022</span></span></span>

So it would overstate the 2002 experiment to say that its higher word counts independently measure a change in perception. They measure the final output of a process in which **attention, persistence, expectation and perceptual judgement were allowed to interact**.

## Effort Alone Cannot Explain the Results

The motivation confound does not, however, make the priming result disappear. If the cover stories had merely persuaded people to work longer, the straightforward prediction would be **more guesses**, but not necessarily guesses systematically related to the supplied story.

That is not all that happened.

The Roswell condition generated readings including “remains”, “crash” and “UFO”. The atomic condition generated distinctive readings including “glasses”, “flash”, “atomic” and “laboratory”. The blind condition produced much less material and lacked the same strong thematic pattern. Some proposed words, including “Fort Worth”, appeared across conditions, which is one reason the authors did not conclude that every apparent feature of the memo was simply invented by suggestion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The comparison between the **two primed groups** is particularly useful here. Their average word totals were virtually identical — 4.6 for Roswell and 4.8 for atomic testing — despite their different average viewing times and, more importantly, their different thematic outputs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> If motivation merely increased the quantity of searching, it is harder to explain why changing the cover story also changed the *kind* of words reported.

This gives the experiment two related findings rather than one. Priming appears associated with **greater engagement relative to the blind condition**, while the content of the prime is also associated with **which interpretations emerge from the ambiguous image**. The first observation supports a motivation mechanism; the second is harder to reduce to motivation alone.

That distinction is consistent with wider research showing that ambiguous visual information can be susceptible to prior experience or priming. Experimental work with ambiguous figures has demonstrated that previous exposure can bias subsequent perceptual interpretations, while research on visual attention shows that motivational significance can alter where and for how long observers direct attention. These are separable mechanisms in principle, even though the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) experiment did not isolate them cleanly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2939375/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Uninformative visual experience establishes long term perceptual bias - PMCAugust 23, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 23, 2010</span></span></span>

## Why the Experiment Cannot Separate the Two Mechanisms

A cleaner experiment would have held search effort constant while manipulating only the story. For example, every participant could have received exactly ten minutes with identical displays and controls. If Roswell and atomic-test participants still produced different theme-congruent readings, that would make the perceptual or interpretative priming inference stronger because unequal persistence could no longer explain the difference.

The 2002 design did the opposite on this particular dimension: it deliberately imposed no time limit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That was reasonable if the aim was to let people attempt a natural decipherment, but it makes causal interpretation harder afterwards. Viewing time becomes a possible mediator: the prime may have increased interest, increased interest may have prolonged searching, and prolonged searching may have increased the number of apparent words.

Nor can the published dataset fully reconstruct every route by which that happened. The paper states that the research assistant discarded the original score sheets after compiling the computerised data and notes on words common to the conditions. When a referee requested counts showing exactly how many participants in each condition had deciphered each word, the researchers could therefore supply detailed counts only for the material preserved in their records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That loss does not erase the reported experimental results, but it limits the possibility of conducting finer-grained retrospective analyses of individual responses.

There is another reason for caution. The original discussion describes Roswell participants as spending more time and subsequently perceiving the greatest number of words, yet the numerical means printed in the results are actually **4.6 for Roswell and 4.8 for atomic testing**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> The substantive comparison with the blind group's 1.6 remains clear, but the wording is a reminder to distinguish the paper's interpretation from the numbers it actually reports.

## What Search Effort Means for Ramey Memo Claims

The effort confound cuts in two directions.

On one side, it weakens any simple claim that the experiment proved suggestion literally caused participants to *see* different letters. Because primed participants could choose to examine the material longer, part of their increased output may have resulted from additional searching rather than a direct alteration of perception. The experiment was not designed to estimate how much of the effect came from each mechanism.

On the other side, increased effort does **not rescue confident Roswell readings** of the memo. It creates another route through which prior expectations can influence the final transcription. A highly motivated investigator does not merely bring expectations to an ambiguous image; that investigator may also spend much longer exploring possible matches, repeatedly enhancing or enlarging passages and testing candidate phrases. With severely degraded material, persistence can increase opportunities both for genuine recognition and for false pattern completion.

That is why Houran and Randle ultimately argued for independent, blind analysis using standardised enhancement procedures and multiple specialist laboratories. Their proposed safeguard was not simply better image-processing technology. It was a procedure designed to prevent analysts' knowledge of the Roswell controversy from determining how the available visual information was searched and interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The Ramey experiment therefore supports a narrower conclusion than either side of the controversy sometimes wants. **Priming probably affected more than one stage of the decipherment process.** The 20-, 16- and 14-minute viewing averages make motivation a credible contributor, while the sharply different Roswell- and atomic-themed readings indicate that extra effort by itself is unlikely to account for the whole pattern. The experiment demonstrates that when an image is this ambiguous, the story supplied to the reader can influence both **how hard the reader looks and what the reader ultimately reports seeing**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
