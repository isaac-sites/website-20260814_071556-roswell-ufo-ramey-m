## Introduction

“Fort Worth” survived the early attempts to read the Ramey memo unusually well. That is significant not because the early [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) agreed overall—they emphatically did not—but because several readers retained essentially the same place name while producing sharply different readings of the words around it. J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})’s team included “AT FORT WORTH, TEX”; Neil Morris preserved “Fort Worth, Texas” even as most of his reconstruction diverged from Johnson’s; and John Kirby, who said he could make out relatively little, independently accepted “At Fort Worth, Tex.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That pattern makes the location fragment one of the more defensible pieces of the disputed text. It does not establish what the whole document says, still less settle the Roswell controversy. Rather, it shows why “Fort Worth” deserves to be treated differently from dramatic phrases that appeared in one reconstruction but disappeared or changed radically in another.

## The Johnson team's Fort Worth transcription

The important comparison begins with the work associated with J. Bond Johnson, the Fort Worth Star-Telegram photographer who took the famous 8 July 1947 photograph of Brigadier General Roger [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) holding the document. The surviving photograph belongs to the newspaper's photographic collection preserved by the University of Texas at Arlington; the university describes the wider Star-Telegram archive as containing roughly 179,500 prints and an estimated six million film and glass-plate negatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/specialcollections_startelegram/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mavmatrix.uta.edu">[mavmatrix.uta.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mavmatrix.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uta.edu.</span></span></span>

In 1998, Johnson assembled investigators to examine enlargements of the photograph using computers, photographic equipment and image-processing techniques. Their published partial reading contained a sequence beginning “AS THE … 4 HRS THE VICTIMS OF THE … AT FORT WORTH, TEX”, followed by other proposed fragments involving a “CRASH” [story]({{ 'story/' | relative_url }}), emergency powers, Magdalena, weather balloons and a possible signature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[noufors.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on noufors.com.</span></span></span>

For the present question, the striking point is not those more sensational proposed words. It is that “AT FORT WORTH, TEX” appeared as a comparatively straightforward geographical fragment embedded among large gaps and much more controversial material. The Johnson reconstruction was plainly incomplete: ellipses separated supposed islands of readable text, and later investigators did not reproduce much of what the team believed it had found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That subsequent disagreement provides the useful test. If later readers had rejected “Fort Worth” along with most of the Johnson transcription, there would be little reason to give the phrase special status. Instead, the location was one of the conspicuous survivors.

## Morris and Kirby kept the place name

Neil Morris is particularly useful for comparison because he did not merely endorse the Johnson team's prose. According to James Houran and Kevin Randle's 2002 review in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration*, Morris introduced a more explicit line-by-line notation: capitals represented characters of which he felt confident, lower-case letters indicated guesses, asterisks represented undeciphered characters and dashes marked areas offering little more than a smudge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The result did not reproduce Johnson's reading. Houran and Randle emphasised that Morris's version pointed in different directions and that, apart from “victims” and “Fort Worth, Texas”, “nearly everything else was different”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[noufors.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on noufors.com.</span></span></span> This is precisely what makes the comparison useful. Morris was not simply repeating the complete Johnson interpretation; substantial disagreement remained visible in his transcription.

John Kirby supplied an even sharper contrast. Houran and Randle reported that Kirby, despite using his computer expertise and equipment, could decipher very little of the document. Yet he agreed that the third line contained “At Fort Worth, Tex.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span>

Kirby's proposed preceding line illustrates how deep the disagreement could run. Where the Johnson material was associated with the highly contentious word “victims”, Kirby read a phrase about “the remains of the material”. Houran and Randle explicitly observed that replacing “victims” with “remains” altered the meaning of the document substantially.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[noufors.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on noufors.com.</span></span></span> The geographical reading nevertheless remained.

The comparison can therefore be reduced to a revealing pattern:

* **Johnson's team:** included “AT FORT WORTH, TEX” within a much longer, highly disputed reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[noufors.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on noufors.com.</span></span></span>
* **Neil Morris:** produced a transcription in which most of the surrounding material changed, but “Fort Worth, Texas” remained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002...</span></span></span>
* **John Kirby:** reported being unable to make out much of the document, disagreed over important nearby wording, yet still accepted “At Fort Worth, Tex.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[noufors.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on noufors.com.</span></span></span>

That is a stronger historical pattern than three nearly identical copies of the same transcription would have been. The disagreement demonstrates that the readers were capable of reaching different conclusions; “Fort Worth” is notable because it persisted through those differences.

## Why agreement amid disagreement carries weight

The value of this convergence is best understood as **local agreement**, not proof that one complete transcription is correct. The photograph is intrinsically difficult evidence. The memo occupies only a small portion of a 1947 press photograph, its paper is angled and partly obscured, and researchers have repeatedly disagreed about individual letters, words and entire sentences. Even sophisticated enlargement and image-processing efforts have failed to produce a universally accepted reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ramey-memo.com/national-level-organization-failed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramey-memo.com">[ramey-memo.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramey-memo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National-Level Organization FAILED! – Unlocking The Mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National-Level Organization FAILED! – Unlocking The Mystery</span></span></span>

Against that background, repeated recognition of the same character sequence at the same position is meaningful. Houran and Randle's later study was motivated partly by precisely this methodological problem: ambiguous visual material can encourage observers to perceive words that fit their expectations. Their historical review therefore distinguished between the sweeping claims made for complete transcriptions and the smaller areas where readers showed agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

“Fort Worth” is a useful example because the neighbouring disagreements act almost like a control. If Johnson, Morris and Kirby had all reproduced essentially the same sentence, shared expectations or influence between investigators would be difficult to separate from genuine legibility. Instead, Morris changed much of Johnson's proposed text, while Kirby was openly pessimistic about how much he could read. Their retention of the location therefore cannot simply be treated as agreement on a common full reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/Journal%20of%20Scientific%20Exploration/jse_16_1_houran.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[noufors.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on noufors.com.</span></span></span>

There is still an important qualification. These were not three formally blinded observers working in isolation under laboratory conditions. Morris was associated with Johnson's Roswell Photo Interpretation Team, and the developing interpretations circulated within the same research community. The historical convergence consequently should not be described as fully independent experimental replication. Houran and Randle themselves subsequently argued for controlled comparisons of readers and for measuring agreement on particular words at precise locations rather than relying upon confident-looking complete transcriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span>

Nor is agreement necessarily evidence that the document was a secret military message about extraordinary wreckage. Researcher Barry [Greenwood]({{ 'greenwood/' | relative_url }}) later approached the memo from a markedly different direction, arguing that it might resemble news-wire material. Yet even Greenwood considered “…AT FORT WORTH, TEX.” among the fragments that appeared sufficiently distinct to have attracted general agreement. Kevin Randle, discussing Greenwood's work in 2015, likewise said that this phrase appeared to stand out more clearly than much of the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/09/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: September 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 2009</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. Readers can agree on **what a few letters appear to say** while disagreeing profoundly about **what kind of document they are reading**.

## Fort Worth is stronger than the sentences built around it

The early transcription history therefore supports a deliberately narrow conclusion. “Fort Worth” is not compelling because it forms part of one persuasive reconstruction. It is compelling because competing reconstructions repeatedly failed to dislodge it.

The difference can be seen in the fate of neighbouring language. “Victims” could become “remains”; proposed references to emergency powers, a crash story or other operations were not reproduced consistently; and later readers reconstructed lines in substantially different ways. Houran and Randle characterised the broader decipherment problem in exactly these terms: investigators could find apparently meaningful text without agreeing on what most of that text actually was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

By contrast, the Fort Worth phrase continued to appear not only in the Johnson, Morris and Kirby readings but in later discussions of areas where a measure of agreement existed. Greenwood's 2009 assessment, despite advancing an interpretation of the document at odds with UFO-crash readings, still identified “AT FORT WORTH, TEX” as one of the comparatively recognisable fragments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2009/09/?m=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: September 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 2009</span></span></span>

There is also a mundane contextual reason why this particular reading is plausible without assuming anything about the memo's disputed message. The photograph itself was made in Fort Worth on 8 July 1947, and the archival image is identified as showing Ramey at Fort Worth Army Air Field inspecting debris brought from Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABrig_General_Ramey_Roswell_debris.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Brig General Ramey Roswell debris.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Commons File:Brig General Ramey Roswell debris.jpg</span></span></span> “Fort Worth” is therefore a place name that makes historical sense in the document's immediate setting.

That contextual fit is useful but also demands caution. Knowing that the photograph was taken in Fort Worth could itself make observers more inclined to perceive the place name in ambiguous marks. Indeed, Greenwood noted that wording such as “AT FORT WORTH, TEX” also appeared in contemporary newspaper reporting, a point relevant to competing theories that the sheet might contain news-wire material rather than a [military communication]({{ 'military-communication/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Scientific Exploration Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific ExplorationAnomalisticsAugust 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2022</span></span></span> Context therefore strengthens the phrase's plausibility but cannot, by itself, prove the reading.

The more persuasive feature is the combination: the proposed letters resemble a contextually sensible place name, the phrase occupies a consistent position in competing transcriptions, and it survived even when readers disagreed sharply about adjoining words.

## What the early readings actually establish

The safest assessment is that the early transcription history raises “Fort Worth, Tex.” above most disputed [Ramey-memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) wording without turning it into a key for deciphering the whole document. Johnson's team supplied the initial prominent reading; Morris substantially revised the surrounding text but retained the location; Kirby could decipher comparatively little yet reached essentially the same reading; and Greenwood later acknowledged the same fragment despite pursuing a very different interpretation of the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That is exactly why the fragment matters as a **location clue**. The early readers did not establish a stable sentence about what happened at Fort Worth. They established something narrower and more defensible: among the shifting shapes and competing interpretations of the photographed sheet, the character group read as “Fort Worth” proved unusually resistant to disagreement.

The surviving agreement should therefore be used asymmetrically. It gives reasonable grounds for treating “Fort Worth” as one of the memo's stronger candidate readings, but it gives no licence to attach equal confidence to “victims”, “remains”, “crash”, “disc”, emergency instructions or any complete narrative reconstructed around it. The most informative feature of the early decipherment effort is precisely that those larger narratives changed while the place name endured.
