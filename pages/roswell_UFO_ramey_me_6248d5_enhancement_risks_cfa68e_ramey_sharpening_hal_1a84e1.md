## Introduction

Sharpening halos are a particularly important risk when disputed [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) characters are examined at extreme enlargement. A halo is not new documentary information. It is a light or dark fringe created beside an existing brightness transition when sharpening increases local edge contrast. On a normal photograph, such a fringe may merely make an object look crisper. On the Ramey memo, however, the photographed writing occupies so little of the original [negative]({{ 'negative/' | relative_url }}) that a fringe only a few samples wide can become a substantial part of what an observer perceives as a letter.

## How sharpening halos form beside weak edges

Unsharp masking sounds as though it should blur an image, but it is a sharpening technique. Conceptually, a blurred copy of an image is compared with the original; the difference emphasises higher-frequency information, especially brightness changes at edges, and a scaled version of that difference is added back. The resulting transition can look steeper and therefore sharper. Adobe's explanation of unsharp masking likewise notes that excessive sharpening produces visible halo effects around object edges.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.adobe.com/products/photoshop/unsharp-masking.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adobe.com">[Adobe]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adobe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unsharp masking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sounds as though it should blur an image, but it is a sharpening technique. The important point for the Ramey memo is what happens immediately beside the original edge. Sharpening does not simply identify an ink boundary and trace it more accurately. It changes pixel values around brightness transitions. Dark material beside lighter material can acquire an exaggerated dark side and an exaggerated light side. In signal-processing terms these excursions can be understood as overshoot and undershoot; visually, they become the familiar dark and bright fringes associated with over-sharpening.

Research by Matthew Trentacoste, Rafal Mantiuk, Wolfgang Heidrich and Florian Dufrot examined precisely this family of countershading effects. They found that such edge profiles can make an image appear sharper or higher in contrast than its physical values alone would suggest, but that inappropriate parameters or viewing conditions make the same profiles visible as objectionable halos. Crucially, their results linked the transition between useful [enhancement]({{ 'enhancement/' | relative_url }}) and visible artefact to the **width of the countershading profile**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2012.03056.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research by Matthew Trentacoste, Rafal Mantiuk, Wolfgang Heidrich and Florian Dufrot examined precisely this family of countershading eff...</span></span></span>

That width is unusually consequential here. Suppose a well-resolved printed character spans dozens of pixels. A one-pixel sharpening fringe represents only a small fraction of its structure. If an uncertain component of a Ramey memo character is represented by only a handful of meaningful samples, an artificial fringe of comparable scale can occupy a much larger fraction of the apparent stroke. Enlarging the processed image then enlarges the genuine signal and the sharpening artefact together.

The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative (FADGI), whose current technical guidance covers digitisation of photographs, negatives and textual cultural-heritage material, draws an important distinction between sharpness and resolution. It describes sharpening as artificially increasing detail contrast to create an impression of greater definition and notes that an image can be sharpened yet still possess lower actual resolution than an unsharpened comparison. Once genuine high-frequency information has fallen below a sufficiently low response, FADGI says sharpening cannot reliably recover the missing higher-resolution information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/FADGITechnicalGuidelinesforDigitizingCulturalHeritageMaterials_ThirdEdition_05092023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative (FADGI), whose current technical guidance covers digitisation of photographs, negative...</span></span></span>

For the memo, therefore, a harder-looking edge is not necessarily a better-measured edge.

## The memo's scale makes small fringes unusually consequential

The strongest Ramey-specific evidence comes from attempts to image the original photographic negatives rather than endlessly enlarge old reproductions. Work beginning at the University of Texas at Arlington in 2015 examined the negative using microscope, microfiche, digital-camera and film-based techniques. The later published account reports that the negative was adequately exposed, that camera or subject shake was not evident, and that the memo fell within the camera's focus zone. Yet it identified **scale** as the dominant problem: whether letters could be distinguished depended on their dimensions relative to the distribution of silver-halide grains making up the photographic image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This is not a hypothetical concern about digital photography in general. The numbers illustrate the difficulty. In the cited working representation, the complete 4×5 frame measured approximately 3,663 by 2,743 pixels, whereas the message was reported as only about 148.5 pixels wide. Later microscopic work produced files at more than 65,000 pixels per inch and resolved individual silver-halide grains, reported as roughly 3–10 micrometres across. That enormous scanning density made the physical grain structure clearer, but it did not create a correspondingly enormous increase in recoverable letter information. The investigators ultimately reported that further physical scanning was not increasing visual readability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This is not a hypothetical concern about digital photography in general. This distinction is central to halo analysis. A scan may contain thousands of digital samples across an enlarged patch of negative while the *photographic information defining a letter* remains limited by the much coarser grain-and-optics record created in 1947. A sharpening halo can therefore be sampled very smoothly and displayed very convincingly even though the underlying character boundary remains uncertain.

FADGI's guidance makes the same broader distinction in technical terms. Its Spatial Frequency Response measurements assess how faithfully an imaging system retains contrast as detail becomes finer. The guidelines note that unusually high modulation can itself indicate digital enhancement filtering, and explicitly contrast a sharpened low-resolution image with an unsharpened higher-resolution image: sharpening can boost contrast at frequencies the system retains, but it cannot reliably reconstruct fine information that was never adequately captured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/FADGITechnicalGuidelinesforDigitizingCulturalHeritageMaterials_ThirdEdition_05092023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitizationguidelines.gov">[Digitization Guidelines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitizationguidelines.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative (FADGI), whose current technical guidance covers digitisation of photographs, negative...</span></span></span>

For a disputed memo letter, that means the relevant question is not “How many pixels wide is this stroke in today's TIFF?” It is “How much independent source information defines this stroke before edge enhancement?”

## A halo can alter the anatomy used to name a letter

The risk becomes easier to understand by treating character recognition as a geometry problem. Identifying an ambiguous mark as a particular capital letter depends on features such as stems, bowls, crossbars, diagonals, openings, junctions and terminals. When those components are already near the resolution limit, sharpening does not need to fabricate an entire letter to change an interpretation. It may only need to modify one decisive local relationship.

A dark fringe can make a weak vertical feature look wider or more continuous. A light fringe can enlarge the apparent separation between neighbouring dark marks. Halos around adjacent boundaries can alter the apparent width of an internal opening. At the end of a faint stroke, edge enhancement can make a terminal look longer or more sharply defined. Two uncertain components separated by a tiny gap may consequently look either connected or distinct depending on the filter radius, strength, threshold and preceding processing.

That matters because Ramey memo interpretations demonstrably turn on character-level distinctions. Houran and Randle's 2002 paper tabulated markedly different readings from earlier investigators. ## How to recognise a halo that may be reshaping a character

A single sharpened enlargement cannot reliably reveal whether a disputed edge is intrinsic or processing-induced. The stronger test is comparative: the supposed letter feature should remain spatially stable when processing parameters change and should be traceable backwards into minimally processed source imagery.

One warning sign is a paired bright-and-dark band closely following a boundary. Such countershading is characteristic of sharpening profiles, and the Trentacoste study shows that the perceptual effect depends importantly on profile width and viewing conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2012.03056.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research by Matthew Trentacoste, Rafal Mantiuk, Wolfgang Heidrich and Florian Dufrot examined precisely this family of countershading eff...</span></span></span> On a tiny memo character, a bright rim outside a dark stroke should therefore not automatically be interpreted as clean paper separating two ink structures.

A second warning sign is **parameter-dependent anatomy**. If modest sharpening merely makes a pre-existing vertical stroke easier to locate, the result is less concerning. If increasing the radius or amount causes an extra crossbar to appear, closes a previously open gap, joins two components, produces a new terminal or changes which letter best fits the mark, then the recognition depends partly on the processing.

A third check is to inspect unquestionably non-text edges at comparable scale. If paper texture, grain clumps, scratches or unrelated small boundaries acquire similar bright/dark fringes, that provides a local demonstration of how the chosen enhancement treats weak transitions. It does not prove that a particular letter is false, but it makes clear that crispness itself cannot distinguish ink from non-ink structure.

A fourth test is [reproducibility]({{ 'reproducibility/' | relative_url }}) across source states. The 2022 Ramey review describes raw and enhanced recordings, bracketed exposures, microscope imagery and different acquisition systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/download/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This is not a hypothetical concern about digital photography in general. ## What would count as robust character evidence?

A sharpened Ramey character becomes more credible when its interpretation does **not** depend on the halo. ## The key limit: a halo can clarify an edge without validating it

Sharpening remains useful for examining the Ramey memo. A restrained sharpened version may help locate faint transitions, compare repeated character forms or direct attention to structures worth checking in less processed captures. The same-width fringe beside a barely resolved character component can alter a substantial fraction of the geometry used to call that mark a `V`, `P`, `R`, `S`, `5` or another candidate.

The appropriate evidential rule is therefore simple but strict: **a disputed letter feature should be considered stronger only when the geometry survives the sharpening process, not because the sharpening process makes that geometry appear.** A darkened stem, closed gap or extended terminal that becomes decisive only after edge enhancement remains an enhancement-dependent interpretation. For the Ramey memo, where film grain and character information have been reported at overlapping scales and decades of investigators still disagree about critical words, that distinction is fundamental to separating a clearer-looking image from genuinely stronger evidence.
