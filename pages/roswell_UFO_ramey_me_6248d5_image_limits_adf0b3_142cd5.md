## Introduction

The Ramey memo is difficult to read for a simple physical reason: the photograph contains very little reliable image information about most individual letters. The document was not photographed as a document. It happened to be in Brigadier General Roger Ramey’s hand during a wider press photograph taken on 8 July 1947, so its typewritten characters occupy only a tiny fraction of the original large-format negative. The sheet is also folded, curved and angled rather than lying flat in the camera’s focal plane. Those conditions push many letter strokes towards the same scale as [film grain]({{ 'film-grain/' | relative_url }}), blur and other photographic irregularities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span>

## Character size and film grain

The first difficulty is scale. [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) and the material on the floor were the subjects of J. [Bond Johnson]({{ 'johnson/' | relative_url }})’s photograph; the paper in Ramey’s hand was incidental. Consequently, the complete memo occupies a relatively small patch of a 4-by-5-inch press negative, while individual typewriter characters occupy only a minute part of that patch. Researchers can scan that region into thousands of digital pixels, but the number of pixels in the modern file should not be confused with the amount of detail originally resolved photographically.

Film can possess high resolving power, but graininess becomes increasingly visible as an image is enlarged; Kodak’s own technical material treats grain and resolving power as separate limits on enlargement and image structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kodakprofessional.com/en-gb/photographers/film/black-white" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kodakprofessional.com">[Kodak Professional]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kodakprofessional.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kodak Professional Black &amp; White &#124; Kodak Alaris</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kodak Professional Black &amp; White &#124; Kodak Alaris</span></span></span>

In the Ramey photograph, this creates a signal-to-noise problem. The useful signal consists of tiny differences in density produced by typewriter strokes on the paper. The competing noise includes individual or clustered film grains, development irregularities, dust and subsequent reproduction artefacts. Once both exist at comparable scales, a dark patch may represent part of a letter, a grain cluster or some combination of the two.

This is not merely a theoretical objection raised after the controversy began. Investigators Bill McNeff and Glenn Fishbine, quoted in a detailed critical examination of the memo, reported that some proposed letter readings appeared to use film-grain patterns rather than the underlying characters. They also found apparently text-like patterns in areas of grain where no genuine text should have been present. That is precisely the failure mode expected when an image is operating near its information limit: random structure begins to look linguistically meaningful after enlargement and filtering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

A sharp face or recognisable piece of debris elsewhere in the frame does not prove that every microscopic feature on the memo was equally well recorded.

## Folds, curvature and focus

The sheet itself makes the problem worse. It is neither flat nor square to the camera. In the photograph the memo bends around Ramey’s grip, is visibly folded and presents different regions of the typed surface at different orientations. Houran and Randle noted that the paper was turned away from the camera, even while judging the angle small enough to make attempted reading worthwhile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdo cz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Abcdo cz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdo cz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span></span></span>

A flat document photographed for reproduction can be positioned perpendicular to the optical axis, evenly lit and held within a single focal plane. The Ramey memo had none of those advantages. Its geometry introduces several distinct losses at once.

**Perspective compression** makes characters on angled portions of the sheet appear narrower in one direction. Closely spaced strokes therefore move closer together on the film, making letters such as `M`, `N`, `H`, `E` or `R` harder to distinguish when only a few fine density changes separate one possible reading from another.

**Paper curvature** changes continuously across the sheet. A correction that stretches one region into a plausible rectangular shape cannot prove what the original strokes were; it merely transforms the already-recorded densities. Geometric rectification can make lines easier to compare, but it cannot reverse blur or recover missing edges.

**Folds interfere directly with type.** A crease changes the orientation of the paper over a very short distance and can break, darken or distort portions of characters. The fold can also create highlights and shadows whose density transitions are much stronger than the tiny transitions generated by the type itself.

**Depth differences affect focus.** With the sheet bent towards and away from the camera, not every section is necessarily at exactly the distance for which the lens was optimally focused. Depth of field can keep a region acceptably sharp for ordinary viewing while still failing to preserve enough micro-detail to separate one typewriter character from another. At this scale, “looks in focus” and “contains sufficient character-level resolution” are not the same standard.

That final distinction explains an apparent contradiction in discussions of the memo. Some analysts describe the fundamental obstacle chiefly as film grain rather than gross defocus, while others point to focus and lens resolution as additional constraints. These positions are compatible. A photograph need not be obviously blurred for tiny letters to suffer a modest loss of edge definition. When the characters already occupy very little negative area, even a small amount of optical blur can push their remaining structure into the grain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/SUNlite7_6.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOShedding some light on UFOlogy and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 1, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2015</span></span></span>

Lighting adds another layer. Because the paper is bent and held in a room rather than illuminated for document copying, its surface brightness varies. Digital contrast adjustment can make dark strokes stand out more strongly, but it can also make grain, dust and crease shadows more conspicuous. Aggressive sharpening or blurring can then create edge patterns that look more letter-like without supplying independent evidence that those shapes belonged to the original type.

## Why better scans have not settled the text

There is an important difference between a poor copy of a photograph and an information-poor portion of an otherwise good negative. Better digitisation unquestionably helps with the first problem. Scanning the original negative is preferable to scanning a newspaper reproduction, photocopy, print or compressed JPEG because every intermediate generation can discard fine contrast and introduce new artefacts.

That is why access to the Fort Worth Star-Telegram negatives at the University of Texas at Arlington has mattered so much. UTA identifies Johnson’s original 8 July 1947 negatives as part of its Special Collections and has supported attempts to document and examine the memo. Yet the institution continues to state that the words have not been definitively deciphered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/spcouta/36080122212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell UFO Incident, July 1947 &#124;...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1947</span></span></span>

Modern archival practice also illustrates why increasing scan resolution has diminishing returns. The US Geological Survey notes that scanning resolution must be chosen according to the quality and intended use of the source and recommends high optical resolutions for photographic transparencies because enlargement rapidly exposes their limitations. But once a scan is sampling the source finely enough to record its actual grain and image structure, adding still more digital samples does not automatically reveal new scene detail. It increasingly records the same analogue structure at greater numerical resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/national-geological-and-geophysical-data-preservation-program/scanning-specifications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[USGS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scanning Specifications &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scanning Specifications &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span></span></span>

On 20 July 1994, that organisation reported that, even after digitisation, the images were of insufficient quality to reveal the details being sought. Whatever one thinks of the Air Force’s wider conclusions about Roswell, that specific episode demonstrates that the memo’s image-quality problem was recognised during a technical examination rather than arising only from later online copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF History The Roswell Report</span></span></span>

## Enlargement is not recovery

Popular culture encourages the idea that an analyst can repeatedly zoom into a photograph until an indistinct object becomes legible. Real imaging does not work that way. Enlargement changes the display scale of recorded information. It does not increase the spatial detail captured by the original exposure.

A simple example is a typewriter stroke that reached the film only as an unresolved grey-dark patch. Enlarging that patch can make its boundary easier to inspect, but it cannot tell the analyst whether the original paper contained one thin vertical stroke, two adjacent strokes blurred together, or a partly recorded curve. Several different letters may therefore remain compatible with essentially the same photographic pattern.

Image processing can still be useful. Contrast adjustment can separate faint typing from the paper. Rotation and perspective correction can make lines horizontal. Stacking multiple scans of the same negative can reduce scanner noise. Deconvolution may partially compensate for a known blur. Grain reduction can make large-scale structures easier to see. These are legitimate analytical techniques when carefully documented.

Their limitation is that they operate on evidence already present in the image. As the source becomes more ambiguous, processing also becomes increasingly assumption-dependent. A smoothing filter may remove genuine portions of a letter because they resemble grain. Sharpening may turn random density changes into apparent edges. Thresholding can make a broken stroke appear continuous or erase it altogether. JPEG compression is particularly undesirable for this sort of work because its block and ringing artefacts can modify features at the very scale researchers are trying to interpret. Critical examinations of the Ramey memo have specifically warned that filtering and compressed derivatives can change apparent character shapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomyufo.com/UFO/Ramey.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomyufo.com">[Astronomy UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomyufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy UFOThe Ramey Document: Smoking gun or empty water pistol?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2003</span></span></span>

Newer computational restoration does not abolish that distinction. Modern algorithms can produce visually persuasive reconstructions from damaged or undersampled material, but historical-image restoration research continues to treat [ambiguity]({{ 'ambiguity/' | relative_url }}) as a central problem: an algorithm can generate a plausible missing feature without demonstrating that the generated feature is what the original scene contained. Work on analogue-film restoration, for example, explicitly distinguishes damaged scans from known clean reference images when evaluating whether restoration methods have actually reconstructed the correct content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

For a historically contentious document, that evidential distinction is crucial. A reconstruction may be useful for proposing candidate readings; it is not automatically a transcription.

## What the physical limits mean for claimed readings


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The physical limitations do not require the conclusion that every character in the Ramey memo is unreadable. Some areas contain stronger signals than others. Short fragments may benefit from favourable paper angle, contrast, letter spacing or contextual structure. This explains why several researchers have reported partial overlap in their readings while disagreeing sharply about other words.

</div>

What the limits do undermine is the idea that a striking phrase can be validated merely because an enlargement appears to resemble it. When letter strokes are interrupted by grain and folds, several competing words may fit approximately the same set of marks. The 2002 Houran-Randle experiment demonstrated the practical consequence: people examining high-quality versions did not converge on a single complete transcription, and the authors found that prior knowledge and expectations affected the number and character of words participants believed they could decipher.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdo cz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Abcdo cz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdo cz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span></span></span>

That perceptual issue begins with the physical one. If the negative contained crisp, high-contrast letters, expectations would have little room to operate. A clearly photographed `B` does not easily become an `R` because the viewer knows the Roswell story. Expectations become influential precisely where the source leaves several character identities physically possible.

This is why disputed phrases associated with the memo should be treated differently from an ordinary archival quotation. They are reconstructions made from marginal photographic evidence, not words read cleanly from the original sheet. The fact that researchers can sometimes agree on fragments does not establish that equally confident readings of neighbouring blurred regions are reliable.

## The practical ceiling on the Ramey memo

The most useful way to understand the Ramey memo is therefore to separate three levels of information. The first is the original typewritten text, which presumably had perfectly definite wording in 1947. The second is the optical information about that text actually captured on Johnson’s film. The third consists of later prints, scans, enlargements and digital reconstructions derived from that film.

Only the first contained the complete message. Every later stage is constrained by the second.

Better scanning can prevent additional loss between the negative and the researcher. It can reveal faint density variations that older prints concealed. Multiple processing methods can test whether a proposed feature persists rather than disappearing when contrast settings change. These improvements are worthwhile and explain why modern examination can outperform a casual inspection of a 1947 newspaper print.

But they cannot bypass the decisive bottleneck: whether each distinguishing letter stroke was recorded clearly enough on the negative in the first place. In the Ramey photograph, tiny character size, film grain, paper folds, curvature, perspective, lighting and finite optical focus all consume part of that already-small information margin. UTA’s continuing description of the memo as not definitively deciphered, despite preservation of the original negatives and years of specialised attention, is consistent with an image that has reached this kind of physical ceiling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uta.edu/utamagazine/spring-2017/stories/collected.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uta.edu">[UTA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ramey Memo &#124; UTA Magazine...</span></span></span>

The result is less mysterious than the controversy surrounding it. Enlargements can make the Ramey memo easier to examine, but they cannot guarantee that its lost letter shapes become recoverable. Where the negative preserves enough structure, cautious partial readings may be possible. Where grain and blur have replaced the distinctions between letters, enlargement merely gives the ambiguity a larger canvas.
