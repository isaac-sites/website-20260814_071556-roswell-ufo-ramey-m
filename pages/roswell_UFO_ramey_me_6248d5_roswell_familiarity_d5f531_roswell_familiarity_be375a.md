## Introduction

The “UFO–Roswell scale” used in James Houran and Kevin Randle’s 2002 [Ramey-memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) experiment sounds, at first glance, like a measure of how familiar somebody was with Roswell. It was not that simple. The eight-item questionnaire combined prior exposure to Roswell and the memo with belief in UFOs and extraterrestrial life, interest in UFO media, a claimed personal UFO experience, and acceptance of an extraterrestrial explanation for Roswell. The authors themselves described it as a study-specific index of prior “exposure/knowledge”, constructed on the basis of face validity rather than as a previously validated expertise test.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_roswell_familiarity_be375a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Familiarity Scale illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_roswell_familiarity_be375a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/roswell_UFO_ramey_me_6248d5_roswell_familiarity_d5f531_roswell_familiarity_be375a-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That matters because the study found a positive correlation between this combined score and the number of words participants reported deciphering. The result therefore cannot cleanly establish that **greater factual knowledge or technical expertise made people better at reading the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo**. It shows that a broader package of [familiarity]({{ 'familiarity/' | relative_url }}), engagement, belief and claimed experience was associated with reporting more readable words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## The eight ingredients of the UFO–Roswell scale

The questionnaire appeared in an appendix to Houran and Randle’s paper under the heading “Questionnaire to Index Knowledge About the Roswell Case”. Participants rated eight statements from strong disagreement to strong agreement. They can usefully be separated into four groups:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **General UFO or extraterrestrial belief:** belief that intelligent life exists elsewhere in the universe, and belief in UFOs.
* **Claimed personal experience and interest:** reporting having personally experienced a UFO, and enjoying television documentaries or radio programmes about UFOs and extraterrestrial life.
* **Roswell exposure and belief:** having encountered Roswell through books, films or television, and believing that the 1947 incident involved an extraterrestrial craft.
* **Ramey-memo familiarity:** having heard of the memo and having read published accounts of proposed interpretations of its contents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span>

The important point is not merely that the questionnaire was broad. Its components represented **different constructs**. Reading books about Roswell is an exposure measure. Believing Roswell involved an extraterrestrial spacecraft is a conclusion about the case. Enjoying UFO programmes is an interest measure. Claiming a UFO experience is autobiographical self-report. Having previously read proposed Ramey-memo [transcriptions]({{ 'transcriptions/' | relative_url }}) is unusually direct prior exposure to the very interpretive problem participants were subsequently asked to solve.

Yet these responses were combined into one study-specific index. The paper describes the lead author as constructing its items “based on face validity”: in other words, they were selected because they appeared relevant to prior Roswell/UFO exposure and knowledge. The study did not report the scale as a separately validated measure of archival-document expertise, photographic interpretation skill or objective Roswell factual knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This explains why the paper itself sometimes uses language broader than “knowledge”. When reporting the results, Houran and Randle describe the relevant participant characteristic parenthetically as involving “belief, alleged experience, and exposure to UFO information”. That description is more precise than treating the resulting number simply as an expertise or familiarity score.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why familiarity and belief cannot be cleanly separated

The scale design creates a problem when interpreting its most interesting statistical result. Houran and Randle reported a Spearman correlation of **0.35** between the UFO–Roswell score and the number of words deciphered, statistically significant at their stated *p*<.05 threshold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That correlation establishes an association with the **combined index**. It does not identify which ingredient produced it.

Suppose, for example, that people who had already read proposed Ramey-memo transcriptions reported more words because they recognised shapes they had previously encountered. That would be a familiarity effect. But suppose instead that strong commitment to an extraterrestrial interpretation encouraged participants to search longer or accept more uncertain letter combinations as meaningful. That would be closer to a belief, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) or motivation effect. A third possibility is that both processes operated together.

The published aggregate score cannot separate those explanations.

This distinction becomes particularly important because the experiment independently demonstrated that supplied context could alter what participants thought they saw. The 176 participants were randomly assigned to a Roswell suggestion condition, an atomic-bomb-testing condition or a blind condition. The Roswell and atomic groups reported roughly similar numbers of deciphered words — means of 4.6 and 4.8 respectively — while the blind group reported only 1.6. The words themselves also shifted with context: participants given the atomic-testing story produced interpretations such as “flash”, “glasses”, “atomic” and “laboratory”, whereas the Roswell condition generated Roswell-congruent readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That finding makes the mixed scale especially difficult to treat as a simple measure of useful prior knowledge. The experiment was explicitly investigating suggestion and expectation in an ambiguous visual stimulus. But several items in its supposed prior-knowledge index themselves captured beliefs, interests and experiences capable of carrying expectations into the task. The predictor therefore bundled together both **potentially useful prior information** and **potential sources of interpretive bias**.

The authors did take one sensible procedural precaution: participants completed the questionnaire **after** attempting the decipherment, reducing the danger that the questionnaire itself would prime them immediately before viewing the memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That strengthens the interpretation that the scale was recording pre-existing differences among participants rather than creating those differences during testing. It does not, however, solve the measurement problem: once those different characteristics are collapsed into one score, their individual contributions remain unidentified.

## Familiarity may also mean prior exposure to the answer

Items seven and eight deserve particular attention because they are much closer to the decipherment task than the general UFO questions.

One asked whether participants had heard of the Ramey memo. The other asked whether they had read popular accounts of its possible contents or interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span> Someone scoring highly on the latter item might therefore enter the experiment already knowing candidate words that earlier investigators believed were present.

That is fundamentally different from possessing a transferable skill such as experience reading degraded archival documents.

The paper illustrates why this matters by presenting earlier proposed transcriptions side by side. Previous Ramey-memo investigators had already circulated readings containing such expressions as “VICTIMS OF THE WRECK”, “FORT WORTH”, “DISC” or “DISK”, “STORY”, “WEATHER BALLOONS” and “LAND”, although their fuller transcriptions differed substantially. The paper also notes that some of those investigators had shared information while attempting their readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Prior exposure can consequently work in two directions. It might help someone identify a genuinely visible word. But it can also provide a ready-made vocabulary against which indistinct shapes are matched. When the image is highly degraded, recognition and expectation become unusually difficult to disentangle.

That is exactly the situation the experiment was designed to investigate. Participants were examining enlargements and high-resolution scans of a document the authors themselves described as very difficult to read. Despite limited consensus on several locations, suggestion strongly affected the larger pattern of interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## What the scale cannot tell us about expertise

Calling the UFO–Roswell score an “expertise” measure would therefore go beyond what was actually tested. Nothing in its eight items asked whether somebody could accurately read degraded documents, understood 1940s military communications conventions, had professional image-analysis training, or could demonstrate factual [Roswell knowledge]({{ 'knowledge-effect/' | relative_url }}) on questions with objectively correct answers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcdocz.com/doc/32352/%E2%80%9Ca-message-in-a-bottle-%E2%80%9D-confounds-in-deciphering-the-j-h" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcdocz.com">[Abcdocz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcdocz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abcdocz“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the J H...</span></span></span>

Those distinctions are not theoretical niceties in the Ramey-memo dispute. Houran and Randle themselves discussed technical constraints that could affect a legitimate transcription — for example, whether the document was produced by a teletype, the expected use of capital letters, military jargon and period communications conventions. They also noted large differences among previous investigators' proposed readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A stronger expertise test would therefore have needed to separate at least three questions: **How much does this person already know about Roswell? How strongly does this person believe particular Roswell claims? How skilled is this person at extracting text from degraded historical images?** The 2002 scale largely addressed the first two and did not independently measure the third.

That limitation also prevents a stronger causal reading of the reported 0.35 correlation. It would be incorrect to translate the result into “Roswell experts deciphered more of the memo”. A defensible summary is narrower: **participants reporting greater UFO/Roswell belief, experience and exposure on the combined scale also reported deciphering more words**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e162b50a3885a28c826cb31c3deca69abaaba9ee&amp;repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: citeseerx.ist.psu.edu">[CiteSeerX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">citeseerx.ist.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration, Vol. 16, No. 1, pp. 45–66, 2002...</span></span></span>

Nor does “more words” automatically mean “more correct words”. The dependent measure concerned the amount participants believed they could decipher, while the true complete text of the memo was not independently available as an answer key. Some cross-group agreement did emerge — notably around “Fort Worth TX”, “story” and “weather balloons” — which led Houran and Randle to argue that parts of the document may genuinely be legible. But the researchers could not simply score every proposed word against a known transcript.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There is an additional data limitation. When a referee requested participant-by-participant counts for individual deciphered words, the researchers discovered that the original score sheets had been discarded after the responses were entered into the computer database. They retained detailed information about words common across the three conditions, but not the original sheets needed for a fuller reconstruction of who perceived which individual words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> That further restricts retrospective attempts to separate the scale's ingredients using the published experiment.

## Why the mixed score still mattered

The measurement weakness does not make the experiment meaningless. Its value lies in answering a narrower question than is sometimes attributed to it.

Houran and Randle were testing whether interpretations of an exceptionally ambiguous image could be affected by contextual expectations and participants' pre-existing relationship with the UFO/Roswell subject. For that purpose, a broad index encompassing engagement, beliefs and previous exposure had some practical logic: these were all ways in which a participant might arrive at the image carrying a pre-existing cognitive framework. The experiment then found that both experimentally supplied context and the combined UFO–Roswell score were associated with how much text participants reported seeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

What the design sacrificed was diagnostic precision. Because belief and familiarity travelled together in the scale, the result cannot tell us whether prior Roswell knowledge improved perception, whether prior beliefs encouraged more interpretation, whether previous exposure to proposed transcripts supplied candidate words, or whether some combination of these processes explains the correlation.

Later discussion of the experiment has continued to emphasise the role of priming while acknowledging criticism of the original study. In a 2022 review, Randle noted objections to its design and statistical reporting but also reported that subsequent analyses supported the basic context effect: more engaging contexts were associated with longer examination, more perceived words, and more condition-specific interpretations than words shared across conditions. He also reiterated the original recommendation that serious future work should use independent, blind laboratories and compare agreement at precise locations in the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/2635/1631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Scientific Exploration</span></span></span>

That proposed methodology highlights the central lesson of the mixed UFO–Roswell score. Familiarity with Roswell is not inherently a disadvantage, any more than unfamiliarity guarantees objectivity. Relevant historical knowledge can help an analyst recognise genuine language and context. The problem arises when **knowledge of candidate readings, commitment to a particular explanation and technical ability to recover text are treated as though they were the same variable**.

For assessing [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) in Ramey-memo decipherment, the 2002 experiment therefore supports a limited but important conclusion: familiarity was associated with reporting more readable text, but because the study deliberately bundled familiarity with belief, interest and claimed experience, it cannot establish that greater Roswell expertise produced more accurate decipherment. The distinction between **seeing more words** and **reliably recovering more of the original document** remains essential.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>
