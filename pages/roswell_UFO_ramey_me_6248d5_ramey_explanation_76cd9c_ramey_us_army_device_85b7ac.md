## Introduction

Brigadier General Roger M. [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }})’s public position on the Roswell material was narrower than the claim often attributed to him. On 8 July 1947, after debris recovered near Roswell, New Mexico, reached his [Eighth Air Force]({{ 'command-control/' | relative_url }}) headquarters at Fort Worth, Ramey said the object was “definitely” a United States Army device. Contemporary Associated Press reports identified it more specifically as a balloon with a radar-reflecting target used for weather work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/galvestondailynews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and another &#x27;Roswell&#x27;, Galveston Daily News, July 9July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

The important qualification is that Ramey did **not** claim that such Army devices explained every “flying disc” being reported during the extraordinary saucer wave of 1947. In fact, contemporary reports record him explicitly declining to make that broader claim. His certainty concerned the **Roswell object in Army custody**; his comments about the wider flying-disc phenomenon were noticeably more limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/abilenereporternews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: the newspapers, Roswell explained, etc., Abilene Reporter-News, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

That distinction matters when interpreting both Ramey’s press handling of Roswell and the disputed “[Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }})” visible in photographs taken that day. His public message was not simply “flying saucers are balloons”. It was, in effect: **this particular object is ours**.

## Ramey’s statement that the object was American

The clearest evidence comes from the Associated Press account circulated on 8–9 July 1947. After describing the balloon and its [radar target]({{ 'radar-target/' | relative_url }}), the report stated that Ramey said the object recovered in New Mexico “definitely was a United States army device”. The same wording appeared in newspapers in different cities, including the *Galveston Daily News*, *Amarillo Daily News* and *Abilene Reporter-News*, showing that this was part of the nationally distributed AP account rather than an isolated later recollection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/galvestondailynews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and another &#x27;Roswell&#x27;, Galveston Daily News, July 9July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

That wording carried more institutional weight than simply saying that the wreckage *looked like* a balloon. Ramey was commander of the Eighth Air Force, and the Roswell material had been sent to his Fort Worth headquarters after the original Roswell Army Air Field announcement that a “flying disc” had been recovered. Contemporary reporting therefore presented his statement as the higher command’s identification of material already in Army custody.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/roswell-aliens-fermi-paradox/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">roswell aliens fermi paradox</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Here’s the Proof There’s No Government Alien Conspiracy Around Roswell &#124; WIREDNovember 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2023</span></span></span>

The identification was also accompanied by an explanation of what kind of American device it supposedly was. Warrant Officer Irving [Newton]({{ 'newton/' | relative_url }}) of the Fort Worth base weather station examined the material and identified it as a high-altitude weather apparatus consisting of a balloon and a radar reflector. Reports described the reflector as a lightweight, shiny structure, variously called a kite or six-sided star, which could be tracked by radar so that winds aloft could be calculated. Newton said that roughly 80 US weather stations used balloons of this type.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/abilenereporternews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: the newspapers, Roswell explained, etc., Abilene Reporter-News, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

Ramey’s statement consequently had two related parts. The first was about **origin**: the Roswell object was American and belonged to the Army. The second concerned **function**: it was being identified publicly as ordinary meteorological equipment rather than an unknown aircraft.

This distinction becomes particularly useful in light of the US Air Force’s much later investigation. Its 1995 *Roswell Report* concluded that the recovered material was associated not simply with routine weather observation but with the balloon programme now generally known as [Project Mogul]({{ 'project-mogul/' | relative_url }}), which was connected with efforts to detect evidence of Soviet atomic testing. The official report nevertheless found the debris displayed in Ramey’s Fort Worth photographs consistent with the radar targets carried by balloons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>

In other words, the later Air Force account changed the deeper explanation of **why** the balloon equipment was operating in New Mexico, but it retained the central element of Ramey’s 1947 assertion: the material was American military equipment rather than an unidentified foreign or extraterrestrial machine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>

## What his claim did and did not explain

Ramey’s wording is especially revealing because contemporary reporting preserves a limit that is easily lost in simplified versions of the Roswell story. After discussing the weather device, Ramey said: “I don't say these devices are what people have called disks.” He then added that no such “gadget” as the reported disc was known to the Army, qualifying this with “at least this far down the line”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/abilenereporternews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: the newspapers, Roswell explained, etc., Abilene Reporter-News, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

Those remarks separate two questions that were competing for attention in July 1947.

**The Roswell question** was whether the physical material recovered in New Mexico represented one of the mysterious flying discs. Ramey gave an unequivocal answer: no. The debris was an American Army weather device. After that identification, plans to send the material onwards to [Wright Field]({{ 'wright-field/' | relative_url }}) in Ohio for further investigation were reported as cancelled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/galvestondailynews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and another &#x27;Roswell&#x27;, Galveston Daily News, July 9July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

**The wider flying-disc question** was whether the balloon-and-reflector explanation accounted for the numerous objects people elsewhere claimed to have seen moving through the sky. On that question, Ramey stopped short. He did not announce that all saucer reports were caused by Army weather equipment, nor did he claim that the Army had solved the national flying-disc mystery. His recorded statement explicitly declined to equate the devices categorically with everything witnesses were calling discs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/abilenereporternews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: the newspapers, Roswell explained, etc., Abilene Reporter-News, July 9, 1947July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

This was not an insignificant distinction in July 1947. The Roswell episode unfolded amid a much larger surge of reported sightings, and contemporary newspapers were simultaneously discussing possible explanations for them. The very AP coverage carrying Ramey’s Roswell explanation could also report other balloon finds and comparisons without presenting the New Mexico identification as a universal solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/galvestondailynews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and another &#x27;Roswell&#x27;, Galveston Daily News, July 9July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

The difference can therefore be expressed simply: **Ramey claimed certainty about an object he said his headquarters had examined, but not certainty about an entire phenomenon composed largely of distant eyewitness reports.**

That helps avoid a common historical overstatement. Saying that “the Army explained flying saucers as weather balloons” makes Ramey’s position sound broader than the contemporary record supports. Saying that **Ramey explained the Roswell debris as an American Army balloon-and-radar-target device** is much closer to what was actually reported.

## Why the limited wording matters in the Roswell debate


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Ramey’s carefully bounded claim matters because Roswell later became entangled with two different arguments: whether the displayed debris was genuinely what had been recovered near Roswell, and whether the Army’s weather explanation concealed the classified purpose of an otherwise terrestrial balloon system.

</div>

The second question changed substantially once the US Air Force revisited the case in the 1990s. Its official investigation concluded that the original “weather balloon” explanation had not disclosed the connection to the classified balloon research associated with Project Mogul. The 1995 report described the Fort Worth photographs as showing material consistent with a radar target normally carried beneath balloons and argued that the recovered material came from the classified balloon programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>

That later explanation creates an important distinction between **false origin** and **incomplete purpose**. Under the Air Force’s later account, Ramey’s statement that the Roswell object was an American military device was essentially correct about provenance, while the public description of it as an ordinary weather device concealed the more sensitive reason such balloon equipment was being used. The historical debate therefore cannot be reduced to a choice between “Ramey told the complete truth” and “Ramey identified an extraterrestrial craft as a balloon”. The official record itself allows a third possibility: an American object whose classified purpose was not publicly disclosed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>

This is also relevant to interpretations of the photographed document popularly called the **Ramey memo**. Whatever disputed readings researchers propose for the blurred text, the document was photographed during a press event in which Ramey was publicly presenting a very definite position about the Roswell material. The baseline against which any claimed transcription must be compared is therefore unusually clear: Ramey publicly maintained that the object brought from Roswell was American military equipment.

The contemporary press record also gives that statement stronger evidential standing than later memories of what Ramey or other participants supposedly believed. Multiple July 1947 newspaper reports preserve essentially the same formulation and place it directly within the Fort Worth identification of the debris.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/galvestondailynews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and another &#x27;Roswell&#x27;, Galveston Daily News, July 9July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span>

## The precise meaning of “Army device”

Read in its immediate 1947 setting, “United States Army device” was fundamentally a claim of **ownership and terrestrial origin**. It told journalists that the material was not an unknown machine captured by the Army but equipment belonging to the American military establishment itself.

The surrounding details made the intended meaning even clearer. Ramey’s headquarters described a damaged balloon, reflective material and a radar target; Newton explained how such equipment was used to determine winds aloft; and the proposed transfer to Wright Field was cancelled once the identification was considered settled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/rw/p/galvestondailynews9jul1947.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: Roswell 1947 - 1947 Press, Roswell explained, and another &#x27;Roswell&#x27;, Galveston Daily News, July 9July 9, 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 1947</span></span></span> The official message was therefore not merely that experts had failed to find anything unusual. It was that they believed they knew what the object **was and where it came from**.

Yet Ramey preserved a boundary around that certainty. He did not extend the Roswell identification into a comprehensive explanation for every flying-disc report then reaching newspapers and military offices. That restraint is one of the most useful details in the contemporary record because it separates the Army’s handling of a specific recovered object from the much larger and unresolved 1947 saucer controversy.

For the Roswell debate, that leaves Ramey’s position more precise than either side’s shorthand sometimes suggests. His public claim was unequivocal about the debris in Fort Worth: **the Roswell object was American**. What remained undisclosed in 1947, according to the Air Force’s later investigation, was the classified context behind the balloon programme. And what Ramey himself explicitly declined to settle was whether similar equipment explained the wider phenomenon that Americans had begun calling flying discs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>
